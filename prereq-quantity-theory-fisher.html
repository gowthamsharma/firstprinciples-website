The quantity theory, the Fisher effect and the costs of inflation

By Gowtham KumarLesson 45 of 50

Over long periods, faster money growth means higher inflation, and higher expected inflation means higher nominal interest rates.

MV=PY(1+i)=(1+r)(1+πe)MV = PY \qquad (1 + i) = (1 + r)(1 + \pi^e)MV=PY(1+i)=(1+r)(1+πe)

From first principles

The quantity equation

MV=PYMV = PYMV=PY

Money MMM times velocity VVV (how many times each unit of money is spent in a year) equals the price level PPP times real output YYY. Both sides measure total spending on final goods, so this is an identity.

It becomes a theory, the quantity theory of money, with one assumption: velocity and real output are roughly stable, or at least not driven by money. In growth rates:

%ΔM+%ΔV≈%ΔP+%ΔY\%\Delta M + \%\Delta V \approx \%\Delta P + \%\Delta Y%ΔM+%ΔV≈%ΔP+%ΔY

So if velocity is steady and output grows at its potential rate, money growth above output growth shows up as inflation. This is money neutrality: in the long run, money affects prices, not real output. It holds well over long periods and in high-inflation countries, and poorly over short ones, when velocity can swing sharply.

Why hold money at all?

Three motives:

  • Transactions: paying for things. Rises with nominal income.
  • Precautionary: a buffer for emergencies.
  • Speculative: holding money when other assets look risky or about to fall.

Money pays little or no interest, so higher interest rates raise the cost of holding it and money demand falls.

The Fisher effect

Lenders care about real returns: what their money will buy. If they expect 3% inflation and want a 2% real return, they demand:

(1+i)=(1.02)(1.03)=1.0506  ⇒  i=5.06%≈2%+3%(1 + i) = (1.02)(1.03) = 1.0506 \;\Rightarrow\; i = 5.06\% \approx 2\% + 3\%(1+i)=(1.02)(1.03)=1.0506⇒i=5.06%≈2%+3%

So nominal rates move roughly one-for-one with expected inflation. Investors also demand a premium for the risk that inflation turns out different from what they expected:

i≈r+πe+inflation risk premiumi \approx r + \pi^e + \text{inflation risk premium}i≈r+πe+inflation risk premium

The costs of inflation

  • Expected inflation has modest costs: changing prices (menu costs) and holding less cash (shoe-leather costs), plus distortions where tax systems are not indexed.
  • Unexpected inflation is worse. It redistributes wealth from lenders to borrowers (debts are repaid in cheaper money), and uncertainty makes long-term planning and lending more expensive.

Worked example 1: the quantity equation

Money supply 500, velocity 4, real output 1,000. Then:

P=MVY=500×41,000=2.0P = \frac{MV}{Y} = \frac{500 \times 4}{1{,}000} = 2.0P=YMV​=1,000500×4​=2.0

Over the next year money grows 7%, velocity is unchanged and real output grows 3%. Inflation ≈7%+0%−3%\approx 7\% + 0\% - 3\%≈7%+0%−3% = 4%. (Exactly: 1.07/1.03−1=3.9%1.07 / 1.03 - 1 = 3.9\%1.07/1.03−1=3.9%.)

Worked example 2: decomposing a bond yield

A 10-year government bond yields 5.8%. Economists estimate the real rate at 2.0% and expected inflation at 3.0%.

Inflation risk premium≈5.8−2.0−3.0=0.8%\text{Inflation risk premium} \approx 5.8 - 2.0 - 3.0 = 0.8\%Inflation risk premium≈5.8−2.0−3.0=0.8%

If expected inflation rises to 4%, the yield should rise to about 6.8%, all else equal. Bondholders who bought at 5.8% suffer a price fall: unexpected inflation hurts lenders.

Exam traps

  • The quantity theory is a long-run relationship. Velocity is not stable in the short run.
  • The Fisher effect uses expected, not realised, inflation.
  • Unexpected inflation helps borrowers and hurts lenders.
  • Higher interest rates lower the demand for money, because holding money costs more.

Questions people ask

What is the equation of exchange?

Money supply times its velocity equals the price level times real output (MV = PY). If velocity and output are stable, faster money growth means faster inflation.

What is the Fisher effect?

Nominal interest rates move with expected inflation, so the real rate stays roughly unchanged. A nominal rate is approximately the real rate plus expected inflation.

Why is inflation costly even if it is expected?

People hold less cash, prices must be changed more often, taxes based on nominal gains rise in real terms, and relative prices become harder to read. Unexpected inflation adds a transfer from lenders to borrowers.

Comparative advantage and the gains from trade

Countries gain from trade by specialising in what they give up least to produce, even if another country is better at everything.

Ricardian and Heckscher–Ohlin trade models

Comparative advantage comes either from differences in technology (Ricardo) or from differences in endowments of capital and labour (Heckscher–Ohlin), and the second predicts who wins and loses.

The balance of payments

Every cross-border transaction is recorded twice. A country that buys more from the world than it sells must finance the gap by selling assets or borrowing abroad.
