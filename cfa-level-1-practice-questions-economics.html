CFA Level I practice questions: Economics, with worked answers

By Gowtham KumarPractice questions

Ten original economics questions written like the real Level I exam: short cases with data, three options each, and worked explanations. Demand and supply, market structures, the business cycle, money and exchange rates. Economics carries 6–9% of the exam from 2027.

Economics at Level I covers microeconomics (demand, costs, market structures), the business cycle, fiscal and monetary policy, trade and currencies. Calculations are short; most marks come from knowing which way something moves and why.

Topic weight from 2027: 6–9% of Level I, about 11 to 16 of the 180 questions.

10 questions · Level I format · about 15 to 20 minutes

Choose an answer to see the explanation.

  1. An economist estimates the monthly demand for a brand of electric scooter as:

    Qd = 1,200−151{,}200 - 151,200−15P + 0.04I + 2.5Ps

    where P is the scooter's price (in € hundreds), I is average household income (in €) and Ps is the price of a rival scooter (in € hundreds). Currently P = 20, I = 50,000 and Ps = 12.

    At current values, the own-price elasticity of demand is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    First find quantity: Q = 1,200−15(20)+0.04(50,000)+2.5(12)=1,200−300+2,000+30=2,9301{,}200 - 15(20) + 0.04(50{,}000) + 2.5(12) = 1{,}200 - 300 + 2{,}000 + 30 = 2{,}9301,200−15(20)+0.04(50,000)+2.5(12)=1,200−300+2,000+30=2,930.

    Own-price elasticity =∂Q∂P×PQ=−15×202,930=−0.10= \dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial P} \times \dfrac{P}{Q} = \dfrac{-15 \times 20}{2{,}930} = -0.10=∂P∂Q​×QP​=2,930−15×20​=−0.10. Its absolute value is below 1, so demand is inelastic.

    −9.77 inverts the ratio (Q/P instead of P/Q). 0.68 is the income elasticity: 0.04×50,000/2,9300.04 \times 50{,}000 / 2{,}9300.04×50,000/2,930.

  2. A furniture maker sells desks in a perfectly competitive market at a price of €180. At its current output, average total cost is €220 per desk and average variable cost is €160 per desk. Market conditions are expected to stay the same.

    The firm should most likely:

    Answer: B.

    In the short run, a firm should keep producing as long as price covers average variable cost (€180 > €160). Each desk then contributes €20 towards fixed costs, which have to be paid anyway.

    In the long run all costs are variable. Price (€180) is below average total cost (€220), so the firm makes an economic loss and should exit if conditions do not improve. Shutting down now would lose all of the contribution to fixed costs.

  3. A competition authority is reviewing the market for regional airport ground-handling services. Four firms supply the market, with shares of 40%, 30%, 20% and 10%.

    The Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) for this market is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    HHI is the sum of squared market shares (in percentage points): 402+302+202+102=1,600+900+400+100=3,00040^2 + 30^2 + 20^2 + 10^2 = 1{,}600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = 3{,}000402+302+202+102=1,600+900+400+100=3,000. Values above about 2,500 are usually treated as highly concentrated.

    2,500 is the HHI if the four firms had equal shares of 25% each, which shows how the uneven split raises concentration. 9,000 multiplies the three-firm concentration ratio (90%) by 100, which is a different measure.

  4. A government raises infrastructure spending by 50 billion. Households spend 75% of each additional unit of disposable income, and the average tax rate is 20%. Assume there is no crowding out and that the economy has spare capacity.

    The resulting increase in GDP is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Fiscal multiplier =11−MPC(1−t)=11−0.75×0.80=10.40=2.5= \dfrac{1}{1 - MPC(1 - t)} = \dfrac{1}{1 - 0.75 \times 0.80} = \dfrac{1}{0.40} = 2.5=1−MPC(1−t)1​=1−0.75×0.801​=0.401​=2.5. The increase in GDP = 2.5 × 50 = 125 billion.

    200 billion uses the multiplier without taxes, 1/(1−0.75)=41 / (1 - 0.75) = 41/(1−0.75)=4, which ignores the share of each round of income that goes to the government. 50 billion assumes no multiplier effect at all.

  5. A central bank targets inflation of 2%. Its economists estimate the economy's long-run real growth rate, which they use as the neutral real interest rate, at 2%. The central bank's policy rate is currently 3%, and inflation is running at 2.1%.

    The central bank's monetary policy stance is best described as:

    Answer: C.

    Neutral policy rate =rneutral+πtarget=2%+2%=4%= r_{\text{neutral}} + \pi^{\text{target}} = 2\% + 2\% = 4\%=rneutral​+πtarget=2%+2%=4%. A policy rate of 3% is below this level, so policy is stimulating the economy (expansionary).

    Comparing the policy rate with current inflation (3% versus 2.1%) shows a positive real rate, but the stance depends on the neutral nominal rate, not on whether the real rate is positive. Inflation being near target does not make the current policy setting neutral.

  6. A treasury analyst sees the following mid-market quotes:

    QuoteRate
    USD/EUR1.0850 (US dollars per euro)
    JPY/USD152.40 (yen per US dollar)

    The JPY/EUR cross rate (yen per euro) is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Use the dollar as the link: 1 EUR = 1.0850 USD, and each USD = 152.40 JPY. So 1 EUR = 1.0850×152.40=165.351.0850 \times 152.40 = 165.351.0850×152.40=165.35 JPY.

    140.46 divides 152.40 by 1.0850, which is the wrong way round. 0.0060 is approximately the inverse of the correct rate (euros per yen).

  7. The spot exchange rate is 1.3650 Canadian dollars per US dollar (CAD/USD). The 180-day interest rate is 4.5% a year in Canada and 3.0% a year in the United States. Both rates use a 360-day year.

    The 180-day forward rate (CAD per USD) implied by covered interest rate parity is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    F=S×1+iCAD×1803601+iUSD×180360=1.3650×1.02251.0150=1.3751F = S \times \dfrac{1 + i_{CAD} \times \frac{180}{360}}{1 + i_{USD} \times \frac{180}{360}} = 1.3650 \times \dfrac{1.0225}{1.0150} = 1.3751F=S×1+iUSD​×360180​1+iCAD​×360180​​=1.3650×1.01501.0225​=1.3751. The quote is in CAD per USD, so the CAD rate goes in the numerator. The forward is above spot (about +101 forward points) because the US dollar, the currency with the lower interest rate, trades at a forward premium.

    1.3550 puts the interest rates the wrong way round. 1.3849 uses the full annual rates without scaling them to 180 days.

  8. Freedonia, a small country that imports steel at the world price, introduces a 15% tariff on steel imports. Freedonia's imports are too small to affect the world price.

    Compared with free trade, the tariff will most likely:

    Answer: B.

    A tariff raises the domestic price by the tariff amount. Domestic producers sell more at a higher price (their producer surplus rises), buyers pay more and buy less (their consumer surplus falls), and the government collects tariff revenue. The loss to consumers exceeds the gains to producers and the government. The difference is the deadweight loss from over-production at home and under-consumption.

    A small country cannot move the world price, so its terms of trade do not improve. Only a large importing country might achieve that.

  9. An economist reviewing recent data for an economy notes that real GDP growth has slowed for two quarters, inventories are rising faster than sales, companies are cutting overtime and temporary staff, and inflation remains above the central bank's target.

    The economy is most likely in which phase of the business cycle?

    Answer: B.

    At a peak, growth slows while inflation is still high from the earlier boom. Unplanned inventory builds as sales disappoint, and firms first cut hours and temporary workers before they lay off permanent staff.

    In a trough or early expansion, inventories are typically being run down relative to sales, firms start adding hours, and inflation pressure is usually low.

  10. The USD/EUR exchange rate (US dollars per euro) moves from 1.1000 to 1.0500 over a year.

    Over the year, the US dollar has:

    Answer: C.

    In USD/EUR the euro is the base currency, and the quote fell from 1.10 to 1.05: the euro depreciated by 1.05/1.10−1=−4.55%1.05/1.10 - 1 = -4.55\%1.05/1.10−1=−4.55%. To measure the dollar's change, invert the quotes to EUR/USD: 1/1.10=0.90911/1.10 = 0.90911/1.10=0.9091 and 1/1.05=0.95241/1.05 = 0.95241/1.05=0.9524. The dollar's change is 0.9524/0.9091−1=+4.76%0.9524 / 0.9091 - 1 = +4.76\%0.9524/0.9091−1=+4.76%, which equals 1.10/1.05−11.10/1.05 - 11.10/1.05−1.

    4.55% is the euro's depreciation, and an appreciation of one currency is not the same percentage as the depreciation of the other.

Three things to remember for economics

  • For exchange rates, write the quote as price currency / base currency and check units before you divide.
  • Elasticity questions are really revenue questions: is the absolute value above or below 1?
  • Leading, coincident and lagging indicators are a list worth memorising.

How to use these questions

Answer each question before you look at the explanation, and time yourself: the real exam allows about 90 seconds per question. Mark every question you got right for the wrong reason as wrong. When you miss one, write down the idea behind it rather than the answer, and come back to the page in a week.

These questions are written by First Principles to match the style and level of the exam. They are not taken from CFA Institute's curriculum or mock exams, which you should also use: they are included with your exam registration.

Questions people ask

Are these real CFA exam questions?

No. CFA Institute does not publish past exam questions. These are original questions written to match the Level I format: a short case, often with a table of data, followed by three answer options. Each explanation shows the working and why the two wrong options are wrong.

How many economics questions are on Level I?

Economics carries 6–9% of the exam from 2027, which is roughly 11 to 16 of the 180 questions.

Where can I find more Level I practice questions?

Use the practice questions and mock exams in CFA Institute's learning ecosystem first; they come with registration. Then use one third-party question bank, and redo every question you missed.
