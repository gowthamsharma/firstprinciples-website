Deferred tax: tax base and temporary differences

By Gowtham KumarLesson 21 of 50

Companies keep two sets of rules, one for the accounts and one for the tax authority. Deferred tax records the tax effect of differences that will reverse, so that tax expense matches the profit it relates to.

If the books and the tax return will catch up with each other later, record the future tax effect now.

From first principles

The accounts follow accounting standards; the tax return follows tax law. The two often recognise the same revenue or expense in different years. Tax depreciation is usually faster than book depreciation; a warranty provision is an expense in the books when the sale is made but deductible for tax only when the repair is paid.

If the company simply reported the tax it pays this year, tax expense would jump around for reasons unrelated to its profit. So it reports income tax expense that matches its accounting profit, and parks the timing difference on the balance sheet:

Income tax expense=Taxes payable+ΔDTL−ΔDTA\text{Income tax expense} = \text{Taxes payable} + \Delta\text{DTL} - \Delta\text{DTA}Income tax expense=Taxes payable+ΔDTL−ΔDTA

Tax base and temporary differences

Every asset and liability has a carrying amount (in the books) and a tax base (its value for tax purposes). The gap between them is a temporary difference, and the deferred tax balance is that gap times the tax rate expected when it reverses.

  • Asset carried above its tax base (tax depreciation ran ahead): more tax will be paid later, a deferred tax liability (DTL).
  • Asset carried below its tax base (written down in the books but not yet for tax): a deferred tax asset (DTA).
  • Liability carried above its tax base (a warranty provision not yet deductible): a DTA, because the deduction comes later.

Permanent differences

Some items never reverse: fines that are not tax-deductible, tax-exempt income, some entertainment costs. They create no deferred tax. Instead they make the effective tax rate (income tax expense ÷ pre-tax income) differ from the statutory rate.

Worked example 1: faster tax depreciation

A machine costs 1,200 with a 4-year life. The books use straight-line, 300 a year. The tax return allows 480, 360, 240 and 120. Tax rate 25%.

End of year Carrying amount Tax base Difference DTL at 25%
1 900 720 180 45
2 600 360 240 60
3 300 120 180 45
4 0 0 0 0

The DTL builds while tax depreciation is faster and unwinds when book depreciation catches up. Over the four years the total tax is the same; only the timing differs.

Worked example 2: putting tax expense together

A company has taxable profit of 800 at a 25% rate, so taxes payable are 200. During the year its DTL rose by 45 (from depreciation) and its DTA rose by 15 (from a new warranty provision).

Income tax expense=200+45−15=230\text{Income tax expense} = 200 + 45 - 15 = 230Income tax expense=200+45−15=230

Separately, suppose pre-tax income is 1,000 and includes a fine of 40 that is not deductible. Taxable income is 1,040 and tax is 260, so:

Effective tax rate=2601,000=26%versus a statutory rate of 25%\text{Effective tax rate} = \frac{260}{1{,}000} = 26\% \quad \text{versus a statutory rate of } 25\%Effective tax rate=1,000260​=26%versus a statutory rate of 25%

How analysts treat deferred tax

A DTL that is expected to reverse is a real liability. But a growing company that keeps buying assets may see its DTL grow for years without reversing; many analysts then treat part or all of it as equity rather than debt. A large DTA depends on future taxable profit to be worth anything (see the valuation allowance).

Exam traps

  • Temporary differences create deferred tax; permanent differences do not. Permanent differences change the effective tax rate instead.
  • Use the tax rate expected when the difference reverses, which is usually the enacted future rate.
  • Tax expense is not taxes paid. The difference is the change in DTL and DTA.
  • IFRS and US GAAP classify all deferred tax as non-current.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between a deferred tax asset and a deferred tax liability?

A deferred tax liability means the company has paid less tax so far than its accounting profit implies and will pay more later. A deferred tax asset is the opposite: it has paid more, or will get deductions later, so future tax will be lower.

What is a permanent difference?

An item that is treated differently by the books and the tax return and will never reverse, such as a non-deductible fine. It changes the effective tax rate but creates no deferred tax.

Should a deferred tax liability be treated as debt?

Only if it is expected to reverse. When it keeps growing because the company keeps investing, analysts often treat it as equity, since the tax may never actually be paid.

Tax rate changes and the valuation allowance

Deferred taxes are measured at the rate expected when they reverse, so a rate change remeasures them all at once. Deferred tax assets count only if future profits will absorb them, and the allowance against them is a judgement worth watching.

Bonds payable and the effective interest method

A bond is recorded at what investors paid for it. Interest expense is the market rate at issue times the carrying amount, and the gap with the coupon walks the carrying amount to face value, upwards for a discount bond and downwards for a premium bond.

Financial Statement AnalysisBy Gowtham KumarConcept 243 min read

Repaying debt early: derecognition

When a company buys back its bonds before maturity, any gap between what it pays and the book value is a gain or loss in profit. The sign depends on how rates and the company's credit have moved since issue, and none of it is operating performance.
