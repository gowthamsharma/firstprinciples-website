Classifying cash flows: operating, investing, financing

By Gowtham KumarLesson 12 of 50

Cash flows are sorted by the activity that caused them. IFRS gives companies choices for interest, dividends and some taxes; US GAAP does not, so the same company can report different operating cash flow under each.

Ask what the cash was for: running the business, buying long-term assets, or dealing with the people who finance it.

From first principles

The cash flow statement explains the change in cash by asking one question of every inflow and outflow: which activity caused it?

  • Operating activities: the day-to-day business: cash from customers, cash paid to suppliers and employees, taxes.
  • Investing activities: buying and selling long-term assets and investments: property, plant and equipment, intangible assets, other companies' shares and bonds (not held for trading), loans made to others.
  • Financing activities: dealings with lenders and owners: issuing or repaying debt, issuing or buying back shares, paying dividends.

Where IFRS and US GAAP differ

Item US GAAP IFRS
Interest paid operating operating or financing
Interest received operating operating or investing
Dividends received operating operating or investing
Dividends paid financing operating or financing
Taxes paid operating operating, unless linked to an investing or financing item
Bank overdrafts financing can be part of cash if repayable on demand and part of cash management

A company must apply its IFRS choices consistently. The logic of the choices: interest and dividends received are a return on investments (investing); interest and dividends paid are a cost of finance (financing). US GAAP keeps all interest in operating because it passes through net income.

Non-cash transactions (buying an asset with a note payable, converting bonds into shares, leasing) are not in the statement at all; they are disclosed separately because no cash moved.

Worked example: one company, two sets of rules

During the year a company:

Item Amount
Cash collected from customers 900
Cash paid to suppliers 500
Cash paid to employees 150
Interest paid 50
Taxes paid 40
Interest received 10
Dividends received 5
Bought equipment 300
Sold an old truck 20
Issued bonds 200
Dividends paid 80

US GAAP:

CFO=900−500−150−50−40+10+5=175\text{CFO} = 900 - 500 - 150 - 50 - 40 + 10 + 5 = 175CFO=900−500−150−50−40+10+5=175

CFI=−300+20=−280CFF=200−80=120\text{CFI} = -300 + 20 = -280 \qquad \text{CFF} = 200 - 80 = 120CFI=−300+20=−280CFF=200−80=120

IFRS, with interest paid in financing and interest and dividends received in investing:

CFO=900−500−150−40=210\text{CFO} = 900 - 500 - 150 - 40 = 210CFO=900−500−150−40=210

CFI=−280+10+5=−265CFF=200−80−50=70\text{CFI} = -280 + 10 + 5 = -265 \qquad \text{CFF} = 200 - 80 - 50 = 70CFI=−280+10+5=−265CFF=200−80−50=70

Net change in cash is +15 both ways. Only the labels moved, but CFO is 35 higher under this IFRS choice.

How the exam tests it

  • Calculating CFO, CFI and CFF from a list of transactions.
  • Knowing which items IFRS lets companies place differently, and the effect on CFO.
  • Recognising non-cash transactions that do not appear in the statement.

Exam traps

  • Before comparing CFO across companies, move interest and dividends to the same bucket. An IFRS firm putting interest paid in financing looks stronger on CFO than an identical US GAAP firm.
  • Buying a bond issued by another company is investing; issuing your own bond is financing; the interest you then pay is operating under US GAAP.
  • Repaying debt principal is financing under both; only the interest has a choice.
  • Securities held for trading are usually operating, not investing, because trading them is part of the business.
  • Sale proceeds, not the gain, go in investing. The gain is removed from operating in the indirect method.

Questions people ask

Why does the classification of interest matter?

Because operating cash flow is the number analysts watch most. Moving interest paid from operating to financing raises CFO without any change in the business, so comparisons need a common treatment.

Are dividends paid an operating cash flow?

Under US GAAP, never: they are financing. IFRS allows operating or financing, and many IFRS companies choose financing.

What is a non-cash investing and financing activity?

A transaction that changes long-term assets, debt or equity without cash moving, such as buying equipment by issuing a note or converting bonds into shares. It is disclosed in a note, not shown as a cash flow.

The indirect method: from net income to operating cash

Most companies report operating cash flow by starting from net income and undoing everything that was not cash. Each adjustment has a simple logic, and the direct method reaches the same answer from the other side.

Financial Statement AnalysisBy Gowtham KumarConcept 143 min read

What goes into the cost of inventory

Inventory carries every cost needed to get goods ready for sale, including a share of factory overheads based on normal capacity. Anything else is an expense of the period, and the choice moves profit between years.

Financial Statement AnalysisBy Gowtham KumarConcept 154 min read

FIFO, LIFO and weighted average cost

When identical units were bought at different prices, the cost-flow method decides which price goes to cost of sales and which stays in inventory. With rising prices the choice moves profit, tax, cash flow and every inventory ratio.
