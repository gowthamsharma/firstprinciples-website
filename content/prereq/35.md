> \[MR = MC\]
>
> For a monopoly, this sets the quantity; the demand curve then sets the price.

## From first principles

A monopolist is the only seller, so the market demand curve is its demand curve. To sell one more unit it must lower the price, and (without price discrimination) lower it on **every** unit. So the extra revenue from one more unit is less than its price: **MR lies below demand**.

### Why MR falls twice as fast

With straight-line demand \(P = a - bQ\):

\[ TR = P \times Q = aQ - bQ^2 \qquad MR = \frac{\Delta TR}{\Delta Q} = a - 2bQ \]

MR starts at the same point as demand but has twice the slope, so it hits zero at half the quantity.

Profit is maximised where **MR = MC**. The monopolist chooses that quantity, then reads off the highest price buyers will pay for it from the demand curve.

### Marginal revenue and elasticity

Marginal revenue is tied to the price elasticity of demand:

\[ MR = P\left(1 - \frac{1}{\lvert E \rvert}\right) \]

- Where demand is **elastic** (\(\lvert E \rvert > 1\)), MR is positive: selling more raises revenue.
- At **unit elasticity**, MR = 0 and revenue is at its maximum.
- Where demand is **inelastic**, MR is negative: selling more **lowers** revenue.

Marginal cost is always positive, and the monopolist sets MR = MC, so MR must be positive too: **a profit-maximising monopolist always operates on the elastic part of demand**. A firm that finds its demand inelastic can raise its price, sell less, earn more revenue and save costs.

Rearranging gives the monopolist's **markup over marginal cost**: \(P = \dfrac{MC}{1 - 1/\lvert E \rvert}\). The less elastic demand is, the larger the markup.

### Compared with perfect competition

- **Quantity is lower** and **price is higher** (price > MC).
- Some buyers who value the good above its marginal cost go without. That lost value is the **deadweight loss**.
- Part of what was consumer surplus becomes the monopolist's profit (a transfer, not a loss to society).

## Worked example 1: the monopoly outcome

Demand \(P = 100 - Q\), so \(MR = 100 - 2Q\). Marginal cost is constant at 20.

\[ 100 - 2Q = 20 \;\Rightarrow\; Q = 40, \qquad P = 100 - 40 = 60 \]

A competitive market would produce where \(P = MC\): \(Q = 80\), \(P = 20\).

| | Monopoly | Competition |
|---|---|---|
| Quantity | 40 | 80 |
| Price | 60 | 20 |
| Consumer surplus | \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 40 \times 40 = 800\) | \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 80 \times 80 = 3{,}200\) |
| Producer profit | \((60 - 20) \times 40 = 1{,}600\) | 0 |
| Deadweight loss | \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 40 \times 40 = 800\) | 0 |

Check: 800 + 1,600 + 800 = 3,200. The total surplus available under competition is split three ways under monopoly, and one part of it (the deadweight loss) simply disappears.

## Worked example 2: checking with elasticity

At the monopoly point \(P = 60\), \(Q = 40\). The point elasticity of demand is:

\[ E = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \times \frac{P}{Q} = -1 \times \frac{60}{40} = -1.5 \]

\[ MR = 60 \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{1.5}\right) = 60 \times \frac{1}{3} = 20 = MC \]

The same answer from a different route, and demand is elastic (1.5 > 1), as it must be.

## Natural monopoly and regulation

Where fixed costs are huge and marginal cost low (water networks, power grids, rail track), **average cost keeps falling** across the whole range of market demand. One firm can supply everyone more cheaply than two. Breaking it up would raise costs, so governments regulate instead:

- **Marginal-cost pricing** (P = MC) gives the efficient quantity, but because average cost is above marginal cost, the firm makes a **loss** and needs a subsidy.
- **Average-cost pricing** (P = ATC) lets the firm **break even** with no subsidy, at a slightly higher price and lower quantity.
- **Rate-of-return regulation** allows a fair return on capital, but weakens the incentive to cut costs (and may encourage over-investment).

## Exam traps

- **Monopolists do not charge "the highest possible price"**; they choose the profit-maximising point on demand.
- **A monopoly has no supply curve.** The quantity it supplies at a given price depends on the shape of demand, not just on cost.
- **MR is zero where total revenue is at its maximum**, which is not the profit-maximising point when MC > 0.
- **Monopoly profit is a transfer; deadweight loss is a true loss.**

## Questions people ask

### Why is marginal revenue below price for a monopolist?

To sell one more unit, the monopolist must cut the price on every unit, not just the last one. The extra revenue is the new price minus the loss on all the others.

### What is deadweight loss?

The value of trades that would benefit both sides but do not happen because the monopolist restricts output to keep the price high.

### What is a natural monopoly?

An industry where one firm can serve the whole market more cheaply than several, because fixed costs are huge, as with water pipes or power grids. These are usually regulated rather than broken up.
