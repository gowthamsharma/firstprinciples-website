> The goods are identical; the costs are not. The method just decides which cost leaves the shelf first.

## From first principles

You bought 100 units at 10, then 100 at 12, then 100 at 14, and sold 200. The physical units are interchangeable, so accounting needs a rule for which costs count as sold:

- **FIFO (first in, first out)**: the oldest costs go to cost of sales. Ending inventory holds the newest costs, close to current prices.
- **LIFO (last in, first out)**: the newest costs go to cost of sales, close to current prices. Ending inventory holds old, possibly very stale, costs. **Allowed under US GAAP only; prohibited under IFRS.**
- **Weighted average cost**: every unit carries the same average cost.
- **Specific identification**: used for items that are not interchangeable, such as cars or artworks.

Whatever the method, one identity holds:

\[ \text{Opening inventory} + \text{Purchases} = \text{Cost of sales} + \text{Closing inventory} \]

The method only decides how the total cost of goods available is split between the income statement and the balance sheet.

## Worked example 1: three methods, prices rising

Using the purchases above, 200 units are sold at 20 each (revenue 4,000). Goods available cost 1,000 + 1,200 + 1,400 = 3,600; the average cost is 3,600 / 300 = 12.

| Method | Cost of sales | Ending inventory | Gross profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| FIFO | 1,000 + 1,200 = 2,200 | 1,400 | 1,800 |
| Weighted average | 200 × 12 = 2,400 | 1,200 | 1,600 |
| LIFO | 1,400 + 1,200 = 2,600 | 1,000 | 1,400 |

## Worked example 2: opening stock and an average

Opening inventory is 50 units at 8. The company buys 150 units at 10 and sells 120.

\[ \text{Average cost} = \frac{50 \times 8 + 150 \times 10}{200} = \frac{1{,}900}{200} = 9.50 \]

| Method | Cost of sales | Closing inventory (80 units) |
|---|---|---|
| FIFO | 50 × 8 + 70 × 10 = 1,100 | 80 × 10 = 800 |
| Weighted average | 120 × 9.50 = 1,140 | 80 × 9.50 = 760 |

Both rows add up to 1,900, the cost of goods available.

## Periodic or perpetual?

A **periodic** system works out cost of sales once, at the period end; a **perpetual** system updates it with every sale. **FIFO gives the same answer under both.** LIFO and weighted average can differ, because under a perpetual system the "latest" cost or the average at the moment of each sale depends on the order of purchases and sales.

## What the choice does to the analysis

When prices are **rising**, compared with FIFO, a LIFO company reports:

| Item | LIFO versus FIFO |
|---|---|
| Cost of sales | higher |
| Gross profit, net income | lower |
| Income taxes paid | lower, so operating cash flow is **higher** |
| Ending inventory, current assets | lower |
| Inventory turnover (cost of sales ÷ inventory) | higher |
| Current ratio | lower |

When prices fall, every effect reverses. Weighted average always lands between the two.

A **LIFO liquidation** happens when a LIFO company sells more than it buys and dips into old, cheap layers. Cost of sales falls, profit jumps, and the jump is not repeatable: it comes from running down stock, often in a weak year.

## Exam traps

- **When prices rise, LIFO gives the highest cost of sales, lowest profit, lowest taxes and lowest inventory**, and the highest inventory turnover.
- **Total cost of sales + ending inventory = total goods available** under every method.
- **LIFO's tax saving is real cash**, which is why US companies use it despite lower reported profit.
- **LIFO is not allowed under IFRS.**
- **A LIFO liquidation inflates profit** with old, cheap costs; do not extrapolate it.

## Questions people ask

### Which method gives higher profit when prices rise?

FIFO. It charges the oldest, cheapest units to cost of sales, so profit and closing inventory are both higher. LIFO charges the newest, dearest units, lowering profit and tax.

### Is LIFO allowed under IFRS?

No. IFRS permits FIFO, weighted average and specific identification. LIFO is allowed only under US GAAP, mainly because of its tax advantage when prices rise.

### Which balance sheet figure is more realistic?

FIFO's, because closing inventory is made of the most recent purchases. Under LIFO, inventory can be valued at prices that are decades old. The [LIFO reserve](prereq-lifo-reserve.html) lets analysts convert it.
