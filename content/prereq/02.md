> The balance sheet is a photo; profit and cash flow are two films of what happened between one photo and the next.

## From first principles

Start from one fact:

\[ \text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity} \]

It must hold, because every asset was paid for by someone: a lender (a liability) or the owners (equity). Equity itself has only a few ways to change, so we can open it up:

\[ \text{Equity} = \text{Contributed capital} + \text{Retained earnings} \]

\[ \text{Closing retained earnings} = \text{Opening} + \text{Revenue} - \text{Expenses} - \text{Dividends} \]

Those two lines are the whole map:

- The **income statement** measures revenue minus expenses over a period: **net income**.
- Net income not paid out as **dividends** is added to **retained earnings**, part of equity. That is the link between the income statement and the balance sheet.
- Profit is not cash. A sale on credit is revenue today and cash next month, so a third statement, the **cash flow statement**, explains why the cash line on the balance sheet moved, sorted into operating, investing and financing activities.
- A fourth statement, the **statement of changes in equity**, lists every movement in equity: profit, other comprehensive income, dividends, share issues and buybacks.

[[figure]]

Every transaction touches at least two accounts, so the equation never breaks. That is double entry, and it is why the statements **articulate**: you cannot change one without the others moving.

## Worked example: seven transactions through all three statements

A new company starts with nothing. During its first year:

1. Owners invest 1,000 in shares.
2. It borrows 500 from a bank.
3. It buys inventory for 400 in cash.
4. It sells half of that inventory (cost 200) for 300, on credit.
5. It pays wages of 50.
6. It collects 120 from customers.
7. It pays a dividend of 20.

| # | Cash | Receivables | Inventory | Debt | Share capital | Retained earnings |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | +1,000 | | | | +1,000 | |
| 2 | +500 | | | +500 | | |
| 3 | −400 | | +400 | | | |
| 4 | | +300 | −200 | | | +100 |
| 5 | −50 | | | | | −50 |
| 6 | +120 | −120 | | | | |
| 7 | −20 | | | | | −20 |
| End | 1,150 | 180 | 200 | 500 | 1,000 | 30 |

**Balance sheet.** Assets: 1,150 + 180 + 200 = **1,530**. Liabilities and equity: 500 + 1,000 + 30 = **1,530**.

[[fig:bs_balance|The ending balance sheet: both columns total 1,530.]]

**Income statement.** Revenue 300, cost of sales 200, wages 50: **net income 50**. Retained earnings: 0 + 50 − 20 = 30.

**Cash flow statement.**

- Operating: collected 120, paid for inventory 400, paid wages 50: **−330**.
- Investing: nothing (inventory is an operating item).
- Financing: shares 1,000 + loan 500 − dividend 20 = **+1,480** (under US GAAP dividends paid are financing; IFRS also allows operating).
- Net change: −330 + 1,480 = **+1,150**, exactly the ending cash.

The company made a profit of 50 but burned 330 of operating cash, because 180 is still owed by customers and 200 sits in unsold stock:

\[ \text{CFO} = 50 - 180 - 200 = -330 \]

That one line is the [indirect method](prereq-indirect-method.html) in miniature.

## How the exam tests it

- Rolling equity forward: opening equity + net income + OCI + share issues − buybacks − dividends = closing equity.
- Tracing one transaction (a credit sale, a purchase on credit, a loan repayment) through all three statements.
- Spotting why net income and operating cash flow differ.

## Exam traps

- **Net income and operating cash flow differ** by non-cash items (depreciation) and by changes in working capital (receivables, inventory, payables).
- **Dividends** come out of retained earnings; they are a payout to owners, never an expense in the profit calculation.
- **Repaying the principal of a loan is not an expense.** Cash falls and the liability falls; profit is untouched. Only the interest is an expense.
- **Non-cash transactions** (buying equipment with a note payable, converting debt to shares) change the balance sheet but do not appear in the cash flow statement itself; they are disclosed separately.
- **Other comprehensive income** skips profit and lands directly in equity (see [comprehensive income](prereq-comprehensive-income.html)).

## Questions people ask

### Which financial statement is the most important?

None on its own. Profit can be shaped by estimates, cash flow can be lumpy and the balance sheet is a snapshot. An analyst reads all three together, because a story that holds in one but not the others is a warning sign.

### How does net income reach the balance sheet?

Profit for the year, less any dividends declared, is added to retained earnings, which is part of equity. That single link is why the balance sheet still balances after a year of trading.

### Why can a profitable company run out of cash?

Profit counts sales when earned, not when paid. A fast-growing firm can book large profits while its cash is tied up in customer receivables and stock, so cash flow from operations is the check on profit quality.
