> Depreciation does not measure loss of value. It allocates a cost over the years that benefit from it.

## From first principles

A machine bought for 10,000 helps produce revenue for five years. Expensing the whole 10,000 in year one would make year one look terrible and years two to five look too good. So the cost, less the expected **salvage (residual) value** at the end, is spread over the **useful life**. The amount spread is the **depreciable base**:

\[ \text{Depreciable base} = \text{Cost} - \text{Salvage value} \]

How to spread it depends on how the asset's benefit is used up:

- **Straight-line**: the same amount each year. Good when the benefit is even.

\[ \text{Depreciation} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Salvage}}{\text{Useful life}} \]

- **Double-declining balance (DDB)**: a fixed rate of \(2 / \text{life}\) applied to the **opening book value** (not the depreciable base), so early years carry more. Salvage is ignored in the calculation, but the asset is never depreciated below it: the final year takes only what is needed to land on salvage. Good when assets are most productive, or most quickly obsolete, when new.
- **Units-of-production**: in proportion to use.

\[ \text{Depreciation} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Salvage}}{\text{Total expected units}} \times \text{Units this year} \]

## Worked example 1: three methods, one machine

Cost 10,000, salvage 1,000, life 5 years, so the depreciable base is 9,000.

| Year | Straight-line | DDB (40% of opening book value) | DDB book value at year-end |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,800 | 4,000 | 6,000 |
| 2 | 1,800 | 2,400 | 3,600 |
| 3 | 1,800 | 1,440 | 2,160 |
| 4 | 1,800 | 864 | 1,296 |
| 5 | 1,800 | 296 | 1,000 |
| Total | 9,000 | 9,000 | |

Year 5 under DDB would be 40% × 1,296 = 518, but that would take book value below salvage, so it is capped at 296. **The total is 9,000 either way**; only the timing differs.

If the machine instead has a capacity of 30,000 hours and runs 7,500 hours in year 1, units-of-production gives \(9{,}000 \times 7{,}500 / 30{,}000 = 2{,}250\).

## Worked example 2: a change in estimate

Using straight-line, after two years the book value is 10,000 − 2 × 1,800 = 6,400. Management now expects the machine to last **four more years** (six in total) with salvage of only 400. A change in estimate is applied **going forward**:

\[ \text{New depreciation} = \frac{6{,}400 - 400}{4} = 1{,}500 \text{ a year} \]

Years 1 and 2 are not restated. Note what this did to profit: depreciation fell from 1,800 to 1,500, so a longer life lifts reported earnings at once.

## How analysts use it

The notes give gross PP&E, accumulated depreciation and the year's depreciation expense. With straight-line, three quick estimates follow:

\[ \text{Average age} \approx \frac{\text{Accumulated depreciation}}{\text{Depreciation expense}} \]

\[ \text{Remaining life} \approx \frac{\text{Net PP\&E}}{\text{Depreciation expense}} \]

\[ \text{Total life} \approx \frac{\text{Gross PP\&E}}{\text{Depreciation expense}} \]

Gross PP&E 1,200, accumulated depreciation 480, expense 120: average age about 4 years, remaining life about 6, total life about 10. An old asset base hints that big replacement spending is coming.

Comparing firms, accelerated depreciation in the early years means **lower profit, lower asset values and higher asset turnover**. Cash flow is the same before tax; the method only moves profit between years.

## Exam traps

- **DDB applies the rate to book value, not to cost minus salvage**, and stops at salvage.
- **Changes in life, salvage or method are changes in estimate**: applied prospectively, never restated.
- **Cash flow is unaffected** (except through tax). Depreciation is added back in the indirect method.
- **IFRS requires component depreciation**: significant parts with different lives (an aircraft's engines and its body) are depreciated separately. US GAAP allows it but it is rare.
- **Depreciation is not a valuation.** Book value can be far from what the asset would sell for; a sharp fall in value is handled by an impairment test, not by depreciation.

## Questions people ask

### Which depreciation method gives higher profit?

In the early years, straight-line does, because accelerated methods put more of the cost into the first years. Over the asset's whole life the total expense is the same; only the timing differs.

### Does depreciation affect cash flow?

Not directly: no cash leaves when depreciation is booked. It does matter for tax, because tax depreciation lowers the tax bill, and in the indirect cash flow statement it is added back to net income.

### What happens when the useful life estimate changes?

The change is applied going forward: the remaining book value is spread over the new remaining life. Past years are not restated. A sudden longer life is a classic way to lift profit.
