> Revaluation trades reliability for relevance: the balance sheet shows current value, but estimates enter.

## From first principles

Historical cost is reliable but goes stale: a building bought 30 years ago may be worth many times its book value. IFRS therefore lets companies choose, **by class of asset** (all land and buildings, say, but not just one building):

- **Cost model**: cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment. The only option under US GAAP.
- **Revaluation model** (IFRS only): fair value at the revaluation date, less later depreciation and impairment. Revaluations must be frequent enough that the carrying amount does not differ materially from fair value.

### Where revaluation changes go

The rule is built so that a company cannot manufacture profit by revaluing upwards:

- An **increase** goes to **OCI** (a revaluation surplus in equity), **unless** it reverses an earlier decrease that was charged to profit; that part goes to profit.
- A **decrease** goes to **profit**, **unless** it reverses an earlier surplus; that part reduces the surplus in OCI.

[[figure]]

[[fig:reval_routes|Gains are kept out of profit unless they undo an earlier loss; losses hit profit unless they undo an earlier gain.]]

The revaluation surplus is **never recycled** into profit. When the asset is used up or sold, the surplus can be transferred directly to retained earnings inside equity.

### After a revaluation

Depreciation is based on the **revalued amount** over the remaining life, so an upward revaluation raises future depreciation and lowers future profit.

### Investment property

**Investment property** is held to earn rent or for capital appreciation, not to run the business. Under IFRS it can use the cost model or the **fair value model**, where the asset is not depreciated and **every change in fair value goes straight to profit**. US GAAP has no separate category: it is carried at cost.

## Worked example 1: up, then down

A building costs 1,000 (ignore depreciation for simplicity) and is revalued to 1,150. The increase of 150 goes to **OCI**. Next year it falls to 950, a drop of 200: the first 150 reverses the surplus (**OCI −150**) and the remaining 50 is a loss in **profit**.

The other way round: a building with an earlier write-down of 80 charged to profit is revalued upwards by 120. The first 80 reverses the earlier loss, so it goes to **profit**; the remaining 40 goes to **OCI**.

## Worked example 2: depreciation after revaluation

A machine costs 1,000 with a 10-year life and no salvage value, so depreciation is 100 a year. After two years its carrying amount is 800, and it is revalued to 960. The surplus of 160 goes to OCI. Depreciation for the remaining 8 years becomes:

\[ \frac{960}{8} = 120 \text{ a year} \]

So revaluation lifts assets and equity today, and lowers profit by 20 a year from now on.

## Effects on ratios

An upward revaluation raises total assets and equity, so debt-to-equity and other leverage ratios **fall**. Future return on assets and return on equity tend to fall, because the asset base is larger and depreciation higher. When comparing an IFRS company that revalues with a cost-model or US GAAP company, look at the revaluation surplus and the depreciation it adds.

## Exam traps

- **Revaluation is IFRS only and applies to a whole class of assets.**
- **The first upward revaluation goes to OCI**, not profit; a reversal of an earlier loss goes to profit.
- **Investment property under the fair value model**: all changes go to profit and there is no depreciation.
- **Revaluation raises future depreciation** and lowers future profitability ratios.
- **The surplus is not recycled** into profit when the asset is sold.

## Questions people ask

### What is the revaluation model?

An IFRS option to carry property, plant and equipment at fair value, updated regularly. Increases go to a revaluation surplus in equity; decreases go to profit unless they reverse an earlier increase.

### Can US companies revalue assets upward?

No. US GAAP requires the cost model for property, plant and equipment, with impairment write-downs but no upward revaluation.

### How is investment property different?

It is held to earn rent or grow in value, not to run the business. Under IFRS it can use a fair value model where every change goes straight to profit, which can make earnings volatile.
