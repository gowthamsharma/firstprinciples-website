> Capitalise if the benefit lasts beyond this year; expense if it does not.

## From first principles

Matching says a cost belongs to the periods that benefit from it. If spending this year will help earn revenue for several years, it is recorded as an **asset** (capitalised) and spread over those years by depreciation or amortisation. If the benefit is used up this year, it is an **expense** now.

[[figure]]

The choice changes three things, even though the cash spent is identical:

- **Profit timing.** Expensing hits this year hard; capitalising spreads the cost. Over the asset's life, total expense is the same.
- **Cash flow classification.** Expensed spending is an **operating** outflow; capitalised spending is an **investing** outflow. Capitalising therefore makes operating cash flow higher.
- **The balance sheet.** Capitalising adds an asset, so total assets and equity are higher while the asset is being depreciated.

### Rules worth knowing

- **Borrowing costs** on assets that take a substantial time to build (a plant, a ship) are capitalised into the asset's cost during construction, under both IFRS and US GAAP.
- **Research** is always expensed. **Development** costs are capitalised under IFRS once strict criteria are met (technical feasibility, intention and ability to complete and use or sell, probable future benefits, reliable measurement). Under US GAAP they are generally expensed, with exceptions for software.
- **Software for sale** (US GAAP): expensed until technological feasibility, capitalised after. **Software for internal use**: capitalised once the project reaches the development stage.
- **Repairs and maintenance** are expensed; **improvements** that extend life or capacity are capitalised.
- **Internally generated** brands, customer lists and goodwill are never capitalised; the same items **bought** from others are.

## Worked example 1: one system, two treatments

A firm spends 100 on a system with a 5-year life and no salvage value.

| | Expensed | Capitalised |
|---|---|---|
| Year-1 expense | 100 | 20 |
| Years 2 to 5 expense | 0 | 20 a year |
| Year-1 operating cash flow | −100 | 0 |
| Year-1 investing cash flow | 0 | −100 |
| Asset at end of year 1 | 0 | 80 |

Capitalising raises year-1 pre-tax profit by 80 and operating cash flow by 100.

## Worked example 2: capitalised interest and coverage

A company has EBIT of 1,000. It incurred total interest of 200, of which 50 relates to a factory under construction and is capitalised.

\[ \text{Reported interest coverage} = \frac{1{,}000}{200 - 50} = 6.67 \qquad \text{Adjusted coverage} = \frac{1{,}000}{200} = 5.00 \]

The adjusted figure uses all the interest the company actually owes. In the cash flow statement, the capitalised 50 appears in investing rather than operating, so an analyst comparing operating cash flow may move it back.

## Effects on ratios, in the year of spending

Compared with expensing, capitalising gives: higher net income, higher total assets and equity, higher operating cash flow, lower investing cash flow, and higher interest coverage when interest is capitalised. In later years the depreciation of the capitalised amount reduces profit, so the profit advantage reverses; the operating cash flow advantage does not.

## Exam traps

- **Capitalising boosts operating cash flow permanently**; the outflow sits in investing.
- **Interest coverage looks better** when interest is capitalised; analysts often add it back.
- **Development costs differ between IFRS and US GAAP**: compare like with like.
- **Return on assets can go either way**: capitalising raises both profit and assets.
- **A firm that capitalises aggressively** can show smooth profits and strong operating cash flow while spending heavily.

## Questions people ask

### What does it mean to capitalise a cost?

To record the spending as an asset on the balance sheet rather than as an expense, then spread it over the years that benefit through depreciation or amortisation.

### Why does capitalising increase operating cash flow?

Because capitalised spending is classified as an investing outflow. The same cash leaves the company, but it is no longer counted in operating activities.

### Are research and development costs capitalised?

Research is always expensed. Under IFRS, development costs are capitalised once strict criteria show a viable product; under US GAAP they are generally expensed, except for certain software costs.
