> GDP = C + I + G + (X − M).

## From first principles

Every unit of currency spent on a final good is someone's income, and it pays for something produced. So an economy's output can be measured three equivalent ways:

- **Expenditure**: who bought it. Consumption (C), business investment (I), government purchases (G), and net exports (exports X minus imports M).
- **Income**: who earned it: wages, profits, rents and interest, plus a few adjustments such as depreciation and indirect taxes.
- **Value added**: what each producer added at its stage of production.

Only **final** goods count. Counting the steel and then the car would count the steel twice. Transfer payments (pensions, benefits) are not purchases of output, so they are not in G. Used goods and financial transactions are excluded too.

## Nominal and real

**Nominal GDP** uses current prices, so it rises with inflation even if nothing more is produced. **Real GDP** uses base-year prices and measures volume. The **GDP deflator** = nominal GDP / real GDP × 100 is a broad measure of the price level.

## Worked example

C 680, I 180, G 170, X 300, M 330. GDP = 680 + 180 + 170 + (300 − 330) = **1,000**. If nominal GDP grew 6% and the deflator 4%, real GDP grew about **2%**.

## Exam traps

- **Imports are subtracted** because C, I and G include spending on imported goods that were not produced at home.
- **Inventory build-up counts as investment**: output produced but not yet sold.

## Questions people ask

### What is the difference between nominal and real GDP?

Nominal GDP values output at this year's prices; real GDP uses fixed base-year prices, so it rises only when the quantity of output rises. Growth figures in the news are real.

### What are the ways to measure GDP?

Add up spending on final goods (consumption, investment, government, net exports), add up incomes earned producing them, or add up value added at each stage. In principle all three give the same answer.

### What does GDP leave out?

Unpaid work at home, the informal economy, leisure, pollution and how income is shared. It measures output, not well-being.
