> Compare like with like: undo accounting choices before comparing economics.

## From first principles

Two companies with identical businesses can report very different numbers because of their accounting standard (IFRS or US GAAP) and their choices within it. A screen or ratio comparison on raw numbers mostly measures those choices. So before comparing, an analyst puts both companies on one basis, usually by adjusting the company whose choice is less common in the peer group.

[[figure]]

The usual adjustments, and the lessons behind them:

- **Inventory**: convert LIFO to FIFO with the [LIFO reserve](prereq-lifo-reserve.html): inventory, cost of sales, equity and deferred tax.
- **Investments**: put securities held in different categories (fair value through profit, through OCI, or amortised cost) on one basis.
- **Goodwill**: remove it from assets and equity to compare **tangible** book value and returns, especially between companies that grew by acquisition and companies that grew organically.
- **Depreciation**: compare useful lives and methods; restate if one company uses much longer lives.
- **Capitalised costs**: put capitalised interest and development costs back into expenses, or capitalise them for the other company (see [capitalise or expense](prereq-capitalise-or-expense.html)).
- **Leases and other obligations**: add lease liabilities, pension deficits and guarantees to debt before comparing leverage.

## Worked example 1: goodwill and returns

Company A (organic growth) has equity of 800 and no goodwill. Company B (grew by acquisitions) has equity of 1,000, including goodwill of 300. Each earns net income of 120.

\[ \text{Reported ROE: A} = \frac{120}{800} = 15\%, \quad \text{B} = \frac{120}{1{,}000} = 12\% \]

\[ \text{Tangible ROE: A} = 15\%, \quad \text{B} = \frac{120}{1{,}000 - 300} = 17.1\% \]

B's operating business earns more on its tangible capital; it looks worse only because it paid premiums for its acquisitions. Whether those premiums were worth paying is a separate question.

## Worked example 2: depreciation lives

Two airlines each own aircraft costing 10,000 with no salvage value. Airline X depreciates them over 25 years (400 a year); Airline Y over 15 years (about 667 a year). With identical operations, X reports operating profit about 267 higher every year. Before comparing margins, an analyst restates X on Y's 15-year life (or both on the industry norm). The cash flows of the two airlines are identical; only the accounting differs.

## Leases and EBITDA

Under IFRS 16, almost all leases are on the balance sheet, and the lessee's cost is split into **depreciation** of the right-of-use asset and **interest** on the lease liability, both below EBITDA. Under US GAAP (ASC 842), operating leases are also on the balance sheet, but the lessee reports a single straight-line **lease cost** within operating expenses, above EBITDA. So for identical leases, the **IFRS company reports higher EBITDA**. Compare EBITDA-based ratios only after adding the operating lease cost back for the US GAAP company, or removing it for both.

## How the exam tests it

- Choosing the right adjustment for a stated difference (LIFO, goodwill, leases, capitalised interest).
- Calculating an adjusted ratio and saying how the ranking changes.
- Explaining why a reported difference is accounting rather than economics.

## Exam traps

- **Adjust the numerator and denominator consistently**: removing goodwill from equity without considering amortisation or impairment history can mislead.
- **IFRS and US GAAP treat leases differently in the income statement**, so EBITDA is not comparable without adjustment.
- **Capitalised interest flatters coverage**; add it back to interest expense.
- **Adjustments change ratios, not cash**: they make companies comparable, they do not make either one richer.

## Questions people ask

### Why do analysts adjust financial statements?

Because companies use different standards and accounting choices, which change reported numbers without changing the underlying business. Adjusting puts them on one basis so that ratios compare economics rather than accounting.

### What is tangible book value?

Equity minus goodwill and other intangible assets. It shows the book value of the physical and financial assets, and is useful when comparing companies that grew by acquisition with those that grew organically.

### Why is EBITDA different under IFRS and US GAAP for leases?

IFRS splits the cost of almost every lease into depreciation and interest, both below EBITDA, while US GAAP shows operating lease cost as a single operating expense above EBITDA. Identical leases therefore give an IFRS company higher EBITDA.
