> \[\text{Interest expense} = r_{\text{market at issue}} \times \text{carrying amount},\qquad \text{coupon} = r_{\text{coupon}} \times \text{face value}\]
>
> The difference amortises the discount or premium.

## From first principles

When a company issues a bond, investors hand over what its coupons and final repayment are **worth today**, discounted at the **market rate** on the day of issue:

\[ \text{Price} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{\text{Coupon}}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{\text{Face}}{(1+r)^n} \]

- Coupon **below** the market rate → investors pay less than face value: a **discount**.
- Coupon **above** the market rate → a **premium**.
- Coupon equal to the market rate → issued at **par**.

The liability is recorded at that price. The true cost of the borrowing is the market rate at issue, so under the **effective interest method**:

\[ \text{Interest expense}_t = r \times \text{Carrying amount}_{t-1} \qquad \text{Carrying amount}_t = \text{Carrying amount}_{t-1} + \text{Interest expense}_t - \text{Coupon} \]

The coupon is fixed in cash. The difference between expense and coupon is the **amortisation** of the discount or premium, and it walks the carrying amount to face value by maturity.

## Worked example 1: a discount bond

Face 1,000, 5 years, 5% annual coupon (50 a year), market rate at issue 7%.

\[ \text{Price} = 50 \times \frac{1 - 1.07^{-5}}{0.07} + \frac{1{,}000}{1.07^{5}} = 205.0 + 713.0 = 918.0 \]

| Year | Opening carrying amount | Interest expense at 7% | Coupon | Closing carrying amount |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 918.0 | 64.3 | 50 | 932.3 |
| 2 | 932.3 | 65.3 | 50 | 947.5 |
| 3 | 947.5 | 66.3 | 50 | 963.8 |
| 4 | 963.8 | 67.5 | 50 | 981.3 |
| 5 | 981.3 | 68.7 | 50 | 1,000.0 |

Total interest expense over the life is 332.0: the 250 of coupons plus the 82.0 discount.

## Worked example 2: a premium bond

The same bond issued when the market rate is 3%:

\[ \text{Price} = 50 \times \frac{1 - 1.03^{-5}}{0.03} + \frac{1{,}000}{1.03^{5}} = 229.0 + 862.6 = 1{,}091.6 \]

Year-1 interest expense is 3% × 1,091.6 = 32.7, less than the coupon of 50, so the carrying amount **falls** by 17.3 to 1,074.3, and keeps falling to 1,000 at maturity.

## The cash flow statement

- Proceeds at issue (918.0) are a **financing** inflow; repaying the 1,000 at maturity is a financing outflow.
- The coupon paid is the cash interest. Under US GAAP it is **operating**; IFRS allows operating or financing.
- The amortisation of a discount is non-cash interest: it reduces net income but not cash, so it is **added back** in the indirect method (a premium's amortisation is subtracted).

## Exam traps

- **Discount bonds**: interest expense > coupon, rising over time. **Premium bonds**: expense < coupon, falling.
- **The market rate at issue drives the expense for the bond's whole life** under amortised cost. Later changes in market rates do not change the book value, unless the company has chosen fair value measurement.
- **Issue costs** reduce the carrying amount on day one under IFRS and US GAAP, raising the effective rate.
- **Zero-coupon bonds** are the extreme discount bond: all interest is amortisation, and operating cash flow shows none of it until maturity.

## Questions people ask

### Why is a bond issued at a discount?

Because its coupon is below the market rate investors require. They will only buy it at a price low enough that the coupons plus the gain to face value at maturity give them the market rate.

### What is the effective interest method?

A way of recording interest at the market rate at issue applied to the bond's carrying amount each period. The difference between that interest and the coupon gradually moves the carrying amount to face value.

### Does a change in market interest rates change the bond's book value?

Not under amortised cost, which most companies use for their own debt. The carrying amount follows the schedule set at issue; only debt measured at fair value moves with market rates.
