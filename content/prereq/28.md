> \[E_I = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta I},\qquad E_{AB} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_A}{\%\Delta P_B}\]

## From first principles

Price is not the only thing that moves demand. Two other elasticities measure two other forces.

**Income elasticity**: what happens to demand when buyers' incomes rise?

- **Positive: normal good.** Above 1: a **luxury** (spending rises faster than income: travel, fine dining). Between 0 and 1: a **necessity** (food staples, utilities).
- **Negative: inferior good.** People buy less as they get richer (the cheapest brands, long-distance bus travel).

**Cross-price elasticity**: what happens to demand for A when B's price changes?

- **Positive: substitutes.** B gets dearer, buyers switch to A (tea and coffee, two airlines on the same route).
- **Negative: complements.** B gets dearer, buyers use less of both (printers and ink, cars and petrol).
- **Close to zero**: the goods are unrelated.

[[figure]]

A change in income or in another good's price does not move **along** the demand curve; it **shifts** the whole curve. A higher income shifts demand right for a normal good and left for an inferior one.

[[fig:demand_shift_income|Income moves the whole demand curve, in a direction set by the type of good.]]

## Worked example 1: reading the sign

Incomes rise 4%. Demand for restaurant meals rises 6%: income elasticity 6 / 4 = 1.5, a **luxury**. Demand for own-brand pasta falls 2%: −2 / 4 = −0.5, an **inferior** good. A cinema raises prices 10% and streaming subscriptions rise 3%: cross elasticity +0.3, **substitutes**. Printer prices rise 10% and ink cartridge sales fall 5%: −0.5, **complements**.

## Worked example 2: elasticities from a demand equation

A firm estimates demand for its product as:

\[ Q = 1{,}200 - 15P + 0.02I + 8P_c \]

with own price \(P = 40\), average income \(I = 50{,}000\) and a rival's price \(P_c = 10\). Quantity is \(1{,}200 - 600 + 1{,}000 + 80 = 1{,}680\).

\[ E_I = 0.02 \times \frac{50{,}000}{1{,}680} = 0.60 \qquad E_{c} = 8 \times \frac{10}{1{,}680} = 0.05 \]

The product is a **normal good and a necessity** (0 < 0.60 < 1). The rival's product is a **substitute** (positive cross elasticity), but a weak one: a 10% rise in the rival's price raises this firm's sales by only about 0.5%.

## Why analysts care

Income elasticities tell you how a company's sales will behave over the business cycle: luxury goods makers see sales swing more than incomes; necessity producers are steadier; sellers of inferior goods can even benefit in a recession. Cross elasticities measure competition: a high positive value means customers switch easily, so pricing power is weak.

## Exam traps

- **The sign carries the meaning**: watch for negative values.
- **"Normal" and "inferior" describe the reaction to income, not quality.**
- **Luxury means income elasticity above 1**, not just positive.
- **Multiply the coefficient by the ratio of the variable to quantity**, not by the price of the good itself.

## Questions people ask

### What is the difference between a normal good and an inferior good?

Demand for a normal good rises when incomes rise; demand for an inferior good falls, because buyers switch to something they prefer once they can afford it.

### How do you tell substitutes from complements?

From the sign of the cross-price elasticity. Positive means substitutes (a dearer rival pushes buyers to you); negative means complements (a dearer partner good means fewer buyers for both).

### What does an income elasticity above 1 mean?

That spending on the good rises proportionally faster than income, which marks it as a luxury. Its sales tend to grow fast in booms and fall sharply in recessions.
