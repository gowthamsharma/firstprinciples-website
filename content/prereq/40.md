> \[\%\Delta Y \approx \%\Delta A + \alpha\,\%\Delta K + (1 - \alpha)\,\%\Delta L\]
>
> Growth comes from more capital, more labour and better ways of combining them. Only the last raises living standards for ever.

## From first principles

An economy's potential output depends on its inputs and how well it combines them. A **production function** writes this as:

\[ Y = A \cdot F(K, L) \]

with capital \(K\), labour \(L\) and **total factor productivity** \(A\), which captures technology, management, institutions and everything else that turns inputs into output.

### Growth accounting

Taking growth rates of each side gives the **growth accounting** equation in the box above. The weights \(\alpha\) and \(1 - \alpha\) are capital's and labour's shares of national income (often around a third and two-thirds). TFP growth is usually measured as the **residual**: whatever growth the inputs cannot explain.

### Diminishing returns to capital

Adding machines per worker raises output per worker, but by less and less. The first computer in an office transforms it; the fifth adds little. So **capital deepening** (more capital per worker) cannot keep living standards rising for ever.

[[figure]]

Only **TFP growth** shifts the whole relationship up, which is why it is the source of sustained growth in output per worker. It also explains **convergence**: poorer countries with little capital per worker get large gains from each unit of investment, so they can grow faster, provided institutions let that investment happen.

### Sources of growth

- **Labour**: population growth, participation, hours worked.
- **Human capital**: education, training and experience make each hour more productive.
- **Physical capital**: machines, buildings, and **public infrastructure** such as roads and networks.
- **Technology**: new products and processes, the driver of TFP.
- **Natural resources**: helpful, but neither necessary nor sufficient for growth.

**Sustainable (potential) growth** is approximately:

\[ \text{Potential GDP growth} \approx \text{growth in labour productivity} + \text{growth in the labour force} \]

## Worked example 1: growth accounting

TFP grows 1%, capital 3% and labour 0.5%, with capital's share \(\alpha = 0.35\).

\[ \%\Delta Y \approx 1\% + 0.35 \times 3\% + 0.65 \times 0.5\% = 1 + 1.05 + 0.325 = 2.375\% \approx 2.4\% \]

Output **per worker** grows by output growth minus labour growth:

\[ 2.375\% - 0.5\% = 1.875\% = 1\% + 0.35 \times (3\% - 0.5\%) \]

TFP contributes 1 point; capital deepening (capital growing faster than labour) contributes the other 0.875.

## Worked example 2: an ageing population

A country's labour productivity grows 1.5% a year and its labour force 0.8%. Potential growth ≈ 2.3%. As the population ages, labour-force growth falls to −0.2%. Unless productivity speeds up, potential growth drops to about **1.3%**. At 2.3%, real GDP doubles in about 70 ÷ 2.3 ≈ 30 years; at 1.3%, in about 54.

## Why analysts care

Long-run equity returns are tied to growth in earnings, and economy-wide earnings cannot outgrow GDP for ever. A lower potential growth rate means lower long-run returns and, usually, lower equilibrium interest rates.

## Exam traps

- **Investment-led growth fades** unless productivity also rises, because of diminishing returns to capital.
- **TFP is a residual**, measured as what is left after accounting for capital and labour.
- **Output growth and output-per-worker growth differ** by labour growth.
- **An ageing population lowers potential growth** unless productivity or participation rises.

## Questions people ask

### What drives long-run economic growth?

More labour, more capital per worker and, above all, better technology and organisation, measured as total factor productivity. Capital alone runs into diminishing returns.

### What is growth accounting?

A way of splitting GDP growth into the parts explained by labour, capital and what is left over, which is credited to productivity.

### Why does potential growth matter for equity investors?

Over long periods, aggregate corporate earnings cannot grow much faster than the economy. A country's potential growth caps the long-run earnings growth of its market.
