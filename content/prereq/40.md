> Growth accounting: g(Y) ≈ g(A) + α·g(K) + (1 − α)·g(L).

## From first principles

An economy's potential output depends on its inputs and how well it combines them. A **production function** writes this as Y = A·F(K, L): capital K, labour L, and total factor productivity A, which captures technology and efficiency.

Taking growth rates gives the **growth accounting** equation: output growth ≈ TFP growth + capital's share × capital growth + labour's share × labour growth.

Two consequences follow:

- **Capital has diminishing returns.** Adding machines per worker raises output per worker by less and less, so investment alone cannot sustain growth in living standards.
- **Technology (TFP) shifts the whole curve up**, which is the only source of permanent growth in output per worker.

[[figure]]

**Sustainable growth** of potential GDP ≈ growth in labour productivity + growth in the labour force. Other sources include human capital (education and skills), natural resources, and public infrastructure.

## Worked example

TFP grows 1%, capital 3%, labour 0.5%, capital's share α = 0.35. Potential growth ≈ 1% + 0.35 × 3% + 0.65 × 0.5% = 1 + 1.05 + 0.325 = **2.4%**.

## Exam traps

- **Investment-led growth fades** unless productivity also rises.
- An ageing population (slower labour growth) lowers potential GDP growth unless productivity accelerates.

## Questions people ask

### What drives long-run economic growth?

More labour, more capital per worker and, above all, better technology and organisation, measured as total factor productivity. Capital alone runs into diminishing returns.

### What is growth accounting?

A way of splitting GDP growth into the parts explained by labour, capital and what is left over, which is credited to productivity.

### Why does potential growth matter for equity investors?

Over long periods, aggregate corporate earnings cannot grow much faster than the economy. A country's potential growth caps the long-run earnings growth of its market.
