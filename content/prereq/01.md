> Accounts are a decision tool for people who give a company money. Judge every rule by whether it helps them decide.

## From first principles

Investors and lenders cannot walk into a company and inspect it. They need a standard report that says what the company controls and owes, how it performed and how its cash moved. The conceptual framework behind IFRS (and the very similar one behind US GAAP) states the purpose in one sentence: give **existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors** the information they need to decide whether to provide resources to the company, keep them there or take them out.

Everything else follows from that purpose. If a rule helps those users predict the amount, timing and uncertainty of future cash flows, or judge how well management has looked after the money, it earns its place. If it does not, it is clutter.

### Two fundamental qualities

Ask two questions of any piece of information:

- **Would it change a decision?** That is **relevance**. Information is relevant if it has predictive value (it helps forecast), confirmatory value (it checks earlier forecasts) or both. **Materiality** is part of relevance: an item is material if leaving it out or misstating it could reasonably change users' decisions. There is no universal percentage; it depends on the size and nature of the item for that company.
- **Does it show what really happened?** That is **faithful representation**: the description is **complete** (nothing needed is missing), **neutral** (not tilted to look better or worse) and **free from error** (no mistakes in the description or the process, which is not the same as perfectly precise).

### Four enhancing qualities, and a cost

Comparability (across companies and across years), verifiability (independent observers would reach a similar result), timeliness and understandability make relevant, faithful information **more** useful. They cannot rescue information that is irrelevant or misleading.

The constraint is **cost**: information should be worth more than it costs to produce and read. The qualities also pull against each other. The most timely figure (a quick estimate) is usually less verifiable than a figure audited months later; a current market value is more relevant than historical cost but often harder to verify.

### The five elements

The statements are built from five elements. Three describe position at a point in time, two describe performance over a period:

[[fig:elements_map|Assets, liabilities and equity give the position; income and expenses explain how equity changed.]]

- An **asset** is a present economic resource controlled by the company as a result of past events.
- A **liability** is a present obligation to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events.
- **Equity** is what is left: assets minus liabilities.
- **Income** increases equity (other than contributions from owners); **expenses** decrease it (other than distributions to owners).

Two assumptions sit underneath: the **accrual basis** (record transactions when they happen, not when cash moves) and **going concern** (the business will carry on for the foreseeable future). If going concern fails, assets can no longer be shown at values that assume continued use.

### Who sets the rules and who enforces them

Standard setters write the rules: the **IASB** issues IFRS, used in most of the world, and the **FASB** issues US GAAP. They are private, independent bodies with no power to punish anyone. **Regulators** such as the US SEC, the UK FCA or securities regulators coordinated by ESMA in Europe decide which standards listed companies must use and enforce them, with auditors checking each company's accounts.

## Worked example 1: the same item, different materiality

A retailer could report each store's monthly revenue. For a chain of 3 stores, one store's collapse changes the whole outlook: the information is material. For a chain of 3,000 stores, one store is immaterial, but a pattern across a region is not.

Size is not the only test. Suppose a company has profit of 40 million and revenue of 2 billion and leaves out a legal provision of 15 million. As a share of revenue that is

\[ \frac{15}{2{,}000} = 0.75\% \]

which sounds small. As a share of profit it is

\[ \frac{15}{40} = 37.5\% \]

which would clearly change an investor's view. The omission is material.

## Worked example 2: relevance against verifiability

An oil company reports the estimated value of its proved reserves. The estimate rests on engineers' judgement about geology, future prices and costs, so two experts could reach different figures (weak **verifiability**). It is still highly **relevant**: nothing predicts the company's future cash flows better. The framework's answer is to report it with clear disclosure of the assumptions, because an enhancing quality being weak does not cancel a fundamental one.

## How the exam tests it

- Classifying a quality as fundamental or enhancing, or spotting which one a scenario fails.
- Applying the definitions of the elements to a short case, often an unusual contract or a promise to pay in the future.
- Knowing who does what: standard setters set, regulators enforce, auditors attest.

## Exam traps

- **Only two qualities are fundamental: relevance and faithful representation.** Comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability only enhance them.
- **Neutrality is not conservatism.** Deliberately understating assets or profit is a bias, just as overstating them is. Prudence means care with judgements, not a licence to understate.
- **Materiality is judged by effect, not a fixed rule.** A small amount can be material if it turns a profit into a loss or breaks a loan covenant.
- **A contract that neither side has performed yet** (an executory contract, such as an order to buy steel next year at market price) is usually not recognised as an asset or a liability, unless it has become onerous.

## Questions people ask

### Who are financial statements written for?

Mainly for people who provide money to a company and cannot demand custom reports: shareholders, bondholders, banks and anyone thinking of joining them. Managers, regulators and tax authorities use them too, but the rules are designed around outside capital providers.

### What makes accounting information useful?

Two things above all: it must be able to change a decision (relevance) and it must show what really happened, without slant or error (faithful representation). Comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability then make good information better.

### Is being conservative the same as being neutral?

No. Deliberately low numbers are as biased as deliberately high ones. Modern frameworks ask for neutrality and treat prudence as caution in judgement, not a licence to understate.
