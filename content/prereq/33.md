> More sellers, more similar products and easier entry mean less pricing power.

## From first principles

Every market answers the same three questions, and the answers decide how much control a firm has over its price:

1. **How many sellers are there?** Many small firms cannot move the price; one dominant firm can.
2. **How similar are their products?** Identical products force identical prices. Differentiated products (brands, features, location) let each firm charge a little more without losing every customer.
3. **How hard is it to enter?** If anyone can start up, high profits attract rivals and disappear. Barriers to entry (patents, licences, huge fixed costs, network effects, control of a scarce input) protect them.

### The four structures side by side

| | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of sellers | Very many | Many | Few | One |
| Product | Identical | Differentiated | Identical or differentiated | Unique, no close substitutes |
| Barriers to entry | None | Low | High | Very high |
| Pricing power | None (price taker) | Some | Significant, but depends on rivals | Considerable |
| Non-price competition | None | Heavy: advertising, branding | Varies | Little needed |
| Example | Wheat, currency markets | Restaurants, hairdressers | Aircraft makers, mobile networks | Local water utility |

### Demand facing one firm

The structure shows up in the **demand curve the individual firm faces**:

- **Perfect competition**: perfectly elastic (horizontal) at the market price. Charge a penny more and every buyer leaves.
- **Monopolistic competition**: downward sloping but quite elastic, because close substitutes exist.
- **Oligopoly**: depends on how rivals react. If rivals match price cuts but ignore price rises, the curve is **kinked**: elastic above the current price and inelastic below it, which helps explain sticky prices.
- **Monopoly**: the whole market demand curve.

### Measuring concentration

Analysts and competition authorities need a number for "how concentrated is this market?" Two are used.

The **N-firm concentration ratio** adds up the market shares of the largest N firms:

\[ CR_N = \sum_{i=1}^{N} s_i \]

The **Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI)** squares every firm's share (in percent) and adds them up:

\[ HHI = \sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i^2 \]

Squaring gives large firms far more weight, so the HHI reacts to the size of the leader, not only to how many firms there are. It ranges from close to 0 (a huge number of tiny firms) to 10,000 (a single firm with 100%). Many competition authorities treat a market with an HHI above about 2,500 as highly concentrated, and look closely at mergers that raise it by more than a couple of hundred points.

## Worked example 1: CR and HHI

Four firms have shares of 40%, 30%, 20% and 10%.

\[ CR_3 = 40 + 30 + 20 = 90\% \]

\[ HHI = 40^2 + 30^2 + 20^2 + 10^2 = 1{,}600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = 3{,}000 \]

[[fig:hhi_areas|Each square's area is a firm's squared share. The largest firm alone supplies more than half of the HHI.]]

## Worked example 2: a merger

The firms with 20% and 10% merge, leaving shares of 40%, 30% and 30%.

\[ HHI_{\text{after}} = 1{,}600 + 900 + 900 = 3{,}400 \]

The HHI rises by **400**. A shortcut: merging firms with shares \(a\) and \(b\) raises the HHI by \(2ab = 2 \times 20 \times 10 = 400\), because \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 2ab\).

Notice what the concentration ratio misses. Before the merger CR3 was 90%; after it, CR3 = 40 + 30 + 30 = 100%. That looks like a big change. But if the two **smallest of five** firms had merged, CR3 might not move at all, while the HHI still rises. And a market of 4 equal firms (HHI 2,500) and one with shares 70/10/10/10 (HHI 5,200) have the same CR4 of 100%. The HHI separates them; the concentration ratio does not.

## How analysts use it

- **Pricing power and margins.** Firms in concentrated markets with high barriers tend to earn returns above their cost of capital for longer.
- **Merger risk.** A deal that pushes the HHI up sharply may be blocked or need remedies.
- **Concentration is not the whole story.** A market with few firms but low entry barriers ("contestable") can behave competitively, because a high price would invite entry. A market with one firm today and a disruptive technology tomorrow may not stay concentrated.

## Exam traps

- **Use shares in percent, not decimals,** for the HHI on the 0–10,000 scale. With decimals the range is 0–1.
- **The HHI is not the concentration ratio**: CR ignores how shares are split among the top firms and ignores everyone else.
- **Monopolistic competition is "many firms, differentiated products, easy entry"**, not "a few firms". Restaurants, not airlines.
- **Oligopoly is defined by interdependence**: each firm's best decision depends on what rivals do.

## Questions people ask

### What are the four market structures?

Perfect competition (many sellers of identical products), monopolistic competition (many sellers of slightly different products), oligopoly (a few large sellers) and monopoly (one seller). Pricing power rises along that list.

### Why do barriers to entry matter for investors?

Because they protect profits. Without barriers, high returns attract new competitors until profits fall back to normal; with them, a firm can keep earning above its cost of capital.

### How is an oligopoly different?

Each firm's best price depends on what its few rivals do. That interdependence can lead to price wars or to stable, quietly coordinated prices.
