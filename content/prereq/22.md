> A deferred tax asset is only worth something if there will be taxable profit to use it against.

## From first principles

Deferred taxes are future tax effects, so the right rate is the one expected **when the difference unwinds**, using rates enacted (under IFRS, substantively enacted) at the balance sheet date.

**When the rate changes**, every DTA and DTL is remeasured, and the whole adjustment goes through **income tax expense in the period the change is enacted**, even though the cash effect is years away:

- A **rate cut** shrinks DTLs (lower tax expense, good for this year's profit) and shrinks DTAs (higher tax expense).
- A **rate rise** does the opposite: DTLs grow (higher expense), DTAs grow (lower expense).

[[fig:dt_rate_change|One rate change, every deferred balance remeasured, all through this year's tax expense.]]

**Is the asset real?** A DTA, for example from tax losses carried forward, only saves tax if there will be enough future taxable profit to use it.

- **US GAAP**: recognise the full DTA, then reduce it with a **valuation allowance** for any part that is more likely than not (a better-than-even chance) never to be used.
- **IFRS**: recognise a DTA only to the extent it is probable it will be used. There is no separate allowance account, but the effect is similar.

## Worked example 1: a rate cut and an allowance

A company has tax losses of 1,000 to carry forward, at a 30% rate: DTA = 300.

- The rate is cut to 25%: DTA = 1,000 × 25% = **250**, and tax expense rises by **50** this year.
- Management then judges that half the losses are unlikely to be used: a valuation allowance of 250 × 50% = **125**, which raises tax expense by another 125. The net DTA is **125**.

## Worked example 2: a rate rise and a liability

Another company has a DTL of 400, measured at 20%, so the underlying temporary difference is 2,000. The rate rises to 25%.

\[ \text{New DTL} = 2{,}000 \times 25\% = 500 \qquad \text{Extra tax expense} = 100 \]

Profit falls by 100 this year, though no extra tax will be paid until the differences reverse.

## Reading the effective tax rate

Companies reconcile the statutory rate to the **effective tax rate** in the notes. The reconciling items explain why tax expense is not simply profit times the headline rate: foreign profits taxed at different rates, permanent differences, changes in valuation allowances, the effect of rate changes. A low effective rate that rests on one-off items, such as an allowance release, will not last.

## Why analysts watch the allowance

- **An increasing allowance** tells you management now doubts future profitability.
- **Releasing an allowance** reduces tax expense and raises profit with no improvement in the business. Done in a weak year, it smooths earnings.
- **Compare the allowance with forecasts.** A company forecasting losses while releasing its allowance is sending two contradictory messages.

## Exam traps

- **The remeasurement goes through tax expense in the period the new rate is enacted**, not when it takes effect.
- **A rate cut helps companies with net DTLs and hurts those with net DTAs.**
- **Changes in the valuation allowance flow straight to profit.**
- **US GAAP uses an allowance account; IFRS simply recognises less.**

## Questions people ask

### What happens to deferred taxes when the tax rate changes?

Every deferred tax asset and liability is remeasured at the new rate, and the difference goes through income tax expense in the period the change is enacted. A cut lowers DTLs and DTAs; a rise increases both.

### What is a valuation allowance?

Under US GAAP, a reduction in a deferred tax asset for the part that is more likely than not to go unused, usually because future taxable profit looks too low. Changes in it go straight to tax expense.

### Why can a tax cut reduce a company's profit?

If the company has more deferred tax assets than liabilities, a lower rate makes those assets worth less, and the write-down is charged to tax expense immediately, even though the cash benefit of the cut comes later.
