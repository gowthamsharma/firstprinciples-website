> Diminishing marginal returns: in the short run, the extra output from each extra worker eventually falls.

## From first principles

In the short run, a firm can hire more workers but cannot instantly build more factory space. Picture a kitchen with two stoves: the first cooks are very productive, the third and fourth less so as they queue for the stoves, and the tenth mostly gets in the way.

Three measures describe it:

\[ \text{TP} = \text{total output} \qquad \text{AP} = \frac{\text{TP}}{L} \qquad \text{MP} = \frac{\Delta \text{TP}}{\Delta L} \]

Once fixed inputs become a bottleneck, **MP falls**: that is the law of **diminishing marginal returns**. TP keeps rising, but more slowly; if MP turns negative, TP falls.

[[figure]]

The link between marginal and average is the same as with exam grades: if the next worker adds more than the current average, the average rises; if less, it falls. So **MP crosses AP at AP's maximum**.

## Worked example 1: a production table

| Workers | Total output | Marginal product | Average product |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | 20 | 20.0 |
| 2 | 45 | 25 | 22.5 |
| 3 | 63 | 18 | 21.0 |
| 4 | 75 | 12 | 18.75 |
| 5 | 80 | 5 | 16.0 |

The second worker adds 25 (increasing returns: two people can specialise). **Diminishing returns start with the third worker**, whose MP of 18 is below the second's 25. Average product peaks at the second worker: from the third on, MP is below AP, so AP falls.

## The long run: returns to scale

In the long run every input can change. The question is no longer "what does one more worker add?" but "what happens if we double everything?"

- **Increasing returns to scale**: output more than doubles. Long-run average cost falls (**economies of scale**: specialisation, bulk buying, spreading large fixed costs).
- **Constant returns**: output exactly doubles; average cost is flat.
- **Decreasing returns**: output less than doubles; average cost rises (**diseconomies of scale**: coordination problems, bureaucracy).

The lowest output at which long-run average cost reaches its minimum is the **minimum efficient scale**. Industries where it is large relative to the market tend to have few firms.

## Worked example 2: doubling the inputs

A firm doubles its workers and its machines, and output rises from 400 to 1,000 units, a 150% increase (×2.5). That is **increasing returns to scale**: average cost per unit falls as the firm grows. Had output risen to 700 (×1.75), returns to scale would be decreasing.

## Exam traps

- **Diminishing returns is a short-run idea** (some inputs fixed). **Returns to scale is a long-run idea** (all inputs change together). A firm can have both at once.
- **Diminishing returns starts when MP begins to fall**, not when TP falls.
- **MP crosses AP at AP's maximum.**
- **Productivity growth** (better technology) shifts the whole TP curve up; it is not a movement along it.

## Questions people ask

### What is the law of diminishing marginal returns?

When at least one input is fixed, adding more and more of a variable input eventually adds less and less output per extra unit. It explains why short-run marginal cost rises.

### What is the difference between diminishing returns and diseconomies of scale?

Diminishing returns happen in the short run when one input is fixed. Diseconomies of scale happen in the long run when the whole firm grows so large that coordination costs rise and average cost goes up.

### What is minimum efficient scale?

The smallest output at which a firm reaches the lowest point of its long-run average cost curve. Below it, a firm is at a cost disadvantage; above it, there is no further saving from growing.
