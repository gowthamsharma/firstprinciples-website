> Substitution effect: buy more of what got relatively cheaper. Income effect: your money now goes further.

## From first principles

A price cut does two different things to a shopper at once:

1. **Substitution effect.** The good is now cheaper **relative to other goods**, so the buyer shifts towards it. This effect always points the same way: a lower price means more demand, a higher price less.
2. **Income effect.** The buyer's money now buys more overall: **real income** has risen. What they do with it depends on the good:
   - **normal good**: they buy more, which reinforces the substitution effect;
   - **inferior good**: they buy less, which works against it.

[[figure]]

So the total change in quantity is:

\[ \text{Total effect} = \text{Substitution effect} + \text{Income effect} \]

| Type of good | Substitution effect of a price fall | Income effect | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | more | more | more (demand slopes down) |
| Inferior | more | less, but smaller | more (demand still slopes down) |
| Giffen | more | less, and larger | **less** (demand slopes up) |

A **Giffen good** is an inferior good that takes up such a large share of a poor household's budget that the income effect outweighs the substitution effect. When its price rises, the household is so much poorer that it drops the more expensive foods and buys even more of the staple. It is a theoretical possibility with very few convincing real examples.

A **Veblen good** also has demand that rises with price, but for a completely different reason: the high price itself is the attraction, because it signals status (luxury watches, designer goods). It has nothing to do with the income effect, and the good is not inferior.

## Worked example 1: splitting a change in demand

The price of a good falls. Holding real income constant, buyers would take 15 more units (the substitution effect). In fact they take only 12 more.

\[ \text{Income effect} = 12 - 15 = -3 \]

The income effect is negative, so the good is **inferior**, but it is smaller than the substitution effect, so demand still slopes downwards: it is **not** a Giffen good.

[[fig:effect_decomp|The substitution effect dominates, so demand still rises when the price falls.]]

## Worked example 2: bus fares

Bus fares fall 20%. Substitution: some people switch from driving to the bus. Income: commuters who already took the bus have money left over. If bus travel is inferior for them (they would rather drive once richer), some save towards a car. The total effect is more bus travel, but less than the substitution effect alone implies.

## Exam traps

- **The substitution effect is always opposite to the price change.**
- **Inferior does not mean upward-sloping demand.** It only weakens the downward slope, unless the income effect is larger (a Giffen good).
- **Giffen and Veblen goods both break the law of demand, for different reasons.** Giffen: a huge income effect on an inferior good. Veblen: price as a status signal.
- **Every Giffen good is inferior; not every inferior good is Giffen.**

## Questions people ask

### What is the difference between the substitution effect and the income effect?

The substitution effect is the change in demand because the good became relatively cheaper or dearer. The income effect is the change because the buyer's purchasing power changed. A price change triggers both.

### What is a Giffen good?

An inferior good for which the income effect of a price change is so strong that it outweighs the substitution effect, so people buy more when the price rises. It needs the good to be a large share of a poor household's spending.

### How is a Veblen good different from a Giffen good?

A Veblen good is wanted more at a higher price because the price signals status; it is not inferior and the income effect plays no special role. A Giffen good is inferior and its upward-sloping demand comes from the income effect.
