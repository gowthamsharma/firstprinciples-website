> \[\text{Current account} + \text{Capital account} + \text{Financial account} = 0\]
>
> Apart from errors and omissions.

## From first principles

The balance of payments records every transaction between residents of a country and the rest of the world. Like a company's books, it uses **double entry**: every payment out must be matched by something coming in.

### The three accounts

- **Current account**:
  - trade in **goods** and **services**;
  - **primary income**: interest, dividends and wages earned abroad, minus those paid to foreigners;
  - **secondary income** (current transfers): remittances, foreign aid.
- **Capital account**: capital transfers (such as debt forgiveness, migrants' transfers of assets) and sales of non-produced, non-financial assets (mining or broadcast rights, patents). Usually small.
- **Financial account**: purchases and sales of financial assets:
  - **direct investment** (a lasting controlling stake, such as building a factory abroad);
  - **portfolio investment** (shares and bonds);
  - **other investment** (loans, bank deposits, trade credit);
  - **reserve assets** of the central bank.

### The logic of the balance

A **current account deficit** (buying more than you sell) must be financed: foreigners buy your assets or lend to you, which shows up as a **net inflow in the financial account**. A current account surplus means the country is accumulating claims on the rest of the world.

The current account also equals national saving minus investment (see [the saving–investment identity](prereq-saving-investment-identity.html)). A deficit means the country invests more than it saves.

### Sign conventions

Under the international standard, the financial account records **net acquisition of foreign assets minus net incurrence of liabilities**, so a capital inflow (foreigners buying domestic bonds) appears as an increase in liabilities. Textbooks often simplify this by calling inflows a financial account "surplus". The exam focuses on the logic: deficit on one side, financing on the other.

## Worked example 1: financing a deficit

A country imports goods worth 100 more than it exports. Foreign investors buy 70 of its government bonds and a foreign company builds a factory worth 30.

- Current account: −100.
- Financial account: +70 portfolio investment, +30 direct investment = **+100** net inflow.

## Worked example 2: classifying transactions

| Transaction | Account |
|---|---|
| A domestic firm sells software licences abroad | Current (services exports) |
| Dividends received on foreign shares | Current (primary income) |
| Workers abroad send money home | Current (secondary income) |
| A foreign government forgives a loan | Capital account |
| A domestic pension fund buys foreign shares | Financial (portfolio investment, outflow) |
| A foreign carmaker builds a plant at home | Financial (direct investment, inflow) |
| The central bank buys foreign currency | Financial (reserve assets) |

Note the dividends and the shares: buying the shares is a financial account item; the dividends they later pay are current account income.

## Exam traps

- **A current account deficit is not necessarily bad.** It can fund productive investment, as in a fast-growing economy. It is risky when it funds consumption, or depends on short-term flows that can reverse.
- **Investment income is in the current account**; the purchase of the investment is in the financial account.
- **The capital account is not the financial account.** It is small and covers capital transfers.
- **Errors and omissions** absorb measurement gaps; the accounts never sum to exactly zero in practice.

## Questions people ask

### What are the parts of the balance of payments?

The current account (trade in goods and services, income and transfers), the capital account (small items such as debt forgiveness) and the financial account (investment flows and reserves).

### Why must the balance of payments balance?

Every transaction is recorded twice. A country that buys more from abroad than it sells must pay with money borrowed from or assets sold to foreigners, so a current account deficit matches a financial account surplus.

### Is a current account deficit bad?

Not necessarily. Borrowing abroad to fund productive investment can be sensible. It becomes risky when it funds consumption or depends on short-term money that can leave quickly.
