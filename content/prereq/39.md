> Aggregate demand and aggregate supply set the price level and real output. Demand shocks move them together; supply shocks move them apart.

## From first principles

Microeconomic supply and demand price one good. **Aggregate** demand and supply do the same for the whole economy: the vertical axis is the **price level** (a GDP deflator, say) and the horizontal axis is **real GDP**.

### Aggregate demand slopes down

At a higher price level, total spending on real output falls, for three reasons:

1. **Wealth effect**: the real value of money and other nominal assets falls, so households feel poorer and spend less.
2. **Interest rate effect**: people need more money for the same purchases, which pushes interest rates up and discourages borrowing.
3. **Real exchange rate effect**: domestic goods become dearer relative to foreign ones, so exports fall and imports rise.

AD **shifts** with anything that changes spending at a given price level: household confidence and wealth, business expectations, fiscal policy (government spending, taxes), monetary policy (interest rates, credit), foreign growth and the exchange rate.

### Two supply curves

**Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)** slopes up. Wages and many input prices are fixed by contracts for a while, so when output prices rise, margins widen and firms produce more. SRAS shifts with **input costs**: wages, energy and commodity prices, and with expectations of inflation built into wage deals.

**Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS)** is vertical at **potential GDP**: once wages and costs fully adjust, output depends only on labour, capital, natural resources and technology. Nothing about the price level changes those.

### The output gap

\[ \text{Output gap} = \frac{\text{Actual GDP} - \text{Potential GDP}}{\text{Potential GDP}} \]

- **Positive (inflationary) gap**: the economy runs above capacity. Unemployment falls below its natural rate and wage growth picks up.
- **Negative (recessionary) gap**: spare capacity, rising unemployment and falling inflation pressure.

## Worked example 1: a demand boom and the adjustment

Potential GDP is 1,000. A surge in confidence shifts AD right and real GDP rises to 1,030 in the short run, with a higher price level.

\[ \text{Output gap} = \frac{1{,}030 - 1{,}000}{1{,}000} = +3\% \]

Workers soon demand higher wages to catch up with prices. As wages rise, SRAS shifts left. Output falls back towards 1,000 and the price level rises further. In the long run, the boom leaves **only a higher price level**.

## Worked example 2: telling shocks apart

| Shock | Curve that shifts | Price level | Real GDP | Unemployment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Household confidence rises | AD right | Up | Up | Down |
| Central bank raises rates sharply | AD left | Down (or slower inflation) | Down | Up |
| Oil price doubles | SRAS left | Up | Down | Up: **stagflation** |
| Productivity breakthrough | LRAS and SRAS right | Down | Up | Little change |

The oil shock is the policymaker's worst case. Easing policy to support output adds to inflation; tightening to fight inflation deepens the recession.

## Exam traps

- **Demand shocks move prices and output in the same direction**; supply shocks move them in opposite directions.
- **A change in the price level moves along AD**; it does not shift it.
- **Only shifts in LRAS** (more labour, capital or productivity) raise output permanently.
- **Higher expected inflation shifts SRAS left**, because it feeds into wage demands.

## Questions people ask

### What shifts aggregate demand?

Changes in household wealth and confidence, business expectations, interest rates, government spending and taxes, the exchange rate and growth abroad.

### What causes stagflation?

A drop in short-run aggregate supply, for example an oil price shock. Output falls while prices rise, which leaves central banks with a painful choice.

### What is the output gap?

The difference between actual output and the economy's potential. A positive gap means it is running hot and inflation tends to rise; a negative gap means spare capacity and falling inflation pressure.
