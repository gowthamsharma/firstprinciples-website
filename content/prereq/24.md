> Retiring debt for more than its book value is a loss; for less, a gain. Either way it says little about the business.

## From first principles

A bond sits on the balance sheet at its **carrying amount** (amortised cost), set by the market rate when it was issued. Its **market price** moves every day with interest rates and the company's credit quality. If the company retires the bond early, by calling it at a set **call price** or buying it in the market, the difference is recognised in profit:

\[ \text{Gain (loss) on extinguishment} = \text{Carrying amount} - \text{Amount paid} \]

Any unamortised issue costs are part of the carrying amount, so they are written off at the same time.

[[figure]]

Why would the price differ from book value?

- **Rates fell, or credit improved**, since issue: the bond's market price is above its carrying amount, and a call price is usually above par anyway. Retiring it creates a **loss**. Companies often do this to refinance at a lower rate.
- **Rates rose, or credit worsened**: the price is below the carrying amount. Buying the bond back creates a **gain**. A struggling company can book a "gain" precisely because investors fear it may not pay.

## Worked example 1: a call above book value

A bond carried at 960 is called at 990.

\[ \text{Loss on extinguishment} = 960 - 990 = -30 \]

The loss is reported in profit, usually separately from operating results. The cash of 990 leaves through **financing**. In the indirect method the loss is **added back** to net income in operating cash flow, because it used no operating cash.

## Worked example 2: a buyback after rates rose

Another bond is carried at 980. Market rates have risen since issue and the company buys the bonds back in the market for 900.

\[ \text{Gain on extinguishment} = 980 - 900 = 80 \]

Profit rises by 80, but nothing about the business improved; the gain came from the bond's market price falling. In the indirect method the 80 is **subtracted** from net income, and the 900 paid is a financing outflow.

## What to read in the debt notes

Companies disclose, by instrument, the amounts, maturities, interest rates, security and **covenants**: limits on borrowing, dividends or asset sales, and minimum ratios such as interest coverage. They also disclose a maturity schedule for the next five years. Breaching a covenant can let lenders demand repayment, which is why analysts check how close the company is to each limit.

## Exam traps

- **Gain or loss = carrying amount − price paid**, not face value − price paid.
- **The gain or loss is non-operating** and should be excluded from forecasts of core earnings.
- **Cash flow**: the payment is financing; the gain or loss is reversed in operating cash flow (subtract a gain, add back a loss).
- **A gain on buying back debt can be a bad sign**: the bond traded below book because lenders were worried.

## Questions people ask

### Why would a company pay more than book value to repay a bond?

Usually because rates have fallen and it can refinance more cheaply. The loss on extinguishment is the price of escaping a high coupon, and it can be repaid many times over in lower interest.

### Is a gain on debt extinguishment good news?

Not necessarily. It often arises because the bond's price fell, either because market rates rose or because investors became worried about the company's credit.

### Where does a bond buyback appear in the cash flow statement?

The amount paid is a financing outflow. Any gain or loss is a non-cash, non-operating item, so it is reversed out of net income in the operating section.
