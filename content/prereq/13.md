> Net income is cash plus accruals. Undo the accruals and you are left with cash.

## From first principles

Net income counts revenue when earned and expenses when incurred. Operating cash flow counts money in and out. The **indirect method** starts from net income and removes everything that made profit differ from cash, in three groups:

1. **Add back non-cash expenses**: depreciation, amortisation, impairments, and increases in deferred tax liabilities. They reduced profit but no cash left.
2. **Remove non-operating gains and losses**: subtract a gain on selling equipment, add back a loss. The cash from the sale belongs in investing, at the full proceeds.
3. **Adjust for changes in operating working capital**:
   - an **increase in an operating asset** (receivables, inventory, prepaid expenses) **uses** cash: subtract it;
   - an **increase in an operating liability** (payables, accrued expenses, unearned revenue) **provides** cash: add it;
   - decreases work the other way round.

[[figure]]

The sign rule is the one most people get wrong, so think it through once. Receivables rose by 25: sales of 25 were counted in profit but not yet collected, so cash is 25 lower than profit. Payables rose by 20: costs of 20 were counted in profit but not yet paid, so cash is 20 higher.

[[fig:wc_signs|Assets and liabilities move cash in opposite directions.]]

## Worked example 1: the basic reconciliation

Net income 100, depreciation 40, gain on sale of equipment 10, receivables up 25, inventory up 15, payables up 20.

\[ \text{CFO} = 100 + 40 - 10 - 25 - 15 + 20 = 110 \]

## Worked example 2: a fuller set of adjustments

Net income 250. Depreciation 60, an impairment of 30, a gain on the sale of land of 15. Receivables fell by 20, inventory rose by 40, prepaid expenses rose by 5, payables fell by 10, accrued liabilities rose by 12, the deferred tax liability rose by 8.

| Item | Adjustment |
|---|---|
| Net income | 250 |
| Depreciation | +60 |
| Impairment | +30 |
| Gain on sale of land | −15 |
| Receivables fell | +20 |
| Inventory rose | −40 |
| Prepaid expenses rose | −5 |
| Payables fell | −10 |
| Accrued liabilities rose | +12 |
| Deferred tax liability rose | +8 |
| **CFO** | **310** |

## The direct method, from the other side

The **direct method** lists the actual cash receipts and payments. Each line is an income statement figure adjusted by the related balance sheet change:

\[ \text{Cash from customers} = \text{Revenue} - \Delta\text{Receivables} + \Delta\text{Unearned revenue} \]

\[ \text{Cash paid to suppliers} = \text{Cost of sales} + \Delta\text{Inventory} - \Delta\text{Payables} \]

With revenue of 1,000 and receivables up 30, cash collected is **970**. With cost of sales of 600, inventory up 20 and payables up 15, cash paid to suppliers is 600 + 20 − 15 = **605**. Both methods give the same CFO; IFRS encourages the direct method, but almost everyone uses the indirect one.

## How analysts use it

The reconciliation shows **why** cash and profit differ. Profit that is consistently higher than CFO because receivables and inventory keep growing is lower-quality profit. CFO boosted by stretching payables, delaying payment to suppliers, can only last so long. Free cash flow, CFO minus capital spending, starts from this number.

## Exam traps

- **An increase in a current asset is subtracted; an increase in a current liability is added.**
- **Gains are subtracted and losses added**, and the full sale proceeds go to investing.
- **Only operating working capital counts.** Changes in short-term borrowing or dividends payable are financing, not operating.
- **Depreciation is not a source of cash.** Adding it back only undoes a charge that never used cash.

## Questions people ask

### Why is depreciation added back in the cash flow statement?

Because it reduced net income without any cash leaving the company that year. The cash left when the asset was bought, and that appeared in investing cash flow.

### Do the direct and indirect methods give different answers?

No. They reach the same operating cash flow; the direct method shows the receipts and payments, the indirect method shows why profit and cash differ.

### Why does an increase in receivables reduce operating cash flow?

Because sales were counted in profit but the cash has not arrived yet. The bigger the jump in receivables, the more profit is still waiting to turn into cash.
