> Percentages remove size, so you can compare a 50m company with a 900m one.

## From first principles

Raw numbers cannot compare a small firm with a large one, or a firm with itself ten years ago. Two simple transformations fix that:

- **Vertical (common-size) analysis** divides every line by a total for the same period. On the income statement the base is **revenue**, so each line becomes "cents per unit of sales": the cost structure laid bare. On the balance sheet the base is **total assets**, so each line shows what the firm invests in and how it is financed.
- **Horizontal analysis** follows one line across years, either as growth rates or as an index with the base year set to 100.

Used together they answer two different questions: vertical asks "what is this company made of?", horizontal asks "what is changing?". Comparing against peers in the same year is **cross-sectional** analysis; comparing a firm with its own past is **trend** (time-series) analysis.

## Worked example 1: same margin, different risk

Two companies both earn 11 cents of net profit per unit of revenue. Company A spends 62% of revenue on cost of sales and 20% on operating expenses; Company B spends 45% and 38%. Assume cost of sales moves with revenue and operating expenses are fixed. With revenue of 1,000 each:

| | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Operating profit now | 1,000 − 620 − 200 = 180 | 1,000 − 450 − 380 = 170 |
| Revenue falls 10% | 900 − 558 − 200 = 142 | 900 − 405 − 380 = 115 |
| Change in operating profit | −21% | −32% |

B's profit falls more because more of its costs are fixed: it has higher **operating leverage**. The common-size statement revealed the cost structure; the raw margins did not.

## Worked example 2: a margin squeezed

Year 1: revenue 1,000, cost of sales 58% of revenue, operating expenses 250. Year 2: revenue rises 10% to 1,100, cost of sales rises to 62%, operating expenses are unchanged at 250.

\[ \text{Year 1 operating margin} = \frac{1{,}000 - 580 - 250}{1{,}000} = 17.0\% \]

\[ \text{Year 2 operating margin} = \frac{1{,}100 - 682 - 250}{1{,}100} = \frac{168}{1{,}100} = 15.3\% \]

Revenue grew, but the margin fell because each unit of sales now costs more to make. The common-size line alone cannot say whether input prices rose or selling prices fell; the notes and the industry can.

## Horizontal analysis in practice

Over three years, revenue grows from 100 to 115 (index) while receivables grow from 100 to 140. Receivables growing much faster than sales means customers are paying more slowly, or revenue is being recognised earlier than cash can follow. Either deserves a closer look.

## How the exam tests it

- Converting a statement to common-size and reading the cost structure.
- Explaining a change in a common-size line, or comparing two firms of different size.
- Linking a high share of fixed costs to bigger swings in profit.

## Exam traps

- **Vertical** analysis divides by a total within one period; **horizontal** compares a line with a base year.
- **A rising cost-of-sales percentage** can mean higher input prices or lower selling prices: the percentage alone does not tell you which.
- **Equal margins do not mean equal risk.** The mix of fixed and variable costs decides how profit reacts when sales change.
- **Different accounting choices** (inventory method, leases, capitalising costs) change common-size figures, so adjust before comparing firms.

## Questions people ask

### What is the difference between vertical and horizontal analysis?

Vertical (common-size) analysis divides every line by a base in the same year, such as revenue or total assets. Horizontal analysis tracks how one line changes across years. Using both shows structure and trend.

### Why is common-size analysis useful for comparing companies?

It strips out size. A company with revenue of 10 billion and one with 100 million can be compared line by line once everything is a percentage of revenue.

### What should you look for first in a common-size income statement?

Gross margin and the big expense lines. A shrinking gross margin points to pricing or input costs; rising overheads as a share of sales point to weaker cost control.
