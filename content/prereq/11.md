> Equity is not a pile of cash. It is what would be left for the owners if every asset were turned into its book value and every debt paid.

## From first principles

\[ \text{Equity} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities} \]

Equity is a **residual**: it has no value of its own, only what is left after lenders are covered. Its components tell the story of where that residual came from:

- **Contributed capital** (share capital and share premium, or common stock and additional paid-in capital): what owners paid in when shares were issued.
- **Retained earnings**: all profits since the company began, minus all dividends.
- **Accumulated other comprehensive income** (reserves under IFRS): gains and losses parked outside profit (see [comprehensive income](prereq-comprehensive-income.html)).
- **Treasury shares**: shares the company has bought back and still holds. Shown as a **deduction** from equity; they have no votes and receive no dividends.
- **Non-controlling interest (minority interest)**: the share of a subsidiary's equity owned by outsiders. Shown inside equity, separately from the parent's shareholders' equity.
- **Preferred shares**, when they have equity features, sit in equity ahead of ordinary shareholders' claims.

### What changes equity, and what only rearranges it

The **statement of changes in equity** reconciles each component from the start to the end of the year:

\[ \text{Closing} = \text{Opening} + \text{Net income} + \text{OCI} + \text{Shares issued} - \text{Buybacks} - \text{Dividends} \]

Some transactions only move amounts **between** components and leave total equity unchanged:

- a **stock (bonus) dividend** moves an amount from retained earnings to contributed capital;
- a **stock split** changes the number of shares and the par value per share, nothing else.

Neither involves cash, so neither changes total equity or the company's value; each share is simply a smaller slice.

## Worked example 1: the statement of changes in equity

Opening equity 500. During the year: net income 80, OCI 15, new shares issued for 40, dividends 30, buybacks 25.

\[ 500 + 80 + 15 + 40 - 30 - 25 = 580 \]

Each line maps to a component: net income and dividends to retained earnings, OCI to reserves, the issue to contributed capital, the buyback to treasury shares.

## Worked example 2: what a buyback does per share

A company has equity of 1,000 and 100 shares, so book value per share is 10.00. Its shares trade at 15. It buys back 10 shares at the market price: cash and equity both fall by 150.

\[ \text{Book value per share after} = \frac{1{,}000 - 150}{100 - 10} = \frac{850}{90} = 9.44 \]

Buying back shares at **more than book value lowers book value per share** (and buying below book raises it). Earnings per share usually rises, because the same profit is shared by fewer shares: with net income of 100, EPS goes from 1.00 to about 1.11, before the lost interest on the cash spent.

## How the exam tests it

- Rolling equity forward from a list of events.
- Identifying which transactions change total equity and which only move amounts between components.
- The effect of a buyback on book value per share, EPS and leverage.

## Exam traps

- **Treasury shares are a deduction from equity, not an asset.**
- **Stock dividends and splits do not change total equity**, cash or the company's value.
- **Dividends are a distribution, not an expense**; they never reduce net income.
- **Buybacks raise leverage**: equity falls while debt is unchanged, so debt-to-equity rises.
- **Non-controlling interest is part of equity** under both IFRS and US GAAP, but it does not belong to the parent's shareholders.

## Questions people ask

### What is the difference between equity and market capitalisation?

Equity is the accounting residual at book values; market capitalisation is the share price times the number of shares. The market prices expected future earnings and many assets the balance sheet does not show, so the two often differ widely.

### Why do companies buy back shares?

To return cash to owners when they have no better use for it, to offset dilution from employee share schemes, or because they think the shares are cheap. Buybacks are more flexible than dividends and raise earnings per share.

### Can equity be negative?

Yes. Large accumulated losses, or buybacks and dividends funded with debt, can push liabilities above assets. Negative equity is a warning for a struggling firm, but some highly profitable companies run it deliberately after years of buybacks.
