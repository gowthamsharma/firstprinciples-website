> Spending, credit and confidence feed on each other, so real output overshoots and undershoots its trend.

## From first principles

Real GDP does not grow smoothly along its potential. The **business cycle** has four phases:

| Phase | Output | Output gap | Unemployment | Inflation | Typical policy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Recovery** | Starts rising from the trough | Negative, closing | High but peaking | Low | Still easy |
| **Expansion** | Grows above trend | Turns positive | Falling | Rising | Tightening begins |
| **Slowdown** | Growth fades near the peak | Positive, at its largest | Low | Highest | Tight |
| **Contraction** | Falls (a recession if it lasts) | Turns negative | Rising | Falling with a lag | Easing |

### Why cycles happen

- **Inventories**: when sales slow, firms find shelves full and cut orders sharply, which amplifies the downturn. When sales recover, they restock, which amplifies the upturn. Watch the **inventory-to-sales ratio**: rising early in a downturn, falling early in a recovery.
- **Capital spending**: firms invest when they run near capacity and expect demand to grow, then cut back hard when profits fall.
- **Credit**: lending is easy in good times and tight in bad, which reinforces both.

### How the main sectors behave

- **Consumers**: spending on **durables** (cars, appliances) swings most, because purchases can be postponed; services and staples are steadier.
- **Housing**: very sensitive to **interest rates** and credit. It often turns early, and its swings spread to construction, furnishings and banks.
- **External trade**: imports rise with domestic demand (pro-cyclical); exports depend on **foreign** growth and the exchange rate.

### Economic indicators

- **Leading** indicators turn before the economy: building permits, new orders, share prices, the yield curve's slope, average weekly hours.
- **Coincident** indicators move with it: industrial production, real incomes, retail sales, payroll employment.
- **Lagging** indicators turn after it: average duration of unemployment, unit labour costs, the inventory-to-sales ratio, bank lending rates.

## Worked example 1: tracing a tightening

A central bank raises rates sharply. Mortgage costs rise; housing starts fall first; furniture and appliance sales follow; consumer confidence drops; durable spending slows; imports fall with demand. Exports may hold up if trading partners are still growing. Unemployment rises last, after output has already turned down.

## Worked example 2: reading the signals

An analyst sees that new orders and building permits have fallen for four months, industrial production is flat, and unemployment is still at a cycle low. The best reading: the economy is **near a peak, heading into a slowdown**. The leading indicators have turned, the coincident ones are stalling, and the lagging one has not yet reacted. Low unemployment does not mean the expansion is safe; it is the last to move.

## Why analysts care

Sector performance follows the cycle. **Cyclical** sectors (autos, housebuilders, capital goods, materials) gain most in recoveries and suffer most in contractions; **defensive** sectors (utilities, food, healthcare) are steadier. Credit spreads tend to widen ahead of recessions.

## Exam traps

- **Unemployment lags**: firms hold on to staff into a downturn and hire cautiously in recoveries.
- **Inflation usually peaks late in the cycle**, near or after output's peak.
- **Imports rise in expansions**, which can worsen the trade balance just when the economy is strong.
- **The inventory-to-sales ratio is lagging**: it rises after sales have already fallen.

## Questions people ask

### What are the phases of the business cycle?

Recovery from a trough, expansion, a peak or slowdown, and contraction. Each phase has typical patterns in inventories, hiring, inflation and interest rates.

### Which sectors do well in each phase?

Cyclical sectors such as industrials, materials and consumer discretionary tend to lead early in recovery. Defensive sectors such as utilities, staples and healthcare hold up better in a contraction.

### Why are inventories important in the cycle?

Firms over-order in good times and cut orders sharply when sales slow, which magnifies swings in production. Rising inventory-to-sales ratios are an early warning of a slowdown.
