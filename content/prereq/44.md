> \[\text{Money multiplier} = \frac{1}{\text{reserve requirement}}\]

## From first principles

Money does three jobs:

- a **medium of exchange**: no need to barter;
- a **unit of account**: prices in one common unit;
- a **store of value**: spend later.

Anything that does all three well counts. Today that is mostly **bank deposits**, not notes and coins.

### How banks create money

In a **fractional reserve** system, banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend the rest. The loan is spent, and the seller deposits it in another bank, which keeps its fraction and lends the rest again. Each round creates a smaller new deposit, and the series sums to:

\[ \text{Total deposits} = \text{Initial reserves} \times \left(1 + (1 - r) + (1 - r)^2 + \dots\right) = \frac{\text{Initial reserves}}{r} \]

where \(r\) is the reserve requirement.

### Leaks that shrink the multiplier

The formula is a **maximum**. The real multiplier is smaller when:

- people hold some of their money as **cash** rather than deposits (a currency drain);
- banks keep **excess reserves**, because they are cautious or loan demand is weak;
- borrowers simply do not want to borrow.

After the 2008 crisis, central banks added enormous reserves, but broad money grew far less than the simple multiplier implied: banks sat on the reserves.

## Measuring money

**Narrow money** (notes, coins and deposits you can spend immediately) and **broad money** (adding savings and time deposits) have country-specific labels such as M1 and M2. The **monetary base** (notes and coins plus banks' reserves at the central bank) is what the central bank controls directly. Broad money is the measure usually linked to inflation over long periods.

## Worked example 1: the maximum

A central bank adds 1,000 of reserves; the reserve requirement is 10%.

| Round | New deposit | Kept as reserves (10%) | Lent on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,000 | 100 | 900 |
| 2 | 900 | 90 | 810 |
| 3 | 810 | 81 | 729 |
| … | … | … | … |
| Total | **10,000** | 1,000 | 9,000 |

The maximum increase in deposits is \(1{,}000 / 0.10 = 10{,}000\), of which 9,000 is new lending. At the end, all 1,000 of new reserves are held against the new deposits.

## Worked example 2: cautious banks

The same 1,000 of reserves, but banks choose to hold 20% of deposits as reserves (10% required, 10% extra). The effective multiplier is \(1 / 0.20 = 5\), so deposits rise by at most **5,000**, half the textbook figure.

## Exam traps

- **The multiplier is a maximum.** Cash holding, excess reserves and weak loan demand all shrink it.
- **The central bank controls the monetary base**, not directly the money supply.
- **Total deposits include the initial deposit**; new lending is total deposits minus the initial reserves.
- **A lower reserve requirement raises the multiplier.**

## Questions people ask

### How do banks create money?

When a bank makes a loan it credits the borrower's account, creating a new deposit. That deposit is spent and lands in another bank, which can lend part of it again.

### What is the money multiplier?

In the simple model, total deposits can reach the first deposit divided by the reserve ratio. With a 10% reserve requirement, 100 of new reserves can support up to 1,000 of deposits.

### Does the multiplier work like that in practice?

Only roughly. Many central banks no longer set binding reserve requirements, and lending depends more on demand for loans and on capital rules than on reserves.
