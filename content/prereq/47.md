> Ricardo: trade because technologies differ. Heckscher–Ohlin: trade because factor endowments differ.

## From first principles

Comparative advantage says countries should trade. These two models explain **why** opportunity costs differ in the first place.

### The Ricardian model

- **One factor of production**: labour.
- Countries differ in **technology**: the productivity of their labour in each industry.
- Each exports the good in which its labour is **relatively** most productive.

Gains come from specialisation. Because there is only one factor, the model says nothing about **who inside a country** gains or loses: all workers share in the gain. It predicts that countries may specialise completely.

### The Heckscher–Ohlin model

- **Two factors**: capital and labour (or skilled and unskilled labour, land, and so on).
- **The same technology** everywhere.
- Countries differ in how much of each factor they have.

A **capital-abundant** country exports **capital-intensive** goods; a **labour-abundant** country exports **labour-intensive** goods. Each exports the services of its plentiful factor, embodied in goods.

### Who gains: the distribution effect

Heckscher–Ohlin adds a prediction about **income distribution**. Trade raises demand for the goods that use a country's abundant factor intensively, so it raises the return to that factor, and lowers the return to its **scarce** factor. In a capital-rich economy, owners of capital gain from trade and low-skilled workers in import-competing industries can lose. In a labour-rich economy, workers gain.

Both models agree that the country **as a whole** gains. Heckscher–Ohlin explains why the gains are contested.

| | Ricardian | Heckscher–Ohlin |
|---|---|---|
| Source of comparative advantage | Differences in technology (labour productivity) | Differences in factor endowments |
| Factors of production | One (labour) | Two or more |
| Technology across countries | Different | The same |
| Effect on income distribution | None predicted | Abundant factor gains, scarce factor loses |
| Specialisation | Can be complete | Usually partial |

## Worked example 1: a Ricardian comparison

Output per worker-day:

| | Phones | Shirts |
|---|---|---|
| Home | 4 | 20 |
| Foreign | 1 | 10 |

Home's labour is more productive in both. But relative to Foreign, Home is **4 times** as productive in phones and only **2 times** as productive in shirts. Home's comparative advantage is in phones; Foreign's is in shirts. The opportunity cost of a phone is 5 shirts at Home and 10 shirts in Foreign. The source is a technology gap, so this is Ricardian.

## Worked example 2: a Heckscher–Ohlin scenario

Country A has many machines per worker; country B has many workers per machine. Both have access to the same technology. A exports machinery and imports clothing; B does the opposite. After trade opens:

- in A, returns to capital rise and wages in the clothing industry come under pressure;
- in B, wages rise and returns to capital fall.

## Exam traps

- **Ricardo = technology; Heckscher–Ohlin = factor endowments.** The exam often tests which model explains a given scenario.
- **Only Heckscher–Ohlin** predicts changes in the distribution of income.
- **Comparative advantage is about relative productivity**, not absolute: a country more productive in everything still has a comparative disadvantage somewhere.
- **"Same technology everywhere"** is a Heckscher–Ohlin assumption, not Ricardian.

## Questions people ask

### What explains trade in the Ricardian model?

Differences in technology, meaning labour productivity. Each country exports the good where its productivity advantage is largest.

### What explains trade in the Heckscher–Ohlin model?

Differences in resources. A country with plenty of capital exports capital-heavy goods; one with plenty of labour exports labour-heavy goods.

### Who wins and loses under Heckscher–Ohlin?

The owners of the abundant resource gain and the owners of the scarce resource lose, which helps explain why trade can hurt low-skilled workers in rich countries.
