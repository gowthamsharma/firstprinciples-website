> \[\text{Unemployment rate} = \frac{\text{Unemployed}}{\text{Labour force}}\]
>
> Only cyclical unemployment measures the output gap.

## From first principles

The **labour force** is everyone working or actively looking for work. Everyone else of working age (students, retirees, carers, people who have given up) is **outside** it.

\[ \text{Labour force} = \text{Employed} + \text{Unemployed} \qquad \text{Participation rate} = \frac{\text{Labour force}}{\text{Working-age population}} \]

Someone who wants a job but has stopped looking (a **discouraged worker**) is not counted as unemployed. So the unemployment rate can **fall** in a weak economy simply because people give up. The participation rate catches this. **Underemployed** workers (part-time but wanting full-time, or overqualified for their job) are counted as employed, which also flatters the headline rate.

### Three kinds of unemployment

- **Frictional**: people between jobs; searching and matching take time even in a strong economy.
- **Structural**: skills or locations do not match the available jobs, after technology or trade changes. Slow to fix: it needs retraining or relocation.
- **Cyclical**: not enough demand, so it rises in recessions and falls in expansions.

Frictional plus structural unemployment make up the **natural rate** (sometimes called the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment): unemployment when the economy is at potential output. Pushing unemployment below it tends to raise wage growth and inflation.

\[ \text{Cyclical unemployment} = \text{Actual rate} - \text{Natural rate} \]

## Worked example 1: computing the rates

Working-age population 1,000; employed 600; unemployed and searching 40; discouraged 20 (and 340 others not seeking work).

\[ \text{Labour force} = 600 + 40 = 640 \qquad \text{Unemployment rate} = \frac{40}{640} = 6.25\% \qquad \text{Participation} = \frac{640}{1{,}000} = 64\% \]

If the 20 discouraged workers had kept looking, the rate would be \(60 / 660 = 9.1\%\).

## Worked example 2: a misleading improvement

Next year, employment is unchanged at 600, but 10 of the unemployed stop looking. Unemployed falls to 30, labour force to 630.

\[ \text{Unemployment rate} = \frac{30}{630} = 4.8\% \]

The headline rate fell from 6.25% to 4.8% without a single new job. Participation fell from 64% to 63%. An analyst should look at both.

## Worked example 3: splitting the rate

The natural rate is estimated at 4.5% and actual unemployment is 7.0%. Cyclical unemployment is **2.5 percentage points**, a sign of a recessionary gap and room for the economy to grow without much wage pressure.

## Exam traps

- **Discouraged workers are not in the labour force**, so they are not in the unemployment rate.
- **Unemployment is a lagging indicator**: firms hold on to staff into a downturn and hire cautiously in recoveries.
- **Productivity** (output per hour) often rises early in recoveries, because output recovers before hiring does.
- **The natural rate is not zero**: frictional and structural unemployment exist even at full employment.

## Questions people ask

### What are the types of unemployment?

Frictional (between jobs), structural (skills or locations that no longer match jobs) and cyclical (caused by a weak economy). Only the cyclical part responds directly to stimulus.

### Why is unemployment called a lagging indicator?

Firms wait to be sure a downturn is real before laying people off, and wait to be sure of a recovery before hiring. So unemployment turns after the economy does.

### What is the natural rate of unemployment?

The rate when there is no cyclical unemployment, only frictional and structural. Pushing unemployment below it for long tends to raise inflation.
