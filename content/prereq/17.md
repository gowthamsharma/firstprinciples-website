> An asset cannot be shown at more than the benefit it will bring. For inventory, that benefit is what it will sell for.

## From first principles

Inventory is carried at cost until it is sold. But if the goods will sell for less than they cost, showing them at cost would overstate assets and push a loss that has already happened into a later year. So inventory is measured at the **lower** of cost and a measure of what it is worth now.

**Net realisable value (NRV)** is that measure:

\[ \text{NRV} = \text{Estimated selling price} - \text{Estimated costs to complete and sell} \]

[[figure]]

### The rules, side by side

| | IFRS | US GAAP: FIFO or weighted average | US GAAP: LIFO or retail method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Measured at | lower of cost and NRV | lower of cost and NRV | lower of cost or **market** |
| Reversal if value recovers | **yes**, up to the original write-down | no | no |

Under US GAAP for LIFO and the retail method, **market** means replacement cost, but kept inside a band: no higher than NRV (the ceiling) and no lower than NRV minus a normal profit margin (the floor). In practice, market is the **middle value** of replacement cost, the ceiling and the floor.

A few producers and dealers are different: agricultural and forest products, minerals, and commodity broker-traders may carry inventory at NRV or fair value less costs to sell, with changes in profit.

## Worked example 1: an NRV write-down and its reversal

1,000 units cost 100 each. A new model arrives, and the old units can now sell for 90 with selling costs of 8.

\[ \text{NRV} = 90 - 8 = 82 \qquad \text{Write-down} = (100 - 82) \times 1{,}000 = 18{,}000 \]

The 18,000 is an expense, usually inside cost of sales. Next quarter, demand recovers and NRV rises to 88.

- **IFRS:** reverse \((88 - 82) \times 1{,}000 = 6{,}000\), reducing cost of sales. Inventory goes back up to 88 a unit, never above the original 100.
- **US GAAP:** no reversal. The 82 becomes the new cost.

## Worked example 2: lower of cost or market

A US company using LIFO holds goods that cost 50. Replacement cost is 38, NRV is 45 and the normal profit margin is 5.

- Ceiling = NRV = 45. Floor = 45 − 5 = 40.
- Replacement cost 38 is below the floor, so market = **40**.
- Lower of cost (50) or market (40) = 40: write down by 10 a unit.

## What a write-down does to the analysis

In the period of the write-down: higher cost of sales, lower gross margin and profit, lower inventory and current assets. In the next period: lower inventory makes inventory turnover look better and, if the goods sell at their new value, margins look normal again. Repeated large write-downs point to weak demand, obsolete products or over-ordering. Under IFRS, reversals can lift profit in a recovery year, so check the notes.

## Exam traps

- **NRV deducts the costs to complete and sell** from the selling price.
- **IFRS reversals are capped** at the original write-down: inventory never goes back above cost.
- **US GAAP never reverses** inventory write-downs.
- **The market floor and ceiling apply only to LIFO and the retail method** under US GAAP.
- **Write-downs lower profit and inventory now**, then raise inventory turnover next period.

## Questions people ask

### What is net realisable value?

The price the inventory is expected to sell for in the normal course of business, minus the costs still needed to finish and sell it. If NRV is below cost, inventory is written down to NRV.

### Can an inventory write-down be reversed?

Under IFRS, yes, if the value recovers, but only up to the original cost. Under US GAAP, no: the written-down amount becomes the new cost basis.

### Why do inventory write-downs matter to analysts?

They show that goods were bought or made that will not earn their cost back. Frequent write-downs point to weak demand, obsolete products or poor purchasing, and IFRS reversals can make a later year look better than the business really is.
