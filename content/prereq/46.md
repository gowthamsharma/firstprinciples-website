> Trade on opportunity cost, not on who is better at everything.

## From first principles

**Absolute advantage** means producing a good with fewer resources. **Comparative advantage** means producing it at a lower **opportunity cost**: giving up less of other goods to make it.

The surprising part: even a country that is better at producing everything gains from trading, provided the two countries give up different amounts of one good to make the other. Each specialises in what it gives up least to make, and trades for the rest.

### Finding the opportunity costs

Atlantis needs 2 hours for a unit of cloth and 4 for a unit of wine; Borealis needs 6 for each. Atlantis is better at both (absolute advantage in both). But:

| | Cost of 1 wine | Cost of 1 cloth |
|---|---|---|
| Atlantis | 4 / 2 = **2 cloth** | 2 / 4 = **0.5 wine** |
| Borealis | 6 / 6 = **1 cloth** | 6 / 6 = **1 wine** |

Borealis gives up less cloth to make wine (1 against 2), so it has the **comparative advantage in wine**. Atlantis gives up less wine to make cloth (0.5 against 1), so it has the **comparative advantage in cloth**. A country always has a comparative advantage in something, because the two opportunity costs are reciprocals.

### The terms of trade

Trade benefits both only at a price **between the two opportunity costs**: here, between 1 and 2 cloth per wine. At 1.5 cloth per wine, Borealis gets more cloth for its wine than it could make itself (1), and Atlantis pays less cloth for wine than making it would cost (2).

## Worked example: the gains

Each country has 120 hours. Without trade, splitting the hours equally, Atlantis makes 30 cloth and 15 wine, and Borealis 10 cloth and 10 wine. World output: 40 cloth and 25 wine.

With specialisation, Borealis makes only wine (120 / 6 = 20 units), and Atlantis makes 50 cloth (100 hours) and 5 wine (20 hours). World output rises to **50 cloth and 25 wine**: the same wine plus 10 extra cloth.

They trade 10 wine for 15 cloth (1.5 cloth per wine).

| | Before trade | After trade | Gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atlantis | 30 cloth, 15 wine | 50 − 15 = 35 cloth, 5 + 10 = 15 wine | +5 cloth |
| Borealis | 10 cloth, 10 wine | 15 cloth, 20 − 10 = 10 wine | +5 cloth |

Both end up at points **beyond their own production frontiers**, which neither could reach alone.

## Who wins and loses

The country gains overall, but not everyone in it. Producers in the industry that shrinks (Atlantis's wine-makers) lose income and may need to retrain. Consumers and exporters gain. This is why trade policy is politically contentious even when the economics of the aggregate gains is clear.

Arguments for restricting trade (protecting infant industries, national security, retaliating against unfair practices) are about these distributional effects or special cases; they do not overturn the basic gains from specialisation.

## Exam traps

- **Comparative advantage is relative**; a country always has a comparative advantage in something.
- **Opportunity cost = hours for this good ÷ hours for the other good** (or output given up per unit gained).
- **The trade price must lie between the two opportunity costs** for both countries to gain.
- **Gains are not shared evenly within countries.**

## Questions people ask

### What is the difference between absolute and comparative advantage?

Absolute advantage means producing more with the same inputs. Comparative advantage means giving up less of other goods to produce something. Trade follows comparative advantage.

### Can a country gain from trade if it is worse at everything?

Yes. As long as its opportunity costs differ from its partner's, both can specialise in what they give up least to make, trade, and end up with more of both goods.

### Who loses from trade?

Workers and owners in industries that shrink when imports arrive. The country gains overall, but the losses are concentrated, which is why trade policy is contentious.
