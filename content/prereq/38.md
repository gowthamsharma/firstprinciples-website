> \[S - I = (G - T) + (X - M)\]
>
> Here S is private saving. Any gap between what the private sector saves and invests must be absorbed by the government or by the rest of the world.

## From first principles

Start from two ways of looking at GDP (Y).

**Spending**: \( Y = C + I + G + (X - M) \)

**Use of income**: households either consume it, save it or pay it in taxes, \( Y = C + S + T \)

Set them equal and cancel C:

\[ C + S + T = C + I + G + (X - M) \]

\[ S = I + (G - T) + (X - M) \]

That is an **identity**: it holds by definition, in every country, every year. It says private saving has three possible uses:

- funding domestic **investment** (I);
- funding a government **budget deficit** (G − T);
- lending to the rest of the world, which is what a **trade surplus** (X − M) means.

Rearranged as **sector balances**:

\[ \underbrace{(S - I)}_{\text{private}} + \underbrace{(T - G)}_{\text{government}} = \underbrace{(X - M)}_{\text{external}} \]

### National saving

**National saving** is private saving plus government saving, \(S + (T - G)\). The identity then becomes:

\[ \text{National saving} - I = X - M \]

A country that invests more than it saves must borrow the difference from abroad, and that borrowing shows up as a **trade (current account) deficit**.

### Twin deficits

Suppose the government's deficit widens. The identity says something else must change: private saving rises, investment falls (higher interest rates can **crowd out** private investment), or the trade deficit widens. When it is mostly the trade balance, the country runs **twin deficits**: a budget deficit and a current account deficit together.

## Worked example 1: finding the trade balance

In a country, as a share of GDP:

- private saving 22%, investment 20%: private balance **+2%**;
- tax revenue 35%, government spending 40%: government balance **−5%**.

\[ X - M = (S - I) + (T - G) = 2 + (-5) = -3\% \]

The country runs a **trade deficit of 3% of GDP**. The private sector's surplus is not enough to fund the government's deficit, so the rest is borrowed from abroad.

[[fig:sector_balances|The three sector balances always sum to zero: a government deficit not met by private saving becomes a trade deficit.]]

## Worked example 2: a fiscal tightening

The same government cuts its deficit from 5% to 2% of GDP. If private saving and investment do not change, the trade deficit shrinks from 3% to **0%** (+2 − 2 = 0).

In practice the private balance also moves. If the tightening slows the economy and firms invest less, the private surplus might grow to +3%: the trade balance then becomes a surplus of 1%. The identity always holds; the economics is in which balance adjusts.

## How analysts use it

- **Diagnosing a current account deficit.** Is it driven by an investment boom (often less worrying: it may build future capacity) or by a government deficit or a consumption binge (more worrying)?
- **Judging currency risk.** A country that needs constant foreign funding is exposed when global investors become cautious.
- **Testing forecasts.** A forecast that has the budget deficit widening, the trade deficit shrinking and private saving unchanged breaks the identity, so one of the numbers is wrong.

## Exam traps

- **It is an identity, not a theory.** It does not say which balance causes which.
- **Watch the sign of the government term**: G − T is the deficit; T − G is the government's saving.
- **A trade deficit means net borrowing from abroad**, not that the country is "losing".
- **National saving includes the government**; private saving does not.

## Questions people ask

### What does the saving–investment identity say?

Private saving minus investment, plus the government's balance, equals the trade balance. If a country spends more than it saves, the gap must be borrowed from abroad.

### What are twin deficits?

A government budget deficit alongside a current account deficit. The identity links them: unless private saving rises to fund the government, the extra borrowing comes from foreigners.

### Why does this matter for investors?

Countries with large current account deficits depend on foreign capital and are vulnerable when it pulls back, which often shows up as a falling currency and rising bond yields.
