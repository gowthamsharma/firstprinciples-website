Ask candidates why the CFA exams are hard and you rarely hear "the ideas are too difficult". You hear "there is so much of it", "I ran out of time" and "I was working sixty hours a week". The difficulty is real, but it comes from a few specific places, and each one can be planned for.


[[figure]]

## 1. Volume

Level I alone covers ten topic areas, from ethics and statistics to derivatives and portfolio management. Each level needs **300 hours or more** of study. The problem is not any single reading but keeping all of them fresh at once.

## 2. Depth grows at every level

Level I asks whether you know and can apply an idea. Level II puts several ideas into one case study and asks you to value something. Level III asks you to **judge and explain** a recommendation for a client, partly in writing. Each step needs a different skill, not just more knowledge.

## 3. A high pass line

The minimum passing score is set after each exam to reflect what a qualified candidate should know, and recent pass rates have been in the **low to high 40s percent**. There is no curve that rescues a weak session.

## 4. Life gets in the way

Most candidates work full time. Six months of evenings and weekends, with travel, deadlines and family, is where most plans break, not on the exam day itself.

## 5. Memorising instead of understanding

Formulas learned by rote fade within weeks, and the exams are good at changing one detail so that a memorised pattern fails. Understanding why a result is true makes it both easier to recall and harder to trick.

## The hardest topics

Candidates most often name **financial statement analysis** and **fixed income** at Level I, **valuation-heavy readings** at Level II, and **constructed-response answers** at Level III. Ethics is not hard to understand, but it is easy to lose marks on through careless reading.

## How to make it easier

- **Start early** and protect a weekly rhythm of study (see the [study plan](blog-study-plan.html)).
- **Learn from first principles** so ideas stay with you across all three levels.
- **Practise under time** with full mock exams and keep an error log.
- **Give ethics more time** than its size suggests.

## The numbers behind "hard"

- **Pass rates** have been below 50% at every level in most recent sessions (about 43% at Level I, 45% at Level II and 47% at Level III). See [pass rates](cfa-pass-rates.html).
- **Study time:** CFA Institute recommends about 300 hours of study per level, and Level II candidates often need more.
- **Attempts:** at average pass rates, a candidate needs about six to seven exam attempts to clear all three levels, and fewer than one in ten pass every level first time ([our simulation](blog-cfa-time-to-pass.html)).

For a topic-by-topic view, see [the most challenging CFA topics](blog-hardest-cfa-topics.html).


## Where candidates actually lose marks

Score reports, which show performance by topic, and candidates' own accounts suggest the same pattern at every level:

- **Running out of time.** The last 10–15 questions of a session are often guessed. Pacing practice in full mocks fixes most of this.
- **Misreading.** Words such as "least likely", "except" or "before tax" turn a known answer into a wrong one.
- **Weak heavyweight topics.** A poor result in financial statement analysis or fixed income at Level I, or in equity valuation at Level II, is hard to make up elsewhere.
- **Ethics left too late.** It looks like common sense and then is not, because the questions turn on precise rules.
- **At Level III, writing:** knowing the answer but not stating the decision and the reason the grader is looking for.

## What the difficulty means for planning

- **Treat 300 hours as a minimum, not a target.** Candidates without a finance or accounting background often need 350 to 400 hours at Level I.
- **Build in a buffer.** Plan for six months per level, not four, so that a busy month at work does not end the attempt.
- **Expect to repeat at least one level.** At average pass rates, most candidates fail at least once. Planning for that possibility makes it less of a shock. Our guide to [what to do after failing Level I](blog-failed-cfa-level-1.html) covers the retake rules.
- **Use the right tools:** a [study plan](blog-study-plan.html), the [formula sheets](blog-cfa-level-1-formula-sheet.html), [practice questions in the exam format](cfa-level-1-practice-questions.html) and full [mock exams](blog-mock-exams.html).

## Is it getting harder?

Not obviously. Pass rates fell after the move to computer-based testing in 2021, when windows became more frequent, and have since recovered to roughly 40–50% at most levels. The curriculum keeps changing: more quantitative methods and data science, less traditional economics and accounting. The new material is not harder, but older study notes and question banks become out of date, so use materials for your exam year.

## Questions people ask

### Is the CFA harder than the CPA?

They are hard in different ways. The CFA covers broader investment content across three long exams spread over years; the CPA is more accounting-focused and has more sections taken closer together. Because the CPA reports pass rates section by section, the two are hard to compare directly; choose by career, not by difficulty.

### Which CFA level is the hardest?

Many candidates say Level II, for the depth of valuation and accounting, although pass rates are similar across the levels and Level III's written answers are a different kind of challenge.

### Can an average student pass the CFA?

Yes. Steady study, enough practice and an understanding-first approach matter far more than raw talent.
