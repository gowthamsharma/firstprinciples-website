At Level II every question belongs to a case study (a **vignette**) with four questions. You have about **12 minutes per set**. The skill is not reading faster; it is reading **with a purpose**.

[[figure]]

## The routine

1. **Questions first.** Skim the four questions (stems only, not the options). Now you know which numbers and facts matter.
2. **Read the vignette once, with purpose.** Mark the inputs the questions need: the growth rate, the tax rate, the date of the change in policy. Expect distractors: data that is true but irrelevant.
3. **Answer in order, but not at any cost.** If a question needs a long calculation, do the quick ones in the set first.
4. **Check the clock at each set.** After 11 sets you should be at about 2 hours 12 minutes. If a set has taken 18 minutes, the next one gets 9.

## Why one mistake costs more

The questions in a set are linked. Misread one assumption ("the analyst uses the *average* tax rate") and the error can carry through all four answers. Reread the sentence that defines any input before you use it in a calculation.

## What to study differently from Level I

- **Revisit Level I foundations.** Level II valuation assumes Level I accounting and fixed income. If inventories or deferred taxes are hazy, fix them first.
- **Learn models end to end.** For example: from forecasting free cash flow, to choosing the discount rate, to the terminal value. Questions will test any link in that chain.
- **Practise with vignettes, not single questions,** from the practice block onwards.
- **Do not skip a topic.** Weights are 5–15% each, and one set can be a full 12 minutes of a single topic.

## The timing in numbers

Level II gives you **4 hours 24 minutes for 88 questions**: exactly **3 minutes per question, or 12 minutes per item set**. A practical rule: if a set has taken 15 minutes and you still have a question left, make your best choice, flag it and move on. Twelve minutes lost on one set costs four easier questions elsewhere.

## Which topics dominate

The heaviest Level II topics (each 10–15%) are **financial statement analysis, equity valuation, fixed income, portfolio management and ethics**. Financial statements and equity are also the most connected: an accounting adjustment changes the cash flows you value. Study them close together.

## A practice plan

1. **Weeks 1–14:** learn each topic, with end-of-chapter questions the same week.
2. **From week 8:** one full item set a day on topics already covered.
3. **Weeks 15–20:** mixed item sets under time, three or four a session.
4. **Last six weeks:** full mocks, with careful review of every set you lost more than one question on.


## A worked example: reading a vignette with purpose

Here is a short original vignette in the Level II style, with the way a well-prepared candidate would annotate it.

> Priya Nair is valuing Harbourview Logistics, a European freight company, using a free cash flow to equity (FCFE) model. For the year just ended, Harbourview reported net income of €120 million, depreciation of €40 million, fixed capital investment of €70 million and an increase in working capital of €10 million. It borrowed a net €15 million. The corporate tax rate is 25%. Harbourview has 50 million shares outstanding. Nair estimates a required return on equity of 10% and expects FCFE to grow at 4% a year indefinitely. Her supervisor suggests that, because Harbourview's borrowing varies a lot from year to year, Nair should instead assume the company finances 30% of its net new investment with debt, its long-run target.

Before reading, skim the four questions. Suppose they ask for (1) last year's FCFE, (2) the value per share, (3) FCFE under the supervisor's suggestion, and (4) which input is not needed. As you read, mark these inputs:

| Input | Value | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| Net income | €120m | Q1, Q3 |
| Depreciation | €40m | Q1, Q3 |
| Fixed capital investment | €70m | Q1, Q3 |
| Working capital investment | €10m | Q1, Q3 |
| Net borrowing | €15m | Q1 only |
| Target debt ratio | 30% | Q3 |
| Required return, growth | 10%, 4% | Q2 |
| Tax rate | 25% | not needed: FCFE starts from net income, which is already after tax |

The answers follow quickly once the inputs are marked:

1. FCFE = 120 + 40 − 70 − 10 + 15 = **€95 million**.
2. Value \(= \dfrac{FCFE_1}{r - g} = \dfrac{95 \times 1.04}{0.06}\) = €1,646.7 million, or **€32.93 per share**.
3. With a 30% target debt ratio: \(FCFE = NI - (1 - DR)(FCInv - Dep) - (1 - DR)\,WCInv = 120 - 0.7 \times 30 - 0.7 \times 10\) = **€92 million**.
4. The **tax rate** is a distractor. It would matter for FCFF (interest × (1 − t)) but not here.

Two lessons from the example. First, a single misread input (using FCFF instead of FCFE, or forgetting to grow FCFE by one year) would have spoiled three of the four answers. Second, the vignette deliberately includes data you do not need; spotting it saves time and avoids traps.

## The traps vignettes set most often

- **Timing words.** "Just paid" versus "next year", "beginning of the year" versus "end of the year". They decide whether you need D₀ or D₁, or an ordinary annuity or an annuity due.
- **Changes buried in the text.** A switch in accounting method, a new tax rate from next year, or a lease reclassified halfway through the case.
- **The analyst's opinion versus fact.** Statements such as "Nair believes the company is overvalued" are often there to test whether you can tell an assumption from a conclusion.
- **Units and scales.** Thousands versus millions, percentages versus decimals, annual versus semi-annual rates.
- **Questions that ask which statement is *least* likely correct.** Under time pressure, many candidates answer the opposite question. Underline "least".

## Questions people ask

### What is a Level II item set?

A short case study, usually one to two pages, followed by four multiple-choice questions on it. The exam is built entirely from these sets, so reading efficiently is as important as knowing the material.

### Should I read the questions before the vignette?

Many candidates do. Knowing what is asked tells you which numbers and facts to mark as you read, and stops you re-reading the case for every question.

### Why do people find Level II the hardest?

The material is deeper, especially valuation and financial reporting, and each case can mix several ideas. Pass rates are similar to the other levels, but most candidates say it took them the most hours.

### How should I practise for Level II?

Practise whole item sets under time, not single questions. Aim for about 12 minutes a set (264 minutes for 22 sets), and review why each wrong option was tempting.


### How long should one item set take?

About 12 minutes, three per question. Long calculation sets may take 15; make up the time on shorter conceptual sets such as ethics.

### Are all four questions in a set on the same topic?

Usually, yes. Each set focuses on one topic area, though a case may use tools from others, for example financial statement adjustments in an equity valuation set.
