Comprehensive income and OCI

By Gowtham KumarLesson 9 of 50

Some gains and losses skip the income statement and go straight to equity. Total comprehensive income captures every change in equity not caused by owners, and some of it later comes back through profit.

Comprehensive income=Net income+OCI\text{Comprehensive income} = \text{Net income} + \text{OCI}Comprehensive income=Net income+OCI

It equals all non-owner changes in equity.

From first principles

Equity changes for only two reasons: transactions with owners (issuing shares, paying dividends, buying back shares) and everything else. "Everything else" is comprehensive income.

Most of it passes through the income statement as net income. But standard setters decided some gains and losses are too volatile, or not yet realised, to belong in reported profit. They go to other comprehensive income (OCI) and build up in a reserve inside equity, accumulated OCI. Common examples:

  • foreign currency translation adjustments on foreign subsidiaries;
  • pension remeasurements (actuarial gains and losses);
  • the effective part of cash flow hedges of future transactions;
  • fair value changes on certain debt investments held at fair value through OCI, and (IFRS only) on equity investments a company chooses to hold that way;
  • revaluation surpluses on property, plant and equipment (IFRS only).

So the full roll-forward of equity is:

Closing equity=Opening+Net income+OCI+Shares issued−Buybacks−Dividends\text{Closing equity} = \text{Opening} + \text{Net income} + \text{OCI} + \text{Shares issued} - \text{Buybacks} - \text{Dividends}Closing equity=Opening+Net income+OCI+Shares issued−Buybacks−Dividends

Recycling

Some OCI items come back later. When the reason for parking them ends, they are moved (recycled, or reclassified) out of accumulated OCI and into profit. A gain on a hedge of a forecast sale waits in OCI and is recycled when the sale itself is booked; the cumulative translation adjustment is recycled when the foreign subsidiary is sold. Other items are never recycled: under IFRS, revaluation surpluses and pension remeasurements stay in equity for good.

Worked example 1: total comprehensive income

Net income is 500. The euro strengthened against the parent's currency, adding 40 to the value of a euro subsidiary; a pension remeasurement cost 15.

OCI=40−15=25Total comprehensive income=500+25=525\text{OCI} = 40 - 15 = 25 \qquad \text{Total comprehensive income} = 500 + 25 = 525OCI=40−15=25Total comprehensive income=500+25=525

Dividends of 200 reduce equity too, but they are a transaction with owners, so they are not part of comprehensive income.

Worked example 2: rolling equity forward

Opening equity 3,000. Net income 400, OCI −50, dividends 120, a share buyback of 80.

3,000+400−50−120−80=3,1503{,}000 + 400 - 50 - 120 - 80 = 3{,}1503,000+400−50−120−80=3,150

Leave out the OCI loss and you would expect 3,200; the balance sheet would not reconcile. An analyst who only reads the income statement misses that 50.

Why analysts care

Two companies can report the same net income while one carries large losses in OCI. Return on equity built on net income flatters the second. Comparing comprehensive income shows the full change in owners' wealth from everything except dealings with owners. Large, persistent OCI losses (on pensions or on currencies) are economic costs even if they never touch profit.

How the exam tests it

  • Calculating comprehensive income from a list of items, separating owner transactions.
  • Saying whether an item goes to profit or OCI.
  • Recycling: which items come back through profit and when.

Exam traps

  • Dividends and share issues are never in comprehensive income.
  • A gain on selling equipment is profit, not OCI.
  • Some OCI items are later recycled into profit; others never are.
  • Return on equity comparisons can be distorted when one firm's losses sit in OCI.

Questions people ask

What goes into other comprehensive income?

Gains and losses that standard setters keep out of profit for now: currency translation of foreign subsidiaries, certain pension remeasurements, some fair value changes on investments and the effective part of hedges of future cash flows.

What does recycling mean?

Moving a gain or loss that sat in OCI into profit when it is finally realised, for example when the hedged transaction happens. Under IFRS some OCI items are recycled and some never are.

Why should analysts look at comprehensive income?

Because large losses can sit in OCI without ever touching net income. Comparing companies on comprehensive income shows the full change in owners' wealth from things other than dealings with owners.
