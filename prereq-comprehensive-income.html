Comprehensive income and OCI

Some gains and losses skip the income statement and go straight to equity. Total comprehensive income captures every change in equity not caused by owners.

Concept 9 of 50 · Financial Statement Analysis · Income statements · 2 min read

Comprehensive income = net income + other comprehensive income = all non-owner changes in equity.

From first principles

Equity changes for two reasons: transactions with owners (issuing shares, dividends, buybacks) and everything else. "Everything else" is comprehensive income.

Most of it passes through the income statement as net income. But standard-setters decided some gains and losses are too volatile, or not yet realised, to belong in reported profit. They go to other comprehensive income (OCI) and accumulate in a reserve within equity. Common examples:

  • foreign currency translation adjustments on foreign subsidiaries;
  • some pension remeasurements;
  • the effective part of cash flow hedges;
  • fair value changes on certain debt investments held at fair value through OCI;
  • revaluation surpluses on PP&E under IFRS.

Worked example

Net income 500. The euro strengthened against the parent's currency, adding 40 to the value of a euro subsidiary; a pension remeasurement cost 15. OCI = 40 − 15 = 25. Total comprehensive income = 525. Equity also falls by dividends of 200, which are not part of comprehensive income because they are a transaction with owners.

Exam traps

  • Dividends and share issues are never in comprehensive income.
  • Some OCI items are later recycled into profit when realised (for example, cash flow hedges); others never are.
  • Comparing return on equity across firms can be distorted if one firm's losses sit in OCI.

Why it matters at Level III

Large OCI swings, especially currency translation, reveal exposures that net income hides. They matter for risk analysis of multinational holdings.
