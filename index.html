What is a first-principles approach?

Most exam prep hands you conclusions to memorise. A first-principles approach goes the other way: it starts from a few facts that cannot be otherwise and reasons forward until the conclusion is unavoidable. You remember it because you can rebuild it.

1

Start from what must be true

Strip the topic down to its bedrock: money today is worth more than money later; a balance sheet always balances; buyers buy less when prices rise.

2

Build one step at a time

Add one idea at a time, with a picture at every step, and check that each step follows from the last. Nothing is introduced just because the syllabus says so.

3

Name the formula last

When the formula finally appears, it only writes down what you can already see. Then you test yourself by predicting the answer before working it out.

An example: why bond prices fall when rates rise

Memorise: "price and yield are inversely related."

Reason: a bond is a fixed set of future payments. Its price today is what those payments are worth today. A higher interest rate means each future payment is worth less today, so the total, the price, must fall. And the further away a payment is, the more it shrinks. That last sentence is all duration really is.

Prerequisite concept

How the three financial statements connect

The income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement are three views of one set of transactions. Change one, and the others must move.

 Prerequisite concept

Price elasticity of demand and total revenue

Elasticity measures how strongly buyers react to a price change. It decides whether a price rise raises or lowers a seller's revenue.

 Prerequisite concept

Deferred tax: tax base and temporary differences

Companies keep two sets of rules, one for the accounts and one for the tax authority. Deferred tax records the tax effect of differences that will reverse.
