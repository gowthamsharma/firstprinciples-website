Answering CFA Level III constructed-response questions

By Gowtham KumarStudy blog

Graders reward short, specific answers that do exactly what the command word asks. How to read the question, how much to write, and how to get partial credit.

Half of Level III is constructed response: you type answers into a box. CFA Institute's graders are consistent about what earns points: correct, specific content that does what the question asks. Style, grammar and length earn nothing.

Obey the command word

The bolded verb tells you what a full answer looks like. CFA Institute singles out ignoring it as one of the most common mistakes, one that can cost an otherwise correct answer all its points.

  • List / identify: name it. A few words are enough.
  • Describe / explain: what it is, then why. One or two sentences.
  • Compare: similarities and differences. Contrast: only differences.
  • Justify / recommend: your choice, plus the reason taken from the case. A recommendation without a justification usually scores nothing.
  • Calculate: formula, inputs, answer. Show the steps for partial credit.

Give exactly what is asked

If the question asks for two reasons, give two. Graders do not choose the best two from a list of five; extra points can even suggest you are unsure. Mirror the question's structure: if it has parts i and ii, answer in parts i and ii.

Short beats long

CFA Institute's graders say a few short sentences usually earn full credit. Bullet points are fine. Use standard abbreviations (PV, IPS, ↑ and ↓). A good shape for most answers is:

Answer + because + case fact. For example: "Recommend Portfolio B, because the client's 5-year horizon allows more equity risk."

Never leave a box empty

There is no penalty for a wrong answer. Write your best attempt, even a formula you think is the right one, and move on. Budget by points: roughly one minute per point.

Practise with the rubric

From 2026 the official Level III mock exams include scoring rubrics. Mark your own answers strictly: would a grader find the exact point the rubric lists? Then rewrite the answer in half the words. That habit matters more than any template.

A worked example

Question: "Justify whether a 15% allocation to private equity is appropriate for the Lee family, who need 40% of the portfolio for a house purchase in two years."

Weak answer (long, vague): "Private equity can offer higher returns and diversification, but it also has risks such as illiquidity and high fees, so investors need to think carefully about whether it suits them…"

Strong answer (short, specific): "Not appropriate. The family has a large liquidity need in two years (40% of assets), and private equity is illiquid with multi-year lock-ups. A 15% allocation could force sales of other assets at a bad time."

The strong answer states the decision, gives the client-specific reason, and links it to a property of the asset. That is what graders look for.

Command words and what they want

Command word What to write
Calculate The number, with the key step shown
Identify / state A short answer, no explanation needed
Describe The characteristics, in a sentence or two
Explain / justify Your answer and the reason, tied to the case
Recommend A clear choice, then the justification
Discuss Both sides, then a conclusion

How the points work, and how to pace them

Each constructed-response set is worth 12 points, and each 132-minute session contains 11 sets. That is roughly one minute per point. Use the points shown next to each question as a guide:

  • 1–2 points: one decision or one fact, plus a short reason.
  • 3–4 points: usually two or three separate elements (for example, "identify two risks and explain how each affects the client").
  • 5–6 points: a calculation with several steps, or a recommendation with several justifications.

If a 2-point question has taken five minutes, write your best answer and move on. Points are equal across sets, so an unanswered set at the end of a session is the costliest mistake of all.

How graders mark your answer

Graders work from a guideline listing the points that earn credit. Their approach is practical:

  • They look for the required elements, not for style, grammar or full sentences.
  • They do not give credit for things you were not asked for. Writing four reasons when the question asks for two does not earn extra points, and it uses up time.
  • A wrong decision usually loses the justification as well. If you recommend the wrong portfolio, a well-argued reason for it rarely scores. Get the decision right first.
  • Show calculations. Write the formula and inputs, then the result. A slip in the arithmetic can still earn partial credit if the method is visible.

The most common ways to lose points

  1. Answering a different question. "Discuss" and "justify" are not the same as "describe". Re-read the command word before you write.
  2. Generic answers. "The client has a long time horizon" scores only if the case supports it and the question asks about horizon. Use the facts of the case: name, numbers, constraints.
  3. Hedging between two answers. "Portfolio A or B could both work" earns nothing. Choose one.
  4. Calculating without the final step. If the question asks whether the client can meet a goal, a correct return figure without "yes, because 6.1% exceeds the 5.5% required" may miss a point.
  5. Leaving templates incomplete. Where a template asks you to circle or select an option and then justify, do both.

A weekly writing routine

  • Two writing sessions a week from week four, each with two or three past constructed-response questions from CFA Institute's practice materials.
  • Mark your answers against the guideline the same day. Note which points you missed and why.
  • Keep an "answer bank" of phrases that worked: for example, the standard way to justify a lower risk tolerance or a currency hedge.
  • In the last month, write under exam conditions: typed answers, 132-minute blocks, no notes.

Questions people ask

How are Level III written answers marked?

Graders look for specific points that answer the command word. Extra writing earns nothing, and a correct idea buried in a long paragraph can be missed. Short, direct sentences score best.

Do I need full sentences in constructed response?

No. Clear bullet points or short phrases are fine as long as the answer is complete and directly addresses the question. Writing the conclusion first helps.

What does 'justify' mean in a Level III question?

Give your choice and the reason from the case that supports it. A justification without a reason from the facts given usually earns little or nothing.

How much time should each written set take?

Roughly the minutes shown by the points: about one minute per point. Writing more than needed on early questions is the most common reason candidates run out of time.

Can I use bullet points in Level III answers?

Yes. Bullet points and short phrases are fine, and they are faster. What matters is that each point answers the question and uses the facts of the case.

Is there partial credit in Level III?

Yes. Each question lists several gradable elements, and you earn points for each correct element, including the method in a calculation even if the final number is wrong.
