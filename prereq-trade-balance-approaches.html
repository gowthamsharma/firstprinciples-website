Exchange rates and the trade balance: elasticities and absorption

By Gowtham KumarLesson 50 of 50

A weaker currency improves the trade balance only if trade volumes respond enough (elasticities), and only if the country cuts spending relative to income (absorption).

X−M=Y−(C+I+G)X - M = Y - (C + I + G)X−M=Y−(C+I+G)

A depreciation improves the trade balance only if volumes respond enough, and only if domestic spending falls relative to income.

From first principles

A depreciation makes exports cheaper for foreigners and imports dearer at home. Two views explain whether the trade balance actually improves.

The elasticities approach

It depends on how strongly volumes respond to the new prices. After a depreciation:

  • import prices in domestic currency rise at once; import volumes fall only slowly;
  • exports become cheaper abroad, and export volumes rise, again slowly.

If demand is inelastic, the country pays more for roughly the same imports, and the balance worsens. The Marshall–Lerner condition says the trade balance improves when export and import demand elasticities are large enough. Starting from balanced trade:

εX+εM>1\varepsilon_X + \varepsilon_M > 1εX​+εM​>1

(with elasticities in absolute value). When trade starts out unbalanced, the condition weights each elasticity by the size of exports and imports, and the import elasticity matters more for a country with a large deficit.

The J-curve

Because prices move before volumes, the balance often first worsens, then improves: the J-curve. Contracts are set in advance, buyers take time to find new suppliers, and exporters take time to win orders.

The absorption approach

From the national accounts, the trade balance equals national income minus domestic spending (absorption, A=C+I+GA = C + I + GA=C+I+G):

X−M=Y−AX - M = Y - AX−M=Y−A

A depreciation improves the trade balance only if income rises relative to spending.

  • With spare capacity, a depreciation can raise output (Y) as exporters and import-competitors hire idle workers, so the balance improves.
  • At full employment, income cannot rise much, so spending must fall, for example through tighter fiscal policy. Otherwise the depreciation mainly raises prices, and the competitive gain is eroded.

Worked example 1: Marshall–Lerner

A country with balanced trade has export demand elasticity 0.6 and import demand elasticity 0.5.

0.6+0.5=1.1>10.6 + 0.5 = 1.1 > 10.6+0.5=1.1>1

A depreciation improves the trade balance (eventually). If the elasticities were 0.3 and 0.4 (sum 0.7), the depreciation would worsen the balance even in the long run, because higher import prices would outweigh the small volume changes.

Worked example 2: absorption at full capacity

National income is 100 and absorption is 104: a trade deficit of 4. The currency falls 10%. The economy is already at full employment, so income stays at 100. If households and the government keep spending 104, the deficit remains 4; prices simply rise. Only if absorption is cut to 100 does the trade balance close.

Worked example 3: the J-curve in numbers

A country's exports of 100 are priced in its own currency and its imports of 100 are priced in foreign currency, so trade is balanced. The currency falls 10%. In the first quarter, volumes do not change: exports still bring in 100, but the same imports now cost 110 in domestic currency. The balance moves from 0 to −10. Over the following year, cheaper exports win orders and dearer imports lose buyers; with elasticities summing above 1, the balance ends up better than where it started. Plotted over time, that path is the J.

Exam traps

  • Marshall–Lerner is about elasticities; the absorption approach is about income and spending. Both are needed for a full answer.
  • The J-curve is a timing effect, not a failure of depreciation.
  • The simple condition (sum above 1) assumes trade starts balanced.
  • At full employment, a depreciation needs lower spending to improve the trade balance.

Questions people ask

Does a weaker currency always improve the trade balance?

No. It helps only if export and import volumes respond enough to price. The Marshall–Lerner condition sets the threshold for how responsive they must be.

What is the J-curve?

The pattern where the trade balance first worsens after a depreciation, because import prices rise at once while volumes adjust slowly, and only later improves.

What does the absorption approach add?

A spending view: the trade balance improves only if national income rises relative to domestic spending. With no spare capacity, a depreciation mainly raises prices unless spending is cut.
