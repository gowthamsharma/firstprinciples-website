Exchange rates and the trade balance: elasticities and absorption

A weaker currency improves the trade balance only if trade volumes respond enough (elasticities), and only if the country cuts spending relative to income (absorption).

2 min read  /  By Gowtham Kumar

Marshall–Lerner: a depreciation improves the trade balance if export and import demand are elastic enough.

From first principles

A depreciation makes exports cheaper for foreigners and imports dearer at home. Two views explain whether the trade balance actually improves.

Elasticities approach. It depends on how strongly volumes respond. Import prices rise immediately; volumes adjust slowly. If demand is inelastic, the country pays more for roughly the same imports and the balance worsens. The Marshall–Lerner condition says the trade balance improves if the export and import demand elasticities are large enough. Starting from balanced trade, that means their sum must exceed 1. Because volumes respond with a lag, the balance often first worsens, then improves: the J-curve.

Absorption approach. From national accounts, the trade balance = national income − domestic spending (absorption). A depreciation improves the trade balance only if income rises relative to spending. If the economy is at full employment, income cannot rise much, so spending must fall, for example through tighter fiscal policy. Otherwise the depreciation mainly raises inflation.

Worked example

A country's currency falls 10%. In the first months, import prices rise and volumes barely change, so the deficit widens (the bottom of the J). Over a year, exporters win orders and households switch to domestic goods; if the elasticities satisfy Marshall–Lerner, the deficit shrinks. If the economy is already at capacity and the government keeps spending, absorption does not fall and the improvement is small.

Exam traps

  • Marshall–Lerner is about elasticities; the absorption approach is about income and spending. Both are needed for a full answer.
  • The J-curve is a timing effect, not a failure of depreciation.

Why it matters at Level III

Currency forecasts in capital market expectations combine these trade effects with capital flows. In the short run, capital flows usually dominate.
