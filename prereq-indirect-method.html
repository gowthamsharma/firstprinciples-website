The indirect method: from net income to operating cash

Most companies report operating cash flow by starting from net income and undoing everything that was not cash. Each adjustment has a simple logic.

2 min read  /  By Gowtham Kumar

CFO = net income + non-cash expenses − non-cash gains − increases in operating assets + increases in operating liabilities.

From first principles

Net income includes items that are not cash, and misses cash that is not income. To get from one to the other:

  1. Add back non-cash expenses (depreciation, amortisation, impairments): they reduced profit but no cash left.
  2. Remove gains, add back losses on selling assets: the cash from the sale belongs in investing, so its gain must come out of operating.
  3. Adjust for working capital. If receivables rose, some revenue was not collected: subtract the increase. If inventory rose, cash went into the shelves: subtract. If payables rose, you did not pay some bills yet: add.

The rule of thumb: an increase in an operating asset uses cash (subtract); an increase in an operating liability provides cash (add).

Worked example

Net income 100, depreciation 40, gain on sale of equipment 10, receivables up 25, inventory up 15, payables up 20. CFO = 100 + 40 − 10 − 25 − 15 + 20 = 110.

The direct method reaches the same CFO by listing cash receipts and payments; IFRS encourages it but most firms use the indirect method.

Exam traps

  • Gains are subtracted even though they increased profit.
  • The sign of the working-capital adjustment is the most common error: rising receivables reduce CFO.
  • Amortisation of a bond discount is a non-cash expense: add it back.

Why it matters at Level III

A firm whose net income grows while CFO does not is building receivables or inventory. That is a red flag for earnings quality.
