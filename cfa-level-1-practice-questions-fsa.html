CFA Level I practice questions: Financial statement analysis, with worked answers

By Gowtham KumarPractice questions

Ten original financial statement analysis questions written like the real Level I exam: short cases with data, three options each, and worked explanations. The three statements, inventories, long-lived assets, taxes, debt and ratio analysis. Financial statement analysis carries 11–14% of the exam from 2027.

Financial statement analysis is one of the four heavyweights at Level I. Questions test whether you can follow a transaction through the three statements, compare IFRS with US GAAP, and compute ratios without mixing up averages and year-end figures.

Topic weight from 2027: 11–14% of Level I, about 20 to 25 of the 180 questions.

10 questions · Level I format · about 15 to 20 minutes

Choose an answer to see the explanation.

  1. Alba Components, which reports under US GAAP, started the year with 100 units of inventory costing $10 each. During the year it bought 200 units at $12 and then 100 units at $13, and it sold 250 units. Prices rose steadily through the year.

    If Alba uses the LIFO method, its cost of goods sold for the year is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    LIFO assumes the most recent purchases are sold first: 100 units at $13 + 150 units at $12 = $1,300 + $1,800 = $3,100.

    $2,800 is FIFO (100 at $10 + 150 at $12). $2,937.50 is weighted average: total cost $4,700 / 400 units = $11.75 × 250. With rising prices, LIFO gives the highest cost of goods sold and the lowest profit and taxes. That is one reason IFRS does not allow LIFO.

  2. Mosaic Print buys a press for €50,000. It expects to use the press for five years and to sell it for €5,000 at the end. The company uses the double-declining-balance method, switching to straight-line only if that would give a higher charge.

    Depreciation expense in the second year is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    The double-declining rate is 2 / 5 = 40%, applied to the opening book value; salvage value is ignored until book value approaches it. Year 1: 40% × 50,000 = 20,000, leaving a book value of 30,000. Year 2: 40% × 30,000 = 12,000.

    €9,000 is straight-line depreciation: (50,000 − 5,000) / 5. €10,800 wrongly applies 40% to the depreciable base net of salvage after Year 1: 40% × (45,000 − 18,000).

  3. For the year just ended, Harbour Foods reports the following (in $ millions):

    Net income120
    Depreciation30
    Gain on sale of equipment5
    Increase in accounts receivable15
    Decrease in inventory10
    Decrease in accounts payable8

    Using the indirect method, Harbour's cash flow from operating activities is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Start with net income and adjust: + depreciation (a non-cash expense) 30 − gain on sale 5 (the cash proceeds belong in investing activities) − increase in receivables 15 + decrease in inventory 10 − decrease in payables 8. CFO = 120 + 30 − 5 − 15 + 10 − 8 = 132.

    $137 million forgets to subtract the gain. $148 million adds the decrease in payables. Paying down suppliers uses cash, so it is a deduction.

  4. Kirana Retail prepares its financial statements under IFRS. During the year it paid interest of ₹40 million on its bank loans, paid dividends of ₹60 million to shareholders, and received dividends of ₹15 million from an investment.

    Which of these classifications is permitted under IFRS but NOT under US GAAP?

    Answer: B.

    IFRS lets companies classify interest paid as either operating or financing. US GAAP requires interest paid to be an operating cash flow.

    Dividends paid are financing cash flows under US GAAP and may be financing (or operating) under IFRS, so classifying them as financing is allowed under both. Dividends received are operating under US GAAP and may be operating or investing under IFRS, so operating is also allowed under both.

  5. An analyst gathers the following DuPont data for Lumen Optics:

    RatioYear 1Year 2
    Net profit margin6.0%6.0%
    Total asset turnover1.251.50
    Financial leverage (average assets / average equity)2.002.20

    Lumen's return on equity in Year 2 is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    DuPont: ROE = net profit margin × asset turnover × financial leverage = 6.0% × 1.50 × 2.20 = 19.8%, up from 6.0% × 1.25 × 2.00 = 15.0% in Year 1. Margin was unchanged, so the improvement came from using assets more efficiently and from higher leverage, which also adds risk.

    9.0% is the return on assets (margin × turnover). 13.2% leaves out asset turnover (margin × leverage).

  6. At the start of the year Vela Logistics buys a vehicle fleet for $90,000. For accounting purposes it depreciates the fleet straight-line over five years with no salvage value. Tax rules allow straight-line depreciation over three years. The tax rate is 25% and no other temporary differences exist.

    At the end of the first year, Vela's balance sheet will most likely show:

    Answer: C.

    Year 1 depreciation: $18,000 in the accounts and $30,000 for tax. The carrying amount ($72,000) is higher than the tax base ($60,000), so there is a taxable temporary difference of $12,000. Deferred tax liability = 25% × 12,000 = $3,000. Vela has paid less tax than its reported profit implies and will pay more later, when the differences reverse.

    A deferred tax asset would arise if the tax base exceeded the carrying amount. $12,000 is the temporary difference before the tax rate is applied.

  7. On 1 January, Sakura Trading signs a five-year lease for a warehouse and reports under IFRS 16. It pays ¥10,000 (thousand) at the end of each year. The interest rate implicit in the lease is 6%. Sakura recognises a lease liability equal to the present value of the payments, which is ¥42,124.

    The carrying amount of Sakura's lease liability at the end of the first year is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Interest for Year 1 = 6% × 42,124 = 2,527. The ¥10,000 payment covers that interest and repays 7,473 of principal. Closing liability = 42,124 + 2,527 − 10,000 = 34,651.

    ¥32,124 subtracts the whole payment without adding interest. ¥42,124 is the opening balance. Separately, the right-of-use asset is depreciated, typically by 42,124 / 5 = 8,425 a year.

  8. Andes Power is building a new hydroelectric plant financed with a construction loan. In the current year it capitalises $12 million of interest on the loan into the cost of the plant, as accounting standards require, instead of expensing it.

    Compared with expensing the interest, capitalising it most likely results, for the current year, in:

    Answer: A.

    Capitalised interest is added to the cost of the asset, so it is not an expense this year: net income and the interest coverage ratio (EBIT / interest expense) are higher. On the cash flow statement, the interest paid is reported in investing activities as part of the plant's cost, so operating cash flow is higher and investing cash flow is lower (more negative).

    Total assets are higher, not lower. Analysts often move capitalised interest back into interest expense, and into operating cash flow, to compare companies fairly.

  9. Cedar Furniture, which reports under IFRS, holds 1,000 armchairs in inventory at a cost of €500 each. At the year end, it expects to sell them for €520 each, but it must first spend €40 per chair on finishing and delivery.

    The inventory write-down Cedar should recognise is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Inventory is measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV). NRV = estimated selling price − costs to complete and sell = 520 − 40 = €480 per chair. Cost (€500) exceeds NRV by €20, so the write-down is 1,000 × 20 = €20,000, recognised as an expense.

    Comparing cost only with the selling price ignores the costs still to be incurred. €40,000 is the total of the completion costs, not the write-down. Under IFRS, the write-down can be reversed if NRV later recovers.

  10. For the year, Orion Software reports net income of $1,200,000. It has 500,000 ordinary shares outstanding all year and 50,000 convertible preferred shares that pay a total annual dividend of $150,000. Each preferred share converts into two ordinary shares.

    Orion's diluted earnings per share is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Basic EPS = (net income − preferred dividends) / ordinary shares = (1,200,000 − 150,000) / 500,000 = $2.10.

    Diluted EPS assumes conversion: the preferred dividends are no longer paid (add them back) and 100,000 extra ordinary shares are issued. Diluted EPS = 1,200,000 / 600,000 = $2.00. Because this is below basic EPS, the conversion is dilutive and is included.

    $1.75 divides 1,050,000 by 600,000, adding the shares without adding back the dividends.

Three things to remember for financial statement analysis

  • Ask what each choice does to the income statement, the balance sheet and cash flow, in that order.
  • Under rising prices, LIFO lowers profit, inventory and taxes; FIFO does the reverse.
  • Memorise the IFRS and US GAAP differences in cash flow classification.

How to use these questions

Answer each question before you look at the explanation, and time yourself: the real exam allows about 90 seconds per question. Mark every question you got right for the wrong reason as wrong. When you miss one, write down the idea behind it rather than the answer, and come back to the page in a week.

These questions are written by First Principles to match the style and level of the exam. They are not taken from CFA Institute's curriculum or mock exams, which you should also use: they are included with your exam registration.

Questions people ask

Are these real CFA exam questions?

No. CFA Institute does not publish past exam questions. These are original questions written to match the Level I format: a short case, often with a table of data, followed by three answer options. Each explanation shows the working and why the two wrong options are wrong.

How many financial statement analysis questions are on Level I?

Financial statement analysis carries 11–14% of the exam from 2027, which is roughly 20 to 25 of the 180 questions.

Where can I find more Level I practice questions?

Use the practice questions and mock exams in CFA Institute's learning ecosystem first; they come with registration. Then use one third-party question bank, and redo every question you missed.
