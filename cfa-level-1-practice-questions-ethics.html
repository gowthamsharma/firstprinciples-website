CFA Level I practice questions: Ethical and professional standards, with worked answers

By Gowtham KumarPractice questions

Ten original ethics questions written like the real Level I exam: short cases with data, three options each, and worked explanations. The Code of Ethics and the Standards of Professional Conduct, applied to cases. Ethical and professional standards carries 10–15% of the exam from 2027.

Ethics is the topic candidates most often leave too late. The questions are short cases: someone does something, and you decide which Standard applies and whether it was broken. The trick is to read for the duty involved (to clients, to employers, to the market) before you look at the answers.

Topic weight from 2027: 10–15% of Level I, about 18 to 27 of the 180 questions.

10 questions · Level I format · about 15 to 20 minutes

Choose an answer to see the explanation.

  1. Lena Vogt, CFA, covers European chemical companies for a research boutique. At a dinner party she sits next to the executive assistant to the chief financial officer of Rheinchem AG, one of the companies she covers. The assistant mentions, without being asked, that the board was told that afternoon that third-quarter earnings will fall well short of the guidance the company published in July. The information has not been announced. The next morning Vogt downgrades Rheinchem from Buy to Sell, and several of her firm's clients sell their shares before the company releases the figures a week later. Vogt herself owns no Rheinchem shares.

    Vogt's actions most likely:

    Answer: A.

    Standard II(A), Material Nonpublic Information, prohibits acting on, or causing others to act on, information that is both material and nonpublic. An earnings shortfall against published guidance is clearly material, and it had not been disclosed. Changing a published recommendation causes clients to act on it, so it does not matter that Vogt did not trade herself.

    The mosaic theory protects conclusions drawn from public information combined with nonmaterial nonpublic information. A direct statement of the board's earnings figures is material on its own, so the mosaic theory does not apply.

  2. Rahul Mehta, CFA, is a mining analyst at an asset manager in Mumbai. A company he covers invites a group of analysts to visit its new copper mine in a remote part of Zambia, which has no scheduled commercial flights. The company offers to fly the analysts in on its chartered plane and to pay for two nights at the mine's guest house. After the visit, it also offers each analyst and a guest a free weekend at a luxury safari lodge.

    To comply with the Standards, Mehta should most appropriately:

    Answer: B.

    Standard I(B), Independence and Objectivity, asks members to avoid gifts and benefits that could reasonably be expected to compromise their objectivity. Best practice is for an analyst's firm to pay for its own travel to company visits. When no commercial transport exists, accepting modest, company-arranged transport and accommodation that are needed to do the job is acceptable.

    The safari weekend has no research purpose and is a lavish gift. Disclosing it does not make it acceptable, so Mehta should decline it. Paying for a separate charter is not required when no commercial alternative exists.

  3. Sofia Marchetti, CFA, manages 40 discretionary equity accounts at a Milan wealth manager. All of the accounts have the same mandate and the same risk profile. Her firm receives an allocation of shares in a heavily oversubscribed initial public offering, enough for about half of her accounts. Marchetti allocates all of the shares to the 15 accounts that pay the highest fees, reasoning that these clients bring the most revenue to the firm.

    Marchetti's allocation most likely violates the Standard relating to:

    Answer: B.

    Standard III(B), Fair Dealing, requires members to treat all clients fairly when making investment recommendations or taking investment action. Allocating a limited, attractive issue to the highest-fee clients disadvantages the others. The usual fair method is a pro-rata allocation across all accounts for which the investment is suitable.

    The shares are suitable for every account, because all share one mandate, so suitability is not the issue. No outside compensation is involved.

  4. Chen Wei, CFA, manages a portfolio for a 72-year-old retired teacher in Singapore. Her investment policy statement, agreed last year, describes a low risk tolerance, a need for regular income, and a long-term allocation of 70% to investment-grade bonds and 30% to dividend-paying equities. Wei expects emerging markets to rally strongly. Without contacting the client, he moves a quarter of her portfolio into a leveraged emerging-market equity fund. The fund rises by 18% over the following six months.

    Wei's action most likely:

    Answer: A.

    Standard III(C), Suitability, requires a manager to act within the client's stated objectives and constraints and to judge each investment in the context of the whole portfolio. A leveraged emerging-market fund does not fit a low-risk, income-focused mandate.

    Discretion gives Wei authority to trade, but only within the investment policy statement. The later gain is irrelevant: suitability is judged when the decision is made, not by its outcome.

  5. Pinnacle Quant, a new asset manager in Toronto, launches its first fund after building a model that selects stocks by momentum and value scores. Its marketing brochure shows a chart of the strategy's returns over the past ten years, with the title 'Pinnacle Quant: 14.2% a year since 2016'. The returns come from applying the model to historical data. The fund itself started trading three months ago. The brochure is prepared by Tom Hale, CFA, who does not state anywhere that the returns are simulated.

    Hale's brochure most likely violates the Standard relating to:

    Answer: C.

    Standard III(D), Performance Presentation, requires performance information to be fair, accurate and complete. Back-tested (simulated) results may be shown, but they must be clearly labelled as hypothetical. Presenting them as the fund's own record misleads prospective clients.

    The model may well have a reasonable basis, and nothing in the case harms the employer, so the other Standards are not the main concern.

  6. Amara Okafor, CFA, manages the endowment portfolio of a university in Lagos for an investment firm. The chair of the university's finance committee tells her that if the portfolio beats its benchmark by more than two percentage points this year, the committee will pay for her to attend an investment conference in London. The portfolio beats its benchmark by three percentage points. Okafor attends the conference at the committee's expense and tells her manager about it when she returns.

    Okafor's conduct most likely:

    Answer: B.

    Standard IV(B), Additional Compensation Arrangements, requires members to obtain written consent from all parties involved before accepting any compensation or benefit, from a client or anyone else, that could create a conflict with the employer's interest.

    An incentive offered by a client can affect how a manager treats that client compared with others. Telling the manager afterwards, and without written consent, is not enough, even though the reward was linked to good results.

  7. Diego Alvarez, CFA, is a portfolio manager at a firm in Madrid. On Monday morning his investment committee approves buying a large block of shares in a mid-sized Spanish bank for all client accounts. Before he places the client orders, Alvarez buys 2,000 shares of the bank for his own account. He then executes the client orders over the course of the day, and the bank's share price rises by 3%.

    Alvarez most likely violated the Standard relating to:

    Answer: A.

    Standard VI(B), Priority of Transactions, requires client and employer transactions to take priority over personal transactions. Buying ahead of known client orders (front-running) puts his own interest first and benefits from the price impact of client buying.

    Market manipulation concerns trading or information designed to distort prices or volumes to mislead others, which is not what happened. No client information was disclosed to outsiders.

  8. Ana Ruiz passed Level II of the CFA Program in 2025 and is registered for the Level III exam in May 2027. She updates her professional networking profile. Her headline now reads 'Ana Ruiz, CFA Level II', and the summary says she 'expects to earn the CFA designation in 2027'.

    With respect to the Standard on reference to CFA Institute, the CFA designation and the CFA Program, Ruiz has most likely:

    Answer: A.

    Standard VII(B) prohibits implying a partial designation. There is no 'CFA Level II' credential, so the headline violates the Standard. She could instead write that she is a Level III candidate in the CFA Program or that she passed Level II.

    Saying that she expects to earn the charter in 2027 is a factual statement of her plans, which is allowed, as long as she does not overstate her status or promise that she will pass.

  9. Kenji Watanabe, CFA, is promoted to head of equity trading at a Tokyo broker and becomes responsible for supervising 12 traders. In his first week he finds that the firm has no written policies on personal trading by employees and no system for reviewing trades for conflicts. He raises this with senior management, who tell him that they will consider adopting a policy next year.

    To comply with the Standard on responsibilities of supervisors, Watanabe should most appropriately:

    Answer: B.

    Standard IV(C), Responsibilities of Supervisors, requires members to make reasonable efforts to ensure that those they supervise comply with laws, rules and the Standards. If the firm's compliance system is inadequate, members should decline in writing to accept supervisory responsibility until the firm adopts reasonable procedures.

    Reviewing every trade himself is not a substitute for a compliance system, and the Standards do not require him to resign.

  10. Lucas Moreau, CFA, runs a small investment advisory firm in Lyon. His clients' trades are executed through a broker that charges slightly higher commissions than its competitors. In return, the broker pays for Moreau's third-party research subscriptions, which he uses in managing all client portfolios, and also for part of the rent on his office.

    Which of these arrangements is most likely a violation of the Standards?

    Answer: A.

    Client brokerage belongs to the client. Under Standard III(A), Loyalty, Prudence and Care, a manager may use client commissions (soft dollars) only for goods and services that directly help in managing the client's account, such as research. Office rent benefits the manager's business, not the clients, so paying it with client commissions breaches the duty of loyalty.

    The research arrangement is acceptable if it benefits the clients and the manager seeks best execution. Disclosure does not make paying rent with client money acceptable.

Three things to remember for ethics

  • Name the Standard first, then decide. Most wrong answers describe a real rule that does not fit the facts.
  • When local law and the Code differ, the stricter rule wins.
  • Disclosure fixes many conflicts of interest, but not the use of material nonpublic information.

How to use these questions

Answer each question before you look at the explanation, and time yourself: the real exam allows about 90 seconds per question. Mark every question you got right for the wrong reason as wrong. When you miss one, write down the idea behind it rather than the answer, and come back to the page in a week.

These questions are written by First Principles to match the style and level of the exam. They are not taken from CFA Institute's curriculum or mock exams, which you should also use: they are included with your exam registration.

Questions people ask

Are these real CFA exam questions?

No. CFA Institute does not publish past exam questions. These are original questions written to match the Level I format: a short case, often with a table of data, followed by three answer options. Each explanation shows the working and why the two wrong options are wrong.

How many ethics questions are on Level I?

Ethical and professional standards carries 10–15% of the exam from 2027, which is roughly 18 to 27 of the 180 questions.

Where can I find more Level I practice questions?

Use the practice questions and mock exams in CFA Institute's learning ecosystem first; they come with registration. Then use one third-party question bank, and redo every question you missed.
