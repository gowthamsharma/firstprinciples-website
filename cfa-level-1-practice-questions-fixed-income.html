CFA Level I practice questions: Fixed income, with worked answers

By Gowtham KumarPractice questions

Ten original fixed income questions written like the real Level I exam: short cases with data, three options each, and worked explanations. Bond pricing, yields, the term structure, duration, convexity and credit. Fixed income carries 11–14% of the exam from 2027.

Fixed income is where many Level I candidates lose marks: bond pricing, yield measures, the term structure, duration and convexity, and credit. Almost every concept follows from one idea: a bond's price is the present value of its cash flows.

Topic weight from 2027: 11–14% of Level I, about 20 to 25 of the 180 questions.

10 questions · Level I format · about 15 to 20 minutes

Choose an answer to see the explanation.

  1. A five-year corporate bond pays a 4% annual coupon in two semi-annual instalments and has a par value of 100. Its yield to maturity, quoted on a semi-annual bond basis, is 5%.

    The bond's price is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    Use semi-annual periods: N = 10, coupon = 2 per period, yield = 2.5% per period. Price = 2 × [1 − 1.025^−10] / 0.025 + 100 / 1.025^10 = 17.50 + 78.12 = 95.62. The bond trades below par because its coupon rate is below its yield.

    95.67 treats the bond as paying 4 once a year discounted at 5% annually, ignoring the semi-annual timing. 100.00 would be correct only if the coupon rate equalled the yield.

  2. A bond with a 6% annual coupon and a par value of 100 has exactly four years to maturity as of its last coupon date. Its yield to maturity is 5%. The bond is being settled 120 days after the last coupon date, and the market uses a 30/360 day count.

    The bond's flat (clean) price on the settlement date is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Price at the last coupon date: PV of 6, 6, 6 and 106 at 5% = 103.55. Full (dirty) price on settlement = 103.55 × 1.05^(120/360) = 105.24. Accrued interest = 6 × 120/360 = 2.00. Flat price = full price − accrued interest = 105.24 − 2.00 = 103.24.

    105.24 is the full price, which the buyer actually pays. 103.55 is the value on the last coupon date, before the passage of time.

  3. An investor considers a five-year bond with a 7% annual coupon that trades at a price of 104 per 100 of par.

    Which statement about the bond's yield measures is most accurate?

    Answer: B.

    For a premium bond, the investor pays more than par but receives only par at maturity, and that loss pulls the yield to maturity below the coupon rate. Current yield = annual coupon / price = 7 / 104 = 6.73%, which is below the 7% coupon rate. The yield to maturity also accounts for the 4-point loss at maturity, so it is lower still (about 6.05%).

    For a discount bond the order reverses: yield to maturity > current yield > coupon rate.

  4. Government spot rates are 3.0% for one year, 3.5% for two years and 4.0% for three years (annual compounding). A three-year government bond pays a 5% annual coupon.

    The no-arbitrage price of the bond, per 100 of par, is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Discount each cash flow at the spot rate for its own maturity: 5 / 1.03 + 5 / 1.035² + 105 / 1.04³ = 4.854 + 4.668 + 93.345 = 102.87.

    102.78 discounts every cash flow at 4.0%, the three-year spot rate. That rate is close to the bond's yield to maturity, but it misprices the early coupons. 105.66 discounts every cash flow at the one-year rate of 3.0%.

  5. The one-year spot rate is 3.0% and the two-year spot rate is 3.5% (annual compounding). An investor is deciding whether to buy a two-year zero-coupon bond or to buy a one-year zero and reinvest the proceeds for a second year.

    The one-year rate one year from now (the 1y1y forward rate) that would make the investor indifferent is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    No arbitrage requires (1.035)² = 1.03 × (1 + f), so f = 1.071225 / 1.03 − 1 = 4.00%. An upward-sloping spot curve implies forward rates above the spot rates.

    3.50% is the two-year spot rate. 5.01% is the 2y1y forward rate if the three-year spot rate is 4.0% (1.04³ / 1.035² − 1), not the 1y1y rate.

  6. A portfolio manager holds a bond with an annual coupon, a Macaulay duration of 7.20 and a yield to maturity of 6.0%. Its annual convexity is 65. She expects yields to rise by 50 basis points immediately.

    The estimated percentage change in the bond's full price, including the convexity adjustment, is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Modified duration = Macaulay duration / (1 + yield) = 7.20 / 1.06 = 6.79. Duration effect = −6.79 × 0.005 = −3.40%. Convexity effect = ½ × 65 × 0.005² = +0.08%. Total ≈ −3.32%.

    −3.40% ignores convexity, which always helps an option-free bond. −3.60% uses Macaulay duration instead of modified duration.

  7. An analyst values a bond at 100.00. Using her pricing model, she finds that the price would be 100.85 if yields fell by 10 basis points and 99.16 if yields rose by 10 basis points.

    The bond's approximate modified duration is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    Approximate modified duration = (PV− − PV+) / (2 × PV0 × Δyield) = (100.85 − 99.16) / (2 × 100 × 0.001) = 1.69 / 0.2 = 8.45.

    16.90 forgets the 2 in the denominator, measuring the change over a 20 basis point range as if it were 10 basis points. 0.85 is the price change for a 10 basis point fall in yields, not a duration.

  8. A fund holds €5 million par value of a bond that is priced at 98.50 per 100 of par (full price). The bond's modified duration is 6.4.

    The price value of a basis point (PVBP) of the position is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Market value = 5,000,000 × 0.985 = €4,925,000. Money duration = modified duration × full price = 6.4 × 4,925,000 = €31.52 million. PVBP = money duration × 0.0001 = €3,152: the position loses about €3,152 for each one basis point rise in yield.

    €3,200 uses par value instead of the market price. €31,520,000 is the money duration, before scaling to one basis point.

  9. A credit analyst assesses a €1,000,000 corporate bond. She estimates a 2% probability that the issuer will default over the next year, and that bondholders would recover 40% of their exposure in a default.

    The expected loss on the bond over the next year is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    Expected loss = probability of default × loss given default × exposure. Loss given default = 1 − recovery rate = 60%. Expected loss = 0.02 × 0.60 × 1,000,000 = €12,000.

    €8,000 multiplies by the recovery rate instead of the loss rate. €20,000 assumes nothing is recovered in default.

  10. An asset-backed security has three tranches backed by a 100 million pool of auto loans:

    TranchePrincipal (millions)
    Senior70
    Mezzanine20
    Subordinated (equity)10

    Losses are absorbed from the most junior tranche upwards. Over the life of the deal, the pool suffers credit losses of 12 million.

    The loss to the mezzanine tranche, as a percentage of its principal, is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    The subordinated tranche absorbs the first 10 million of losses and is wiped out. The remaining 2 million falls on the mezzanine tranche: 2 / 20 = 10% of its principal. The senior tranche is untouched; its credit enhancement from the tranches below it is 30 million.

    12% applies the pool's loss rate to every tranche, ignoring subordination. 0% would be true only if losses were below 10 million.

Three things to remember for fixed income

  • If the coupon is below the yield, the bond trades below par, and vice versa.
  • Duration gives the first estimate of the price change; convexity corrects it, always upwards for option-free bonds.
  • Forward rates make investing short and rolling equal to investing long.

How to use these questions

Answer each question before you look at the explanation, and time yourself: the real exam allows about 90 seconds per question. Mark every question you got right for the wrong reason as wrong. When you miss one, write down the idea behind it rather than the answer, and come back to the page in a week.

These questions are written by First Principles to match the style and level of the exam. They are not taken from CFA Institute's curriculum or mock exams, which you should also use: they are included with your exam registration.

Questions people ask

Are these real CFA exam questions?

No. CFA Institute does not publish past exam questions. These are original questions written to match the Level I format: a short case, often with a table of data, followed by three answer options. Each explanation shows the working and why the two wrong options are wrong.

How many fixed income questions are on Level I?

Fixed income carries 11–14% of the exam from 2027, which is roughly 20 to 25 of the 180 questions.

Where can I find more Level I practice questions?

Use the practice questions and mock exams in CFA Institute's learning ecosystem first; they come with registration. Then use one third-party question bank, and redo every question you missed.
