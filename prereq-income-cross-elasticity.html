Income and cross-price elasticity

How demand reacts to income separates luxuries, necessities and inferior goods. How it reacts to another good's price separates substitutes from complements.

Concept 28 of 50 · Economics · Demand and supply analysis · 2 min read

Income elasticity = %ΔQ / %Δincome. Cross-price elasticity = %ΔQ of good A / %Δprice of good B.

From first principles

Price is not the only thing that moves demand. Two other elasticities measure two other forces:

Income elasticity: what happens to demand when buyers' incomes rise?

  • Positive: normal good. Above 1: a luxury (spending rises faster than income: travel, fine dining). Between 0 and 1: a necessity (food staples).
  • Negative: inferior good. People buy less as they get richer (the cheapest brands, long-distance bus travel).

Cross-price elasticity: what happens to demand for A when B's price changes?

  • Positive: substitutes. B gets dearer, buyers switch to A (tea and coffee).
  • Negative: complements. B gets dearer, buyers use less of both (printers and ink).

Worked example

Incomes rise 4%. Demand for restaurant meals rises 6% (income elasticity 1.5, a luxury); demand for own-brand pasta falls 2% (−0.5, inferior). A cinema raises prices 10%; streaming subscriptions rise 3% (cross elasticity +0.3, substitutes).

Exam traps

  • The sign carries the meaning: watch for negative values.
  • "Normal" and "inferior" describe the reaction to income, not quality.

Why it matters at Level III

Income elasticities explain why some sectors are cyclical (luxuries, durables) and others defensive (staples). That matters for sector allocation across the business cycle.
