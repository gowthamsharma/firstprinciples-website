CFA Level I practice questions: Alternative investments, with worked answers

By Gowtham KumarPractice questions

Ten original alternative investments questions written like the real Level I exam: short cases with data, three options each, and worked explanations. Hedge funds, private equity, real estate, infrastructure, commodities and their fees. Alternative investments carries 6–9% of the exam from 2027.

Alternative investments covers hedge funds, private equity, real estate, infrastructure, commodities and digital assets. The favourite calculation is a fee waterfall; the favourite concept is why reported returns of illiquid assets look smoother than they are.

Topic weight from 2027: 6–9% of Level I, about 11 to 16 of the 180 questions.

10 questions · Level I format · about 15 to 20 minutes

Choose an answer to see the explanation.

  1. A hedge fund charges a 2% management fee on assets at the start of the year and a 20% incentive fee on gains net of the management fee. There is no hurdle rate. An investor's capital at the start of the year is $100 million, and its gross value at the end of the year, before fees, is $118 million.

    The investor's net return for the year is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    Management fee = 2%×100=2.02\% \times 100 = 2.02%×100=2.0. Incentive fee = 20%×(118−100−2)=3.220\% \times (118 - 100 - 2) = 3.220%×(118−100−2)=3.2. Total fees = 5.2. Net value = 118−5.2=112.8118 - 5.2 = 112.8118−5.2=112.8, so the net return is 12.8%.

    12.4% charges the incentive fee on the gross gain of 18 (3.6), independently of the management fee. 16.0% deducts only the management fee.

  2. An investor puts $100 million into a hedge fund that charges a 20% incentive fee subject to a high-water mark (ignore the management fee). At the end of Year 1, the fund's value after fees falls to $90 million. At the end of Year 2, before fees, it is worth $108 million.

    The incentive fee for Year 2 is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    A high-water mark means the manager earns an incentive fee only on gains above the highest previous net value, here $100 million. Fee = 20%×(108−100)20\% \times (108 - 100)20%×(108−100) = $1.6 million.

    $3.6 million charges 20% on the whole Year 2 gain from 90 to 108, which would make the investor pay twice for recovering the same losses. $0 would apply only if the fund were still below $100 million.

  3. A private equity fund invests $50 million in Company A and $50 million in Company B. It later exits A for $90 million and B for $40 million. The general partner receives 20% carried interest, calculated on a whole-of-fund (European) basis after limited partners get back all their contributed capital. Ignore any hurdle rate and fees.

    The general partner's total carried interest is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Whole-of-fund: total proceeds 130 − capital 100 = profit of 30. Carried interest = 20%×3020\% \times 3020%×30 = $6 million, paid only after the limited partners have their 100 back.

    $8 million is what a deal-by-deal (American) waterfall without clawback would pay: 20%×4020\% \times 4020%×40 on Deal A, with nothing given back for the loss on Deal B. $18 million is 20% of A's exit proceeds.

  4. An investor values an office building in Frankfurt. The building's net operating income next year is expected to be €1.2 million, and comparable buildings sell at a capitalisation rate of 6%. The property carries a mortgage of €12 million.

    The value of the owner's equity in the building is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Direct capitalisation: property value =NOIcap rate=1.20.06= \dfrac{NOI}{\text{cap rate}} = \dfrac{1.2}{0.06}=cap rateNOI​=0.061.2​ = €20 million. Equity = property value − debt =20−12= 20 - 12=20−12 = €8 million.

    €20 million is the value of the whole property, not of the owner's equity. €12 million is the mortgage.

  5. Over a year, a fully collateralised long position in crude oil futures earns the following components:

    Spot price return+3.0%
    Roll return−1.5%
    Collateral (cash) return+4.0%

    The total return of the position is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    Total return on a collateralised commodity futures position = spot price return + roll return + collateral return = 3.0−1.5+4.0=5.5%3.0 - 1.5 + 4.0 = 5.5\%3.0−1.5+4.0=5.5%. The negative roll return reflects a market in contango: each expiring contract is replaced by a more expensive later one.

    1.5% leaves out the return on the collateral. 8.5% adds the roll return as if it were positive.

  6. A pension fund wants to add infrastructure to its portfolio. Its trustees want stable, predictable cash income in the near future and limited exposure to construction risk.

    Which investment best matches these objectives?

    Answer: C.

    Brownfield infrastructure, meaning existing assets with operating histories, offers established, often contracted or regulated cash flows. Greenfield projects (assets still to be built) carry construction and demand risk and produce no income until they are complete.

    The battery startup is venture capital, with highly uncertain and distant cash flows.

  7. A real estate fund reports quarterly returns based on independent appraisals of its properties. Over ten years, its returns show a standard deviation of 5% a year and a correlation of 0.2 with listed equities. A listed REIT index with similar properties shows a standard deviation of 18% and a correlation of 0.6 over the same period.

    The most likely explanation for the difference is that appraisal-based returns:

    Answer: C.

    Appraisals rely partly on past valuations and on infrequent comparable transactions, so they lag the market and smooth reported returns. This understates the true volatility and the correlation with other assets, which makes the fund look like a better diversifier than it is. Analysts often 'unsmooth' appraisal returns before using them in asset allocation.

    Some of the REIT's extra volatility does come from listing and leverage, but most of the gap is the smoothing effect.

  8. An investor holds a fund of hedge funds. The underlying hedge funds earned a combined return of 12% for the year after their own fees. The fund of funds charges a 1% management fee and a 10% incentive fee on returns after its management fee.

    The investor's net return is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    After the 1% management fee, the return is 11%. The incentive fee is 10%×11%=1.1%10\% \times 11\% = 1.1\%10%×11%=1.1%. Net return = 11.0−1.1=9.9%11.0 - 1.1 = 9.9\%11.0−1.1=9.9%.

    Funds of funds add a second layer of fees on top of the underlying funds' fees. 11.0% ignores the incentive fee, and 12.0% ignores both of the fund of funds' fees.

  9. A consortium of ten European banks builds a distributed ledger to settle trades among themselves. Only the member banks can validate transactions and see the ledger, and a central operator approves new members.

    This ledger is best described as a:

    Answer: C.

    In a permissioned network, participation (and often visibility) is restricted to approved members, which suits regulated institutions that need privacy and control.

    In a permissionless network, such as the networks behind Bitcoin or Ethereum, anyone can join, validate transactions and view the ledger. Proof of work is a way of validating transactions on a public chain, not a description of who may participate.

  10. A hedge fund manager learns that Orion Media has agreed to buy Vega Studios in an all-share deal, offering 0.5 Orion shares for each Vega share. Vega trades at a discount to the offer value. The manager buys Vega shares and short-sells Orion shares in the ratio of the offer.

    The main risk of this strategy is that:

    Answer: C.

    This is merger arbitrage. Buying the target and shorting the acquirer in the exchange ratio locks in the spread and largely hedges market moves. The return depends on the deal closing. If it breaks, the target usually falls sharply and the acquirer may rise, so both legs lose. The payoff resembles selling insurance against deal failure.

    General market falls are largely hedged by the paired position, and interest rate changes are a minor factor.

Three things to remember for alternative investments

  • Read fee questions twice: is the incentive fee charged on gross returns or after the management fee? Is there a hurdle or a high-water mark?
  • Value = NOI / cap rate.
  • Commodity futures returns = spot + roll + collateral.

How to use these questions

Answer each question before you look at the explanation, and time yourself: the real exam allows about 90 seconds per question. Mark every question you got right for the wrong reason as wrong. When you miss one, write down the idea behind it rather than the answer, and come back to the page in a week.

These questions are written by First Principles to match the style and level of the exam. They are not taken from CFA Institute's curriculum or mock exams, which you should also use: they are included with your exam registration.

Questions people ask

Are these real CFA exam questions?

No. CFA Institute does not publish past exam questions. These are original questions written to match the Level I format: a short case, often with a table of data, followed by three answer options. Each explanation shows the working and why the two wrong options are wrong.

How many alternative investments questions are on Level I?

Alternative investments carries 6–9% of the exam from 2027, which is roughly 11 to 16 of the 180 questions.

Where can I find more Level I practice questions?

Use the practice questions and mock exams in CFA Institute's learning ecosystem first; they come with registration. Then use one third-party question bank, and redo every question you missed.
