CFA Level I practice questions: Portfolio management, with worked answers

By Gowtham KumarPractice questions

Ten original portfolio management questions written like the real Level I exam: short cases with data, three options each, and worked explanations. Risk and return, diversification, the CAPM, performance measures and the IPS. Portfolio management carries 8–12% of the exam from 2027.

Portfolio management ties the curriculum together: risk and return, diversification, the CAPM and security market line, performance measures, the investment policy statement and risk management. Several questions need the two-asset portfolio variance formula.

Topic weight from 2027: 8–12% of Level I, about 14 to 22 of the 180 questions.

10 questions · Level I format · about 15 to 20 minutes

Choose an answer to see the explanation.

  1. An investor holds 60% of her portfolio in a global equity fund and 40% in a corporate bond fund. The equity fund's standard deviation is 20%, the bond fund's is 12%, and the correlation between their returns is 0.3.

    The standard deviation of the portfolio is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    σp2=w12σ12+w22σ22+2w1w2ρσ1σ2=0.36(0.04)+0.16(0.0144)+2(0.6)(0.4)(0.3)(0.20)(0.12)=0.0202\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 = 0.36(0.04) + 0.16(0.0144) + 2(0.6)(0.4)(0.3)(0.20)(0.12) = 0.0202σp2​=w12​σ12​+w22​σ22​+2w1​w2​ρσ1​σ2​=0.36(0.04)+0.16(0.0144)+2(0.6)(0.4)(0.3)(0.20)(0.12)=0.0202. σp=0.0202=14.2%\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.0202} = 14.2\%σp​=0.0202​=14.2%.

    16.8% is the weighted average of the two standard deviations, which would be the portfolio's risk only with perfect correlation. 12.9% leaves out the covariance term, as if the correlation were zero.

  2. An analyst estimates that Polaris Semiconductors will return 12.5% next year. Polaris's beta is 1.4, the risk-free rate is 3%, and the expected return on the market is 9%.

    According to the capital asset pricing model, Polaris is most likely:

    Answer: B.

    CAPM required return =Rf+β[E(Rm)−Rf]=3%+1.4×(9%−3%)=11.4%= R_f + \beta[E(R_m) - R_f] = 3\% + 1.4 \times (9\% - 3\%) = 11.4\%=Rf​+β[E(Rm​)−Rf​]=3%+1.4×(9%−3%)=11.4%. The analyst's forecast of 12.5% exceeds this, so the stock plots above the security market line: it is undervalued and a buy.

    15.6% multiplies beta by the market return instead of the market risk premium. Beta alone says nothing about whether a stock is mispriced.

  3. A consultant reviews a fund's results for the year:

    MeasureFundMarket index
    Return12.0%10.0%
    Standard deviation18.0%15.0%
    Beta1.11.0

    The risk-free rate was 3.0%. The fund's Jensen's alpha is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Jensen's alpha =Rp−[Rf+βp(Rm−Rf)]=12.0−[3.0+1.1×(10.0−3.0)]=12.0−10.7=1.3%= R_p - [R_f + \beta_p(R_m - R_f)] = 12.0 - [3.0 + 1.1 \times (10.0 - 3.0)] = 12.0 - 10.7 = 1.3\%=Rp​−[Rf​+βp​(Rm​−Rf​)]=12.0−[3.0+1.1×(10.0−3.0)]=12.0−10.7=1.3%.

    2.0% is the simple excess over the index, which ignores the fund's higher beta. 9.0% is the fund's return over the risk-free rate. For reference, the fund's Sharpe ratio is (12−3)/18=0.50(12 - 3) / 18 = 0.50(12−3)/18=0.50, against the market's 0.47, and its M² is 3+0.50×15=10.5%3 + 0.50 \times 15 = 10.5\%3+0.50×15=10.5%.

  4. An adviser uses the utility function U = E(r) − ½Aσ² to choose among three portfolios for a client whose risk aversion coefficient A is 4:

    PortfolioExpected returnStandard deviation
    X12%25%
    Y9%15%
    Z7%10%

    The portfolio that gives the client the highest utility is:

    Answer: A.

    With returns in decimals, U=E(R)−12Aσ2U = E(R) - \tfrac{1}{2}A\sigma^2U=E(R)−21​Aσ2: U(X)=0.12−0.5×4×0.0625=−0.005U(X) = 0.12 - 0.5 \times 4 \times 0.0625 = -0.005U(X)=0.12−0.5×4×0.0625=−0.005; U(Y)=0.09−2×0.0225=0.045U(Y) = 0.09 - 2 \times 0.0225 = 0.045U(Y)=0.09−2×0.0225=0.045; U(Z)=0.07−2×0.01=0.050U(Z) = 0.07 - 2 \times 0.01 = 0.050U(Z)=0.07−2×0.01=0.050. Z gives the highest utility.

    For a less risk-averse client with A = 2, the utilities would be 0.0575, 0.0675 and 0.060, so Y would be best. The higher the risk aversion, the more heavily variance is penalised.

  5. The correlation between the returns of Fjord Shipping and the market is 0.6. Fjord's standard deviation is 25%, and the market's is 15%.

    Fjord's beta is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    β=Cov(Ri,Rm)σm2=ρ σiσm=0.6×2515=1.00\beta = \dfrac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\sigma_m^2} = \rho\,\dfrac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_m} = 0.6 \times \dfrac{25}{15} = 1.00β=σm2​Cov(Ri​,Rm​)​=ρσm​σi​​=0.6×1525​=1.00. Although Fjord is much more volatile than the market, only the part of its volatility that moves with the market counts as systematic risk.

    0.60 is the correlation alone. 1.67 is the ratio of standard deviations, which would be the beta only with perfect correlation.

  6. Karim, aged 35, is a surgeon with a high and secure income, substantial savings and no debts. He plans to retire in 25 years and has no need to draw on his portfolio before then. In their first meeting he tells his adviser that he sold all of his equities in March 2020 because he 'could not sleep', and that he would be very upset to see his portfolio fall by more than 10% in any year.

    Karim's overall risk tolerance is best described as:

    Answer: A.

    Karim's ability to take risk is high: long horizon, secure income, savings and no liquidity needs. His willingness, shown by his past behaviour and his stated loss limit, is low. When ability and willingness conflict, the adviser should generally set risk tolerance at the lower of the two and discuss the gap with the client, perhaps educating him about the costs of being too conservative.

    Ignoring his willingness risks another panic sale at the worst time.

  7. Two stocks, Arbor and Birch, have the same total risk (standard deviation of 30%). Arbor's beta is 0.6 and Birch's beta is 1.4. Both are held in a well-diversified portfolio.

    According to the capital asset pricing model, which stock should offer the higher expected return?

    Answer: A.

    In the CAPM, only systematic (non-diversifiable) risk, measured by beta, is rewarded. Unsystematic risk can be removed by diversification at no cost, so the market pays no premium for bearing it. Birch's beta of 1.4 gives it the higher required and expected return.

    Arbor's lower beta means a larger part of its total risk is diversifiable, which lowers its required return.

  8. A bank reports that its trading book has a one-day 5% value at risk (VaR) of €2 million.

    The statement is best interpreted as:

    Answer: B.

    A 5% one-day VaR of €2 million means that on 1 day in 20, the loss is expected to be €2 million or more, and on the other 95% of days it is expected to be smaller (or there is a gain).

    VaR is not a maximum: it says nothing about how large losses can be in that worst 5% of days, which is why risk managers also use expected shortfall and stress tests.

  9. An investor tends to sell shares soon after they rise in price, to 'lock in' the gain. He holds on to shares that have fallen, saying he will sell them 'once they get back to what I paid'.

    This behaviour is most consistent with:

    Answer: A.

    Loss aversion means losses feel more painful than equivalent gains feel good. It leads investors to realise gains too early and hold losers too long, a pattern known as the disposition effect. The purchase price acts as a reference point, though it has no bearing on the shares' future returns.

    Overconfidence leads to excessive trading and under-diversification. Conservatism is slowness to update views when new information arrives.

  10. An investor puts 70% of her capital in a risky portfolio with an expected return of 10% and a standard deviation of 20%, and the remaining 30% in a risk-free asset yielding 3%.

    The expected return and standard deviation of her complete portfolio are closest to:

    Answer: A.

    Expected return = 0.30×3%+0.70×10%=7.9%0.30 \times 3\% + 0.70 \times 10\% = 7.9\%0.30×3%+0.70×10%=7.9%. The risk-free asset has no volatility and no correlation with the risky portfolio, so the standard deviation = 0.70×20%=14.0%0.70 \times 20\% = 14.0\%0.70×20%=14.0%. The portfolio lies on the capital allocation line, whose slope (the Sharpe ratio) is (10−3)/20=0.35(10 - 3) / 20 = 0.35(10−3)/20=0.35.

    20.0% ignores the 30% held in the risk-free asset. 10.0% is the expected return on the risky portfolio alone.

Three things to remember for portfolio management

  • Only correlations below 1 reduce portfolio risk.
  • Sharpe uses total risk; Treynor and Jensen's alpha use beta.
  • When ability and willingness to take risk differ, the lower one usually sets the policy.

How to use these questions

Answer each question before you look at the explanation, and time yourself: the real exam allows about 90 seconds per question. Mark every question you got right for the wrong reason as wrong. When you miss one, write down the idea behind it rather than the answer, and come back to the page in a week.

These questions are written by First Principles to match the style and level of the exam. They are not taken from CFA Institute's curriculum or mock exams, which you should also use: they are included with your exam registration.

Questions people ask

Are these real CFA exam questions?

No. CFA Institute does not publish past exam questions. These are original questions written to match the Level I format: a short case, often with a table of data, followed by three answer options. Each explanation shows the working and why the two wrong options are wrong.

How many portfolio management questions are on Level I?

Portfolio management carries 8–12% of the exam from 2027, which is roughly 14 to 22 of the 180 questions.

Where can I find more Level I practice questions?

Use the practice questions and mock exams in CFA Institute's learning ecosystem first; they come with registration. Then use one third-party question bank, and redo every question you missed.
