Perfect competition in the long run

By Gowtham KumarLesson 34 of 50

When anyone can enter and every product is the same, profits attract rivals until prices fall to the lowest average cost. Economic profit is competed away.

P=MR=MC=min⁡ATCP = MR = MC = \min ATCP=MR=MC=minATC

In the long run, free entry pushes price down to the lowest point of average total cost.

From first principles

Perfect competition rests on four assumptions: very many buyers and sellers, an identical product, free entry and exit, and full information. Together they make every firm a price taker: it can sell as much as it wants at the market price and nothing above it.

Marginal revenue equals price

Because the price does not move when a small firm sells more, each extra unit adds exactly the price to revenue:

MR=AR=PMR = AR = PMR=AR=P

The firm's demand curve is horizontal at the market price. Its profit-maximising rule, MR=MCMR = MCMR=MC, becomes P=MCP = MCP=MC, so the firm's short-run supply curve is its marginal cost curve above minimum average variable cost (see profit maximisation and shutdown).

Short run: profits and losses can happen

In the short run the number of firms is fixed. If demand is strong, the market price can be above average total cost, and every firm earns an economic profit. If it is weak, firms can make losses (and keep operating as long as price covers AVC).

Long run: entry and exit remove them

  • Economic profit signals that this industry pays more than the next-best use of capital. New firms enter, market supply shifts right, and the price falls.
  • Economic losses push some firms out. Supply shifts left and the price rises.
  • Entry and exit stop only when price equals minimum average total cost. Each firm then earns zero economic profit, which is still a normal accounting return on its capital.

At that point:

P=MR=MC=min⁡ATCP = MR = MC = \min ATCP=MR=MC=minATC

Two kinds of efficiency follow. Allocative efficiency: price equals marginal cost, so the last unit is worth to buyers exactly what it costs to make. Productive efficiency: firms produce at the lowest possible average cost.

Worked example 1: profit, then entry

A firm in a competitive industry faces a market price of 15. Its marginal cost equals 15 at an output of 600 units, where its ATC is 13.

Economic profit=(P−ATC)×Q=(15−13)×600=1,200\text{Economic profit} = (P - ATC) \times Q = (15 - 13) \times 600 = 1{,}200Economic profit=(P−ATC)×Q=(15−13)×600=1,200

That profit attracts entrants. Market supply shifts right until the price reaches minimum ATC, which for this firm (and identical rivals) is 12. At a price of 12, each firm earns zero economic profit, and the industry produces more in total, from more firms.

Worked example 2: a permanent rise in demand

Start in long-run equilibrium at a price of 12. Demand rises permanently.

Short run Long run
Market price Rises above 12 Back to 12 (constant-cost industry)
Each existing firm's output Rises (moves up its MC curve) Back to the minimum-ATC output
Each firm's economic profit Positive Zero
Number of firms Unchanged Higher
Industry output Rises Rises further

In a constant-cost industry, input prices do not change as the industry grows, so the long-run supply curve is horizontal at minimum ATC. In an increasing-cost industry (growth bids up wages or raw materials), each firm's cost curves shift up, and the long-run price ends up higher than before, so long-run supply slopes upwards.

Why analysts care

A company in a near-competitive market (a commodity producer, a generic manufacturer) cannot expect high returns to last: they invite capacity. Analysts watch for the classic cycle: high prices, then a wave of new capacity, then a glut and falling prices. Sustained returns above the cost of capital need something that blocks entry.

Exam traps

  • Zero economic profit is not zero accounting profit. The owners still earn a normal return on their capital.
  • The firm's demand curve is flat; the market demand curve still slopes down.
  • In the long run, price equals minimum ATC, not minimum AVC (that is the short-run shutdown point).
  • A demand increase raises the long-run price only in an increasing-cost industry.

Questions people ask

Why do firms earn zero economic profit in the long run?

Because any profit above normal attracts new firms, which pushes price down, and any losses drive firms out, which pushes price up. The market settles where price equals minimum average cost.

What does price taker mean?

A firm is so small relative to the market that it cannot change the price. It can sell as much as it likes at the market price and nothing above it.

Are any real markets perfectly competitive?

Few are, but some come close: wheat, foreign exchange and widely traded commodities. The model is a benchmark to measure other market structures against.
