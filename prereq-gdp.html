GDP: measuring an economy's output

By Gowtham KumarLesson 37 of 50

GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a period. Spending, income and output are three ways to measure the same thing.

GDP=C+I+G+(X−M)GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)GDP=C+I+G+(X−M)

From first principles

Gross domestic product is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period. Each word in that definition rules something out:

  • Market value: goods are added up at their prices. Unpaid work at home is not counted.
  • Final: goods bought by the end user. Intermediate goods (flour sold to a baker) are left out, or they would be counted twice.
  • Produced: output of the period. Used goods resold this year were counted when they were made; only the dealer's margin is new.
  • Within a country: by location, whoever owns the factory. (GNI instead counts income earned by a country's residents, wherever they produce it.)
  • In a period: a flow, usually a quarter or a year.

Transfer payments (pensions, unemployment benefits) are not part of G: they move income from one person to another without buying any output. Financial transactions (buying shares) are not investment in the GDP sense either; investment means new capital goods, new housing and changes in inventories.

Three ways to measure the same thing

Every sale is spending by one person and income for another, so GDP can be measured three ways:

  1. Expenditure approach: add up spending on final goods, C+I+G+(X−M)C + I + G + (X - M)C+I+G+(X−M).
  2. Income approach: add up incomes earned producing them (wages, profits, rents, interest), plus taxes on production and depreciation.
  3. Value-added (output) approach: add up the value each producer adds, sales minus purchases of intermediate inputs.

Nominal, real and the deflator

Nominal GDP values this year's output at this year's prices. It can rise just because prices rose. Real GDP values this year's output at fixed base-year prices, so it only rises when the quantity of output rises.

GDP deflator=Nominal GDPReal GDP×100\text{GDP deflator} = \frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Real GDP}} \times 100GDP deflator=Real GDPNominal GDP​×100

The deflator is a price index covering everything in GDP, not just consumer purchases.

Worked example 1: the expenditure approach

Component Amount
Household consumption, C 650
Business investment, including inventory build-up, I 200
Government purchases of goods and services, G 180
Exports, X 250
Imports, M 280
Government transfer payments (pensions) 90
Purchases of existing shares 120

GDP=650+200+180+(250−280)=1,000GDP = 650 + 200 + 180 + (250 - 280) = 1{,}000GDP=650+200+180+(250−280)=1,000

Transfers (90) and share purchases (120) are left out: neither is spending on newly produced output. Imports are subtracted because C, I and G include spending on foreign goods, which were not produced here.

Worked example 2: value added

A farmer grows wheat and sells it to a miller for 20. The miller grinds it and sells flour to a baker for 50. The baker sells bread to households for 100.

Producer Sales Bought from others Value added
Farmer 20 0 20
Miller 50 20 30
Baker 100 50 50
Total 170 100

GDP rises by 100, the value of the final good. Adding up every sale (170) would count the wheat three times and the flour twice.

Worked example 3: real growth and the deflator

Last year (the base year) nominal GDP was 1,000. This year nominal GDP is 1,200, and the same output valued at last year's prices (real GDP) is 1,050.

Deflator=1,2001,050×100=114.3\text{Deflator} = \frac{1{,}200}{1{,}050} \times 100 = 114.3Deflator=1,0501,200​×100=114.3

Real growth=1,0501,000−1=5.0%Nominal growth=20.0%\text{Real growth} = \frac{1{,}050}{1{,}000} - 1 = 5.0\% \qquad \text{Nominal growth} = 20.0\%Real growth=1,0001,050​−1=5.0%Nominal growth=20.0%

Prices rose about 14.3%, and output 5%: 1.05×1.143≈1.201.05 \times 1.143 \approx 1.201.05×1.143≈1.20.

Exam traps

  • Intermediate goods, used goods, transfers and financial assets are excluded.
  • Inventory build-up counts as investment in the year the goods are made; selling from inventory next year lowers I as C rises, so GDP is unchanged.
  • Imports are subtracted because they are already inside C, I and G.
  • Real growth, not nominal, measures how much more the economy produces.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between nominal and real GDP?

Nominal GDP values output at this year's prices; real GDP uses fixed base-year prices, so it rises only when the quantity of output rises. Growth figures in the news are real.

What are the ways to measure GDP?

Add up spending on final goods (consumption, investment, government, net exports), add up incomes earned producing them, or add up value added at each stage. In principle all three give the same answer.

What does GDP leave out?

Unpaid work at home, the informal economy, leisure, pollution and how income is shared. It measures output, not well-being.
