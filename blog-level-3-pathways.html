Choosing a CFA Level III pathway

By Gowtham KumarStudy blog

Portfolio Management, Private Markets or Private Wealth: all three lead to the same charter. Choose by the job you want and the material you already use, not by guesses about difficulty.

Since 2025, Level III candidates choose a specialised pathway worth 30–35% of the exam. The other 65–70% is a core shared by everyone: asset allocation, portfolio construction, performance measurement, derivatives and risk management, and ethics.

The three options

Pathway Covers Natural fit
Portfolio Management index and active equity, fixed-income strategies, trade execution, integrated case asset managers, institutional investors, index and ETF roles
Private Markets private equity, private debt, special situations, private real estate and infrastructure private capital, real assets, fund selection
Private Wealth client profiling, goals-based planning, investment planning, risk mitigation, wealth transfer private banks, advisers, family offices

How to choose

  1. The charter is identical. No pathway name appears on it, so the choice does not label you for life.
  2. Choose the material you will use. The pathway readings are some of the most job-relevant in the whole program. If you work with public-market portfolios, Portfolio Management reinforces your day job, and your day job reinforces your studying.
  3. Do not pick on rumoured difficulty. CFA Institute publishes one pass rate for Level III, not one per pathway, so claims that a pathway is easier are guesswork.
  4. Decide before you register. The choice is made at registration and is locked after a short cooling-off period, so treat it as binding for that sitting.

What each pathway goes deeper into

Pathway Goes deeper into Typical jobs
Portfolio Management Portfolio construction and trading, active and passive strategies, performance evaluation, manager selection Portfolio manager, investment analyst, multi-asset and fund-selection roles, institutional asset management
Private Markets Private equity, private credit, real estate and infrastructure: deal structures, valuation, fund terms and the investor's view Private equity and private credit analyst, LP investment teams at pension and sovereign funds, real assets
Private Wealth Advising individuals and families: goals, taxes, estate and succession planning, concentrated positions, client relationships Private banker, wealth manager, family office, financial adviser

Why the choice matters for the exam

The pathway is 30–35% of the Level III exam, the largest single block, so it should be material you will enjoy and can use. The core (asset allocation, portfolio construction, derivatives and risk, performance measurement and ethics) is the same for everyone.

Why it matters less for your career

All three pathways lead to the same CFA charter, with no pathway name attached. Employers hire on your experience and skills, not on which pathway you sat. Choose the one that helps you now: the material you will use at work, or the field you want to move into.

Three quick examples

  • An equity analyst at a mutual fund who wants to run money: Portfolio Management.
  • A credit analyst moving towards private debt funds: Private Markets.
  • A relationship manager at a bank's wealth arm: Private Wealth.

If you cannot decide, Portfolio Management is the broadest choice and closest to the traditional curriculum.

A closer look at what each pathway asks you to do

The table above says what each pathway covers. What matters more for your choice is what the exam asks you to do with it, because Level III questions are cases about a client or a portfolio.

Portfolio Management

You act as a portfolio manager or chief investment officer for an institution or a fund. Typical tasks:

  • choose between full replication, stratified sampling and optimisation for an index fund, and explain the trade-offs in tracking error and cost;
  • pick an active equity style (value, growth, quality, momentum, fundamental or quantitative) for a mandate and say how it shapes portfolio construction;
  • choose a fixed-income strategy for a liability: cash-flow matching, duration matching or contingent immunisation, and position the portfolio for an expected change in the yield curve;
  • evaluate a credit strategy and estimate its expected excess return;
  • plan the execution of a large trade and judge its cost against an arrival-price benchmark.

The final reading is an integrated case that puts these tools together for one institutional client.

Private Markets

You sit on either side of a private fund: the general partner who buys and manages companies or assets, or the limited partner who selects and monitors funds. Typical tasks:

  • compare fund structures, fees and terms such as hurdle rates, catch-up clauses and waterfalls;
  • value a buyout or a venture investment and explain where the returns come from (earnings growth, multiple expansion, debt paydown);
  • assess a private debt investment: direct lending, mezzanine, distressed or venture debt, and how it is secured;
  • analyse special situations, such as companies in restructuring;
  • judge private real estate and infrastructure investments, including how leverage changes their risk and return;
  • read fund performance using IRR, multiples (DPI, TVPI) and public-market equivalents.

Private Wealth

You act as an adviser to an individual or a family. Typical tasks:

  • profile a client: goals, risk tolerance (ability and willingness), time horizon, tax position and family situation;
  • build a goals-based plan that separates essential needs from aspirational goals;
  • choose tax-efficient ways to hold investments and to realise gains;
  • reduce the risk of a concentrated position in one company's shares, such as with hedging, exchange funds or staged sales;
  • manage risks that are not investment risks: longevity, disability, liability and the need for insurance;
  • plan wealth transfer and estate structures, and support the client's philanthropy.

How the pathway fits into your study hours

Most candidates spend around 300 hours on Level III. With the pathway worth 30–35% of the exam, that is roughly 90 to 110 hours of pathway study. A plan that works for most people:

  1. Weeks 1–8: the core, in curriculum order. Asset allocation and portfolio construction come first because the pathway readings assume them. Capital market expectations, the investment policy statement and asset allocation are the backbone of every Level III case.
  2. Weeks 9–13: the pathway. It builds on the core, so it sticks better once the core is in place.
  3. From week 4: written answers. Start practising constructed responses early, a set or two a week, rather than leaving writing for the end. See how to answer constructed-response questions.
  4. Final 4–5 weeks: mock exams and review, mixing core and pathway questions as the real exam does.

Pathway readings use the same tools as the core (duration, the CAPM, options and after-tax returns), so the Level III formula sheet covers both.

A five-question self-check

Answer quickly; the pattern matters more than any single answer.

  1. Do you work, or want to work, on public portfolios (funds, mandates, indexes) or private assets (funds, deals, real estate)?
  2. Are your clients institutions and funds, or individuals and families?
  3. Which part of the Level II curriculum did you enjoy most: equity and fixed income valuation, private equity and real estate, or tax, estate and behavioural material?
  4. Which would you rather be good at in five years: managing a portfolio, underwriting a deal, or advising a family?
  5. Which material will you use at work in the next year?

Mostly public markets and institutions points to Portfolio Management, deals and private funds to Private Markets, and individuals and families to Private Wealth. If your answers are mixed, choose by question 5: material you use at work is cheaper to learn and pays off sooner.

What the pathway does not change

  • The exam format is the same. Every pathway has the same mix of item sets and constructed-response questions, the same length and the same windows.
  • Ethics and the core decide most of the result. About two thirds of the points come from the shared core, so a strong pathway cannot rescue a weak core.
  • The skill being tested is the same: applying a framework to a client's situation and justifying a recommendation in a few precise lines.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing by rumour. Forum claims that one pathway is easier are not based on published data.
  • Choosing the "prestige" option. Private markets are fashionable, but if your job is public equities, you will be studying unfamiliar material with no help from your day job.
  • Ignoring the core while you enjoy the pathway. The pathway is the most interesting part for many candidates, and it is tempting to over-study it.
  • Forgetting the decision is locked. You choose at registration. If you are undecided when you register, pick the pathway closest to your current role.

Questions people ask

Is one Level III pathway easier than the others?

There is no reliable evidence that one is easier. Choose the pathway closest to your job or the job you want, because the material will be more familiar and more useful.

Does the pathway appear on the charter?

No. All pathways lead to the same charter. The pathway affects what you study for one exam, not your designation.

Can I switch pathways later?

You choose when you register for Level III. If you need to sit Level III again, you can usually pick a different pathway at that point.

How much of the exam is the pathway?

About a third. The remaining two-thirds is a core shared by everyone, so the pathway choice matters, but the core decides most of the result.

Which pathway is best for a career in asset management?

Portfolio Management, because it covers index and active strategies, fixed-income portfolio management and trade execution, which are the daily work of most asset managers. If your firm runs private funds, Private Markets may fit better.

Is the Private Wealth pathway only for people in private banking?

No. It suits anyone advising individuals: independent advisers, family offices and wealth platforms. It also helps if you plan to manage your own family's wealth, because the tax, estate and insurance material is useful in its own right.

Does the pathway change the exam format?

No. All three pathways share the same format, length and exam windows. Only the content of about a third of the questions differs.
