What goes into the cost of inventory

By Gowtham KumarLesson 14 of 50

Inventory carries every cost needed to get goods ready for sale, including a share of factory overheads based on normal capacity. Anything else is an expense of the period, and the choice moves profit between years.

If a cost was needed to make the goods ready for sale, it waits in inventory until they are sold. If not, it is an expense now.

From first principles

Inventory is a cost waiting to be matched with revenue. Costs that are part of getting goods into their present location and condition are capitalised (added to inventory) and become cost of sales only when the goods are sold. Other costs are expensed in the period they happen.

Included in inventory cost:

  • purchase price, import duties and non-refundable taxes, less trade discounts and rebates;
  • freight-in (transport to bring goods to the company);
  • conversion costs: direct labour and production overheads, both variable and fixed;
  • storage that is necessary in the production process, such as maturing wine or whisky.

Expensed as incurred:

  • abnormal waste of materials, labour or overheads;
  • storage of finished goods awaiting sale (unless it is part of production);
  • administrative overheads that do not help bring inventory to its condition;
  • selling costs, including freight-out to customers.

Fixed overhead and normal capacity

Fixed production overhead (factory rent, supervisors, machine depreciation) is spread over units on the basis of normal capacity, the output expected on average under normal conditions. If actual output is low, the unabsorbed part is expensed rather than loaded onto fewer units, so a quiet factory cannot make each unit look more expensive and park the idle cost in inventory. If output is abnormally high, the rate per unit is reduced so that inventory is not carried above actual cost.

Worked example 1: sorting the costs

A furniture maker spends 50,000 on wood, 30,000 on factory wages, 10,000 on factory overheads, 4,000 on wood wasted by a machine fault (abnormal) and 6,000 on showroom staff.

Inventory cost=50,000+30,000+10,000=90,000\text{Inventory cost} = 50{,}000 + 30{,}000 + 10{,}000 = 90{,}000Inventory cost=50,000+30,000+10,000=90,000

The 4,000 of abnormal waste and the 6,000 of selling cost are expenses of this period.

Worked example 2: a quiet year at the factory

Fixed production overhead is 100,000 a year and normal capacity is 10,000 units, so the overhead rate is 10 per unit. This year the factory makes only 8,000 units.

Overhead in inventory=8,000×10=80,000Unabsorbed, expensed now=20,000\text{Overhead in inventory} = 8{,}000 \times 10 = 80{,}000 \qquad \text{Unabsorbed, expensed now} = 20{,}000Overhead in inventory=8,000×10=80,000Unabsorbed, expensed now=20,000

Why it matters: if all 100,000 had gone into 8,000 units, each would carry 12.50 of overhead, inventory would be 20,000 higher and this year's profit 20,000 higher, with nothing real having improved.

How the exam tests it

  • Calculating inventory cost from a list of costs, separating capitalised from expensed items.
  • Normal capacity and unabsorbed overhead.
  • The effect of capitalising rather than expensing on profit, assets and ratios.

Exam traps

  • Freight-in is inventory; freight-out is a selling expense.
  • Abnormal waste is always expensed; normal waste is part of the cost of production.
  • Storage is capitalised only when it is part of production (maturing whisky), not while finished goods wait for a buyer.
  • Capitalising a cost raises this year's profit if the goods are still unsold at the year-end; the cost hits profit later when they are sold.

Questions people ask

Is shipping cost part of inventory?

Shipping to bring goods into the company (freight-in) is. Shipping finished goods to customers (freight-out) is a selling expense.

Why are abnormal costs excluded from inventory?

Because they did not add value to the goods. Loading them into inventory would hide inefficiency on the balance sheet and push the cost into a later year.

What is normal capacity?

The production a factory expects to achieve on average over several periods under normal conditions. It sets how much fixed overhead each unit carries, so idle capacity is expensed rather than hidden in inventory.
