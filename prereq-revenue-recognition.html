Revenue recognition in five steps

By Gowtham KumarLesson 4 of 50

Revenue is recognised when a promised good or service is delivered to the customer, at the price allocated to that promise. Bundles, long contracts, bonuses and marketplaces all follow from that one idea.

Find each promise in the contract, put a price on it, and count the revenue when that promise is kept.

From first principles

A customer pays because the seller promises something. Revenue therefore belongs to the moment each promise is kept, not the moment the contract is signed or the cash arrives. IFRS and US GAAP converged on the same five-step model:

  1. Identify the contract with the customer: an agreement that creates enforceable rights and obligations, with payment terms and commercial substance, where collection is probable.
  2. Identify the performance obligations: each distinct good or service promised. A good is distinct if the customer can benefit from it on its own (or with resources it can easily get) and it is separate from the other promises.
  3. Determine the transaction price: what the seller expects to receive. Variable consideration (rebates, bonuses, penalties, likely returns) is estimated, then constrained: it is included only to the extent that a significant reversal later is highly unlikely.
  4. Allocate the price to each obligation in proportion to its stand-alone selling price.
  5. Recognise revenue when (or as) each obligation is satisfied, which means when control passes to the customer, either at a point in time or over time.

At a point in time or over time?

Revenue is recognised over time when the customer receives the benefit as the work is done (a cleaning contract), controls the asset as it is built (work on the customer's own site), or the seller builds something with no alternative use and has an enforceable right to payment for work completed so far (a custom ship). Otherwise it is recognised at a point in time, usually on delivery.

Over-time revenue needs a measure of progress. A common one is costs incurred to date as a share of total expected costs:

Cumulative revenue=Contract price×Costs to dateTotal expected costs\text{Cumulative revenue} = \text{Contract price} \times \frac{\text{Costs to date}}{\text{Total expected costs}}Cumulative revenue=Contract price×Total expected costsCosts to date​

If the total expected costs exceed the contract price, the whole expected loss is recognised immediately, not spread over the remaining work.

Principal or agent?

A company that controls the good before it passes to the customer is the principal and reports the gross sale as revenue. A company that only arranges for someone else to supply it is an agent and reports only its commission. An online marketplace that sells a third party's concert ticket for 80 and keeps 8 reports revenue of 8, not 80.

Worked example 1: a bundle

A phone is sold with a two-year service plan for a single price of 1,000. Sold separately, the phone costs 600 and the service 600 (total 1,200). The 1,000 is allocated in proportion:

Phone=1,000×6001,200=500Service=500\text{Phone} = 1{,}000 \times \tfrac{600}{1{,}200} = 500 \qquad \text{Service} = 500Phone=1,000×1,200600​=500Service=500

The phone's 500 is recognised on delivery. The service's 500 is recognised evenly, about 20.8 a month for 24 months.

Cumulative profit is 6.67−6=0.676.67 - 6 = 0.676.67−6=0.67, so year-2 profit is only 0.67−0.50.67 - 0.50.67−0.5 = 0.17 million. The change in estimate is absorbed in the current and future years; year 1 is not restated.

Had the estimate risen to 10.5 million instead, the contract would be loss-making by 0.5 million. The full loss is recognised at once: cumulative profit must become −0.5, so year 2 shows a loss of 1.0 million (reversing year 1's 0.5 profit and adding the 0.5 loss).

How the exam tests it

  • Allocating a bundle price and saying how much revenue is recognised now.
  • Cost-to-cost progress on a long contract, including a change in estimate or an expected loss.
  • Principal versus agent, and the constraint on variable consideration.

Exam traps

  • Bundles are split: one price, several obligations, several timings.
  • Variable consideration is constrained: a 60% chance of a bonus is usually not enough to include it.
  • Changes in estimate are prospective: earlier years are not restated.
  • Expected losses are recognised immediately, while expected profits are spread over the work.
  • Cash and revenue differ: payments received ahead of performance are a contract liability; performance ahead of the right to invoice is a contract asset.

Questions people ask

What are the five steps of revenue recognition?

Find the contract, list the separate promises in it, set the total price, split that price across the promises by their stand-alone selling prices, and book each slice as the promise is fulfilled, at a point in time or over time.

When is revenue recognised over time rather than at a point?

When the customer gets the benefit as the work proceeds, controls the asset while it is being built, or the seller builds something with no alternative use and has a right to be paid for work done so far. Long construction contracts are the typical case.

How do analysts spot aggressive revenue recognition?

Watch receivables growing faster than sales, unusual end-of-quarter spikes, changes in estimates of contract progress and a rise in unbilled revenue (contract assets). Each means revenue is running ahead of cash.
