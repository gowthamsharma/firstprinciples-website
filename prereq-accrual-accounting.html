Accrual accounting: when revenue and expenses count

By Gowtham KumarLesson 3 of 50

Revenue is recorded when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, not when cash moves. The gaps between the two become balance sheet items, and forgetting one misstates both profit and the balance sheet.

Accruals answer "what did we earn this period?", not "what cash came in?"

From first principles

If profit followed cash, a company that pre-sold a year of subscriptions in January would show a huge January profit and eleven months of losses. That picture would mislead anyone using it to judge performance. So accounting records revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, and matches expenses to the revenue they helped produce or to the period they belong to.

Whenever cash and recognition fall in different periods, the difference has to sit somewhere until they catch up. It sits on the balance sheet:

  • Cash received before revenue is earned: unearned (deferred) revenue, a liability, because the company still owes the customer the service.
  • Revenue earned before cash arrives: accrued revenue, an asset (a receivable).
  • Cash paid before the expense is incurred: prepaid expense, an asset, because the company has paid for a benefit it has not used yet.
  • Expense incurred before cash is paid: accrued expense, a liability, because the company owes for something it has already used.

Adjusting entries

During the year a company records what its systems see: invoices, payments, receipts. At the period end it makes adjusting entries to bring the accounts in line with what was actually earned and used: a month of rent used up from a prepayment, wages earned by staff but not yet paid, interest that has built up on a loan, revenue earned but not yet invoiced. Each adjustment moves one income-statement line and one balance-sheet line together.

That pairing gives you a fast way to reason about errors. If an adjusting entry is forgotten, both statements are wrong, in a predictable direction.

Forgotten adjustment Net income Balance sheet
Accrued wages not recorded overstated liabilities understated
Prepaid rent used up but still shown as an asset overstated assets overstated
Revenue earned but not invoiced understated assets understated
Customer prepayment recorded as revenue too early overstated liabilities understated

Worked example 1: a subscription paid in advance

A software firm receives 1,200 on 1 October for a 12-month licence. At its 31 December year-end it has delivered 3 months:

Revenue=1,200×312=300Unearned revenue=1,200−300=900\text{Revenue} = 1{,}200 \times \tfrac{3}{12} = 300 \qquad \text{Unearned revenue} = 1{,}200 - 300 = 900Revenue=1,200×123​=300Unearned revenue=1,200−300=900

The 900 is a liability: the firm owes nine more months of service. The cash flow statement still shows the full 1,200 inflow this year.

Worked example 2: two adjustments at year-end

A company with a 31 December year-end has two items to adjust.

Insurance. It paid 2,400 on 1 April for 12 months of cover. By 31 December it has used 9 months:

Insurance expense=2,400×912=1,800Prepaid insurance=600\text{Insurance expense} = 2{,}400 \times \tfrac{9}{12} = 1{,}800 \qquad \text{Prepaid insurance} = 600Insurance expense=2,400×129​=1,800Prepaid insurance=600

Interest. It borrowed 10,000 at 6% a year on 1 November, with interest paid once a year on 31 October. Two months of interest have built up but nothing has been paid:

Accrued interest=10,000×6%×212=100\text{Accrued interest} = 10{,}000 \times 6\% \times \tfrac{2}{12} = 100Accrued interest=10,000×6%×122​=100

So the year's profit carries 1,800 of insurance and 100 of interest expense, the balance sheet shows a 600 asset and a 100 liability, and only the 2,400 insurance payment appears in this year's cash flow.

How the exam tests it

  • Calculating a prepaid or accrued balance from dates and amounts (watch which months fall inside the year).
  • Saying how a missing adjustment misstates net income, assets and liabilities.
  • Reading a gap between profit and operating cash flow as a sign of growing accruals.

Exam traps

  • Unearned revenue is a liability, not revenue. Rising unearned revenue is often good news: customers are paying in advance.
  • Count the months carefully. Cover bought on 1 April and a 31 December year-end means 9 months used, not 8 or 12.
  • Accruals reverse. A large accrued revenue balance today means cash should arrive soon; if it does not, revenue may have been booked too early.
  • Accrual profit and cash profit are equal over the whole life of a business; accruals only change the timing. Persistent large gaps are therefore a warning sign.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between cash and accrual accounting?

Cash accounting records money when it moves. Accrual accounting records revenue when it is earned and costs when they are used up, whatever the timing of the payment. Listed companies must use accruals, because cash timing alone hides performance.

What are the four types of accruals?

Cash received before the work (unearned revenue), work done before the cash (accrued revenue), cash paid before the cost is used (prepaid expense) and cost used before it is paid (accrued expense). Each one parks a timing difference on the balance sheet.

Why do analysts watch accruals?

Because they rest on estimates. When profit grows much faster than operating cash flow year after year, the gap is made of accruals, and large accruals have historically been followed by weaker earnings.
