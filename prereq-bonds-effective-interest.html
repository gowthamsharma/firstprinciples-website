Bonds payable and the effective interest method

A bond is recorded at what investors paid for it. Interest expense is the market rate at issue times the carrying amount, and the gap with the coupon walks the carrying amount to face value.

Concept 23 of 50 · Financial Statement Analysis · Non-current liabilities · 2 min read

Interest expense = market rate at issue × carrying amount. Coupon paid = coupon rate × face value. The difference amortises the discount or premium.

From first principles

When a company issues a bond, investors pay the present value of its future coupons and principal, discounted at the market rate at that moment.

  • Coupon below the market rate → investors pay less than face value: a discount.
  • Coupon above the market rate → a premium.

The liability is recorded at that price. Its true cost of borrowing is the market rate at issue, so under the effective interest method interest expense each period = market rate × carrying amount. The coupon is fixed. The difference changes the carrying amount, which walks steadily to face value by maturity.

Worked example

Face 1,000, 5 years, 5% annual coupon, market rate 7%. Price = 918.0. Year 1: interest expense = 7% × 918.0 = 64.3; coupon 50; carrying amount rises by 14.3 to 932.3. By year 5 it reaches 1,000.

Exam traps

  • Discount bonds: interest expense > coupon, rising over time. Premium bonds: expense < coupon, falling.
  • Cash flow: the coupon is the cash; the amortisation is non-cash and is added back in the indirect method. The proceeds at issue and the principal repaid are financing cash flows.
  • Market rate changes after issue do not change the carrying amount under amortised cost. Only bonds measured at fair value move with rates.
  • Issuance costs reduce the carrying amount of the liability under both IFRS and US GAAP.

Why it matters at Level III

Book values of debt can sit far from market values when rates move, which matters for leverage measures and for liability-driven investing.
