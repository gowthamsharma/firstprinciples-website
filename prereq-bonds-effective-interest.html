Bonds payable and the effective interest method

By Gowtham KumarLesson 23 of 50

A bond is recorded at what investors paid for it. Interest expense is the market rate at issue times the carrying amount, and the gap with the coupon walks the carrying amount to face value.

Interest expense=rmarket at issue×carrying amount,coupon=rcoupon×face value\text{Interest expense} = r_{\text{market at issue}} \times \text{carrying amount},\qquad \text{coupon} = r_{\text{coupon}} \times \text{face value}Interest expense=rmarket at issue​×carrying amount,coupon=rcoupon​×face value

The difference amortises the discount or premium.

From first principles

When a company issues a bond, investors hand over what its coupons and final repayment are worth today, using the market rate on the day of issue to discount them.

  • Coupon below the market rate → investors pay less than face value: a discount.
  • Coupon above the market rate → a premium.

The liability is recorded at that price. Its true cost of borrowing is the market rate at issue, so under the effective interest method interest expenset=market rate×carrying amountt−1\text{interest expense}_t = \text{market rate} \times \text{carrying amount}_{t-1}interest expenset​=market rate×carrying amountt−1​. The coupon is fixed. The difference changes the carrying amount, which walks steadily to face value by maturity.

Worked example

Face 1,000, 5 years, 5% annual coupon, market rate 7%. Price = 918.0. Year 1: interest expense = 7%×918.0=64.37\% \times 918.0 = 64.37%×918.0=64.3; coupon 50; carrying amount rises by 14.3 to 932.3. By year 5 it reaches 1,000.

Exam traps

  • Discount bonds: interest expense > coupon, rising over time. Premium bonds: expense < coupon, falling.
  • Cash flow: the coupon is the cash; the amortisation is non-cash and is added back in the indirect method. The proceeds at issue and the principal repaid are financing cash flows.
  • Once issued, the book value ignores market rates under amortised cost. Only bonds measured at fair value move with rates.
  • Costs of issuing the bond are netted against the debt on day one, under IFRS and US GAAP alike.

Questions people ask

What is the effective interest method?

Interest expense each period equals the bond's carrying amount at the start of the period times the market rate at issue. The difference from the cash coupon adjusts the carrying amount toward face value.

Why is interest expense different from the coupon paid?

Because the bond was sold at a discount or premium. A discount means investors receive part of their return at maturity, so expense exceeds the coupon; a premium works the other way.

Where does the coupon go in the cash flow statement?

Under US GAAP, interest paid is operating. Under IFRS it can be operating or financing. The money raised at issue and repaid at maturity is always financing.
