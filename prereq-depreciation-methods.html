Depreciation methods and why they matter

Depreciation spreads the cost of a long-lived asset over the years it helps earn revenue. The method changes the timing of profit, never the total.

2 min read  /  By Gowtham Kumar

Depreciation does not measure loss of value. It allocates a cost over the years that benefit from it.

From first principles

A machine bought for 10,000 helps produce revenue for five years. Expensing the whole 10,000 in year one would make year one look terrible and years two to five look too good. So the cost (less the expected salvage value at the end) is spread over the useful life.

How to spread it depends on how the asset's benefit is consumed:

  • Straight-line: equal each year, (cost − salvage) / life. Good when the benefit is even.
  • Accelerated (double-declining balance): a fixed rate of 2 / life applied to the remaining book value, so early years carry more. Stop when book value reaches salvage. Good when assets are most productive (or most quickly obsolete) when new.
  • Units-of-production: in proportion to use. Good for assets that wear out with use.

Try them on the same machine:

Worked example

Cost 10,000, salvage 1,000, 5 years. Straight-line: 1,800 a year. Double-declining at 40%: 4,000, 2,400, 1,440, 864, then 296 to reach 1,000 salvage. Total is 9,000 either way.

Exam traps

  • Accelerated depreciation lowers early profit and asset values, raising early asset turnover and lowering early return on assets.
  • Cash flow is unaffected (except through tax). Depreciation is added back in the indirect method.
  • Changes in estimates (life or salvage) are applied going forward, not restated.
  • IFRS requires component depreciation: significant parts with different lives are depreciated separately.

Why it matters at Level III

Comparing two firms with different methods or useful lives without adjusting can make the more conservative firm look less profitable. The ratio of accumulated depreciation to gross PP&E also shows how old a firm's assets are.
