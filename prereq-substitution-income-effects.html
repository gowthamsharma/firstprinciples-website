Substitution and income effects

A price change does two things at once: it makes a good relatively cheaper, and it changes how rich the buyer feels. Separating them explains odd demand behaviour.

Concept 29 of 50 · Economics · Demand and supply analysis · 2 min read

Substitution effect: buy more of what got relatively cheaper. Income effect: your money now goes further.

From first principles

When the price of a good falls, two separate things happen to a buyer:

  1. Substitution effect. The good is now cheaper relative to other goods, so the buyer shifts towards it. This effect always points the same way: lower price, more demand.
  2. Income effect. The buyer's money now buys more overall: real income has risen. What they do with it depends on the good: - normal good: they buy more, which reinforces the substitution effect; - inferior good: they buy less, which works against it.

For almost all goods, the substitution effect wins and demand slopes down. In the theoretical case of an inferior good whose income effect outweighs the substitution effect, demand would rise with price.

Worked example

Bus fares fall 20%. Substitution: some people switch from driving to the bus. Income: commuters who already took the bus have money left over. If bus travel is inferior for them (they would rather drive once richer), some spend the saving on a car. Total effect: more bus travel, but less than the substitution effect alone implies.

Exam traps

  • The substitution effect is always opposite to the price change.
  • Inferior does not mean upward-sloping demand. It only weakens the downward slope, unless the income effect is larger.

Why it matters at Level III

The same two-channel logic appears in consumer behaviour across the business cycle, and in how households rebalance spending when energy or food prices spike.
