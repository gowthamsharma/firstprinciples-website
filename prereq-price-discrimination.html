Consumer surplus and price discrimination

By Gowtham KumarLesson 36 of 50

Most buyers would pay more than the market price. Firms that can charge different buyers different prices turn that surplus into revenue.

Consumer surplus=most a buyer would pay−price paid\text{Consumer surplus} = \text{most a buyer would pay} - \text{price paid}Consumer surplus=most a buyer would pay−price paid

From first principles

The demand curve can be read in two directions. Read across, it tells you how many units are bought at each price. Read up, it tells you how much the buyer of each unit was willing to pay for it. Almost everyone in the market would have paid more than the price they actually paid.

Consumer surplus is that gap, added up over every unit bought. On a chart it is the area below the demand curve and above the price.

Producer surplus is the mirror image: the gap between the price received and the lowest price the seller would have accepted (the supply curve, or marginal cost). It is the area above supply and below the price.

For a straight-line demand curve, consumer surplus is a triangle:

CS=12×Q×(Pmax⁡−P)CS = \tfrac{1}{2} \times Q \times (P_{\max} - P)CS=21​×Q×(Pmax​−P)

where Pmax⁡P_{\max}Pmax​ is the price at which demand falls to zero.

Price discrimination

A firm with pricing power could earn more if it charged each buyer closer to what that buyer will pay. That is price discrimination: charging different prices for the same good, for reasons unrelated to cost. It needs three conditions:

  1. Pricing power: a downward-sloping demand curve.
  2. A way to tell buyers apart with different willingness to pay (age, timing, location, quantity).
  3. No resale: cheap buyers must not be able to resell to dear ones.

There are three degrees:

What the firm does Example Consumer surplus
First degree (perfect) Charges every buyer their maximum price An auction; a negotiated contract All captured by the seller
Second degree Offers prices that vary by quantity or version, and buyers choose Bulk discounts, "economy" and "premium" tiers Partly captured
Third degree Charges different groups different prices Student and senior discounts; peak and off-peak fares Partly captured

Under perfect price discrimination, the firm sells to every buyer who values the good above marginal cost, so output reaches the competitive level and the deadweight loss disappears. The efficiency gain all goes to the seller.

Worked example 1: surplus at a single price

Demand is P=100−QP = 100 - QP=100−Q, and the price is 40, so 60 units are bought.

CS=12×60×(100−40)=1,800CS = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 60 \times (100 - 40) = 1{,}800CS=21​×60×(100−40)=1,800

If marginal cost is 10, producer surplus at that price is the rectangle (40−10)×60=1,800(40 - 10) \times 60 = 1{,}800(40−10)×60=1,800. A perfect price discriminator selling all 90 units for which buyers' value exceeds MC would capture 12×90×90=4,050\tfrac{1}{2} \times 90 \times 90 = 4{,}05021​×90×90=4,050 in total, leaving buyers nothing.

Worked example 2: third-degree price discrimination

A firm sells in two markets that cannot trade with each other. Marginal cost is 10 in both. The rule: set MR = MC in each market separately.

Market A: P=50−QP = 50 - QP=50−Q, so MR=50−2QMR = 50 - 2QMR=50−2Q.

50−2Q=10  ⇒  QA=20,PA=3050 - 2Q = 10 \;\Rightarrow\; Q_A = 20, \qquad P_A = 3050−2Q=10⇒QA​=20,PA​=30

Market B: P=30−QP = 30 - QP=30−Q, so MR=30−2QMR = 30 - 2QMR=30−2Q.

30−2Q=10  ⇒  QB=10,PB=2030 - 2Q = 10 \;\Rightarrow\; Q_B = 10, \qquad P_B = 2030−2Q=10⇒QB​=10,PB​=20

Profit is (30−10)×20+(20−10)×10=400+100=500(30 - 10) \times 20 + (20 - 10) \times 10 = 400 + 100 = 500(30−10)×20+(20−10)×10=400+100=500.

The lower price goes to the market with the more elastic demand. At the chosen points, elasticity in A is 30/20=1.530/20 = 1.530/20=1.5; in B it is 20/10=2.020/10 = 2.020/10=2.0. Buyers in B have more alternatives, so the firm charges them less. That is why students, who are price-sensitive, get discounts and business travellers, who are not, pay full fares.

Exam traps

  • Price differences that reflect cost differences are not price discrimination. Delivering to a remote area costs more.
  • The less elastic group pays more.
  • Perfect price discrimination removes the deadweight loss, but consumers lose all their surplus.
  • Without a way to stop resale, price discrimination collapses: buyers in the cheap market resell to the dear one.

Questions people ask

What is consumer surplus?

The gap between the most a buyer would pay and the price actually paid, summed across all buyers. On a demand chart it is the triangle above the price line.

What are the three types of price discrimination?

Charging each buyer their maximum (first degree), offering different prices by quantity or version (second degree) and charging different groups different prices, such as student discounts (third degree).

What does a firm need to price discriminate?

Some pricing power, a way to tell buyer groups apart and a way to stop cheap buyers reselling to dear ones. Airlines, cinemas and software firms all meet these conditions.
