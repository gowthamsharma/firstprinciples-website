Credit analysis with financial statements

By Gowtham KumarLesson 25 of 50

Lenders ask two questions: can the borrower pay interest from what it earns, and how much debt is it carrying relative to what it generates? Coverage and leverage ratios answer them, inside a broader view of the borrower known as the four Cs.

A lender's upside is getting its money back with interest. Credit analysis is about the downside: what could stop that happening?

From first principles

A shareholder benefits from everything going right; a lender only needs things not to go badly wrong. So credit analysis focuses on the capacity to pay under stress, and on what happens if payment fails. A traditional checklist is the four Cs:

  • Capacity: can operating cash flow cover interest and principal? This is where the financial statements do most of the work.
  • Collateral: what assets back the debt, and what would they fetch?
  • Covenants: the terms that limit what the borrower can do: maximum leverage, minimum coverage, restrictions on dividends and further borrowing.
  • Character: management's track record and integrity.

The ratios

Coverage measures how many times earnings or cash flow cover interest:

Interest coverage=EBITInterest expenseEBITDA coverage=EBITDAInterest expense\text{Interest coverage} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest expense}} \qquad \text{EBITDA coverage} = \frac{\text{EBITDA}}{\text{Interest expense}}Interest coverage=Interest expenseEBIT​EBITDA coverage=Interest expenseEBITDA​

Leverage measures debt relative to earnings, cash flow or capital:

DebtEBITDAFFODebtDebtDebt+Equity\frac{\text{Debt}}{\text{EBITDA}} \qquad \frac{\text{FFO}}{\text{Debt}} \qquad \frac{\text{Debt}}{\text{Debt} + \text{Equity}}EBITDADebt​DebtFFO​Debt+EquityDebt​

where FFO (funds from operations) is net income plus depreciation, amortisation and other non-cash items. Debt to EBITDA reads as "years of earnings to repay the debt". Rating agencies adjust these numbers, adding items such as pension deficits and lease obligations to debt, so that companies are compared on the same basis.

Worked example 1: a comfortable borrower

EBIT 120, interest 10, debt 250, EBITDA 160.

If EBIT fell by 40%, to 72, coverage would still be 7.2×. A lender would be comfortable.

B has less room on every measure. A 40% fall in EBIT would take its coverage to 1.8×, close to the point where it cannot pay interest from earnings at all. A lender would want higher interest, collateral or tighter covenants from B.

Beyond the ratios

  • Business risk comes first: stable, diversified businesses can carry more debt than cyclical ones.
  • Trends matter more than levels: rising leverage and falling coverage deserve more attention than a single weak year.
  • Seniority and security decide recovery: secured lenders are repaid before unsecured ones in a default.
  • Liquidity keeps a company alive in the short run: cash, undrawn credit lines and the maturity schedule.

Exam traps

  • Higher coverage is better; lower debt to EBITDA is better. FFO to debt is the reverse of debt to EBITDA: higher is better.
  • Ratios are only as good as the debt figure: include leases and other debt-like items consistently.
  • EBITDA is not cash flow: it ignores working capital, taxes and capital spending.
  • Equity holders and lenders want different things: a riskier strategy can help shareholders and hurt bondholders.

Questions people ask

What are the four Cs of credit?

Capacity (the ability to pay from cash flow), collateral (assets securing the debt), covenants (the terms of the loan) and character (management's reliability). Capacity is where financial statement analysis does most of the work.

What is a good debt to EBITDA ratio?

It depends on the industry and the stability of earnings. Stable businesses can carry higher multiples; cyclical ones need lower. Compare with peers and with the company's own history and covenant limits.

Why do credit analysts adjust reported debt?

To put companies on the same footing. Lease obligations, pension deficits and other commitments behave like debt, so agencies add them to reported borrowing before calculating leverage and coverage.

Analyst adjustments for comparability

Reported numbers follow each company's standards and choices. Before comparing companies, put them on the same basis: inventory, goodwill, depreciation, capitalised costs and leases are the usual suspects.

Price elasticity of demand and total revenue

Elasticity measures how strongly buyers react to a price change. It decides whether a price rise raises or lowers a seller's revenue, and it changes along a straight-line demand curve.

Income and cross-price elasticity

How demand reacts to income separates luxuries, necessities and inferior goods. How it reacts to another good's price separates substitutes from complements. Both come straight out of a demand equation.
