Marginal returns and productivity

With some inputs fixed, adding more of a variable input eventually adds less and less output. That single fact shapes every cost curve.

Concept 30 of 50 · Economics · Demand and supply analysis · 2 min read

Diminishing marginal returns: in the short run, the extra output from each extra worker eventually falls.

From first principles

In the short run, a firm can hire more workers but cannot instantly build more factory space. Picture a kitchen with two stoves: the first cooks are very productive, the third and fourth less so as they queue for the stoves, and the tenth mostly gets in the way.

Three measures describe it:

  • Total product (TP): total output.
  • Average product (AP): output per worker, TP / L.
  • Marginal product (MP): extra output from one more worker, ΔTP / ΔL.

Once fixed inputs become a bottleneck, MP falls. TP keeps rising, but more slowly; if MP turns negative, TP falls.

Worked example

Workers Total output Marginal product
1 20 20
2 45 25
3 63 18
4 75 12
5 80 5

Diminishing returns start with the third worker.

Exam traps

  • Diminishing returns is a short-run idea (some inputs fixed). Returns to scale is a long-run idea (all inputs change together).
  • MP crosses AP at AP's maximum: when the marginal worker is above average, the average rises.

Why it matters at Level III

Because marginal product falls, marginal cost rises. The same logic, applied to capital in a whole economy, explains why long-run growth needs technology, not just more machines (see economic growth).
