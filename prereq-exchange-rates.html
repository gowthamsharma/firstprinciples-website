Exchange rates: quotes and real exchange rates

By Gowtham KumarLesson 49 of 50

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another. What matters for trade is the real exchange rate, which adjusts for prices in each country.

Real rated/f=Sd/f×PfPd\text{Real rate}_{d/f} = S_{d/f} \times \frac{P_f}{P_d}Real rated/f​=Sd/f​×Pd​Pf​​

The real exchange rate is the nominal rate adjusted for the two price levels.

From first principles

Reading a quote

The convention here is price currency / base currency: USD/EUR = 1.10 means one euro (the base) costs 1.10 dollars (the price currency). If it rises to 1.15, the euro has appreciated and the dollar has depreciated.

Percentage changes are not symmetric:

euro: 1.151.10−1=+4.55%dollar: 1.101.15−1=−4.35%\text{euro: } \frac{1.15}{1.10} - 1 = +4.55\% \qquad \text{dollar: } \frac{1.10}{1.15} - 1 = -4.35\%euro: 1.101.15​−1=+4.55%dollar: 1.151.10​−1=−4.35%

To measure the change for the price currency, invert the quote first.

Cross rates

If you know each currency against a third, you can find the rate between them. Multiply so that the common currency cancels:

JPYEUR=JPYUSD×USDEUR\frac{\text{JPY}}{\text{EUR}} = \frac{\text{JPY}}{\text{USD}} \times \frac{\text{USD}}{\text{EUR}}EURJPY​=USDJPY​×EURUSD​

Why real rates matter

A tourist does not care what a euro costs; they care what a euro buys. The real exchange rate adjusts the nominal rate for each country's price level, measuring the relative price of goods. With the domestic currency as the price currency:

Real rated/f=Sd/f×PfPd\text{Real rate}_{d/f} = S_{d/f} \times \frac{P_f}{P_d}Real rated/f​=Sd/f​×Pd​Pf​​

  • If the real rate rises, foreign goods have become dearer relative to domestic ones: the domestic currency has depreciated in real terms, and domestic producers have become more competitive.
  • If it falls, the domestic currency has appreciated in real terms, and competitiveness has worsened.

In percentage terms:

%ΔReal rated/f≈%ΔSd/f+πf−πd\%\Delta \text{Real rate}_{d/f} \approx \%\Delta S_{d/f} + \pi_f - \pi_d%ΔReal rated/f​≈%ΔSd/f​+πf​−πd​

Worked example 1: real appreciation with no nominal change

Take the dollar as domestic. The nominal rate USD/EUR stays at 1.10 for a year. Euro-area prices rise 5%; US prices rise 1%.

Real rateUSD/EUR=1.10×1.051.01=1.144\text{Real rate}_{USD/EUR} = 1.10 \times \frac{1.05}{1.01} = 1.144Real rateUSD/EUR​=1.10×1.011.05​=1.144

The real rate rose about 4% (5%−1%5\% - 1\%5%−1%). Euro-area goods have become about 4% dearer for Americans, even though the quote never moved: US producers gained competitiveness, euro-area producers lost it.

Worked example 2: a cross rate

USD/EUR = 1.10 and JPY/USD = 150.

JPY/EUR=150×1.10=165\text{JPY/EUR} = 150 \times 1.10 = 165JPY/EUR=150×1.10=165

One euro buys 165 yen. Check the units: (yen per dollar) × (dollars per euro) = yen per euro.

Why analysts care

A currency can look stable in nominal terms while its real value drifts far from fair value, slowly eroding exporters' margins. Persistent real appreciation is often a warning sign for a country's trade balance.

Exam traps

  • Check the base currency before deciding which currency appreciated. In USD/EUR, the euro is the base.
  • Percentage changes are not symmetric across the two currencies.
  • A higher real exchange rate (domestic/foreign) means the domestic currency buys fewer foreign goods: domestic competitiveness improves.
  • For cross rates, make the common currency cancel, rather than dividing by habit.

Questions people ask

How do you read an exchange rate quote?

A quote of EUR/USD 1.10 means one euro costs 1.10 dollars. The first currency is the one being priced; the second is the one you pay with.

What is the real exchange rate?

The nominal rate adjusted for the two countries' price levels. It shows how many foreign goods your domestic goods can buy, which is what matters for trade.

Why can a currency lose competitiveness without moving?

If domestic inflation runs ahead of inflation abroad, the real exchange rate rises even when the nominal rate is flat, making exports dearer.
