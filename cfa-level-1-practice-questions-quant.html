CFA Level I practice questions: Quantitative methods, with worked answers

By Gowtham KumarPractice questions

Ten original quantitative methods questions written like the real Level I exam: short cases with data, three options each, and worked explanations. Time value of money, statistics, probability, hypothesis tests and regression. Quantitative methods carries 11–14% of the exam from 2027.

Quantitative methods carries 11–14% of Level I from 2027, as much as fixed income or equities. Most questions are either a calculation you should do in under ninety seconds on the BA II Plus, or a definition you need to state exactly (a Type I error, a p-value, what R² measures).

Topic weight from 2027: 11–14% of Level I, about 20 to 25 of the 180 questions.

10 questions · Level I format · about 15 to 20 minutes

Choose an answer to see the explanation.

  1. Ines Moreau is comparing three savings accounts. Each pays interest at a different stated annual rate and compounding frequency:

    BankStated annual rateCompounding
    Bank A6.00%Monthly
    Bank B6.10%Semi-annually
    Bank C6.05%Quarterly

    The account with the highest effective annual rate is offered by:

    Answer: B.

    Effective annual rate = (1 + stated rate / m)^m − 1, where m is the number of compounding periods per year.

    Bank A: (1 + 0.06/12)^12 − 1 = 6.168%. Bank B: (1 + 0.061/2)^2 − 1 = 6.193%. Bank C: (1 + 0.0605/4)^4 − 1 = 6.189%.

    Bank B wins narrowly. More frequent compounding helps, but here it does not make up for Bank A's lower stated rate, and Bank C's extra compounding falls just short of Bank B's higher rate.

  2. Kwame Asante, aged 45, plans to save for retirement by depositing €5,000 into an account at the beginning of each year for the next 10 years, starting today. The account earns 5% a year, compounded annually.

    The value of the account at the end of the tenth year is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Deposits at the start of each period form an annuity due. FV of an ordinary annuity = 5,000 × [(1.05^10 − 1) / 0.05] = 62,889. Because each deposit earns one extra year of interest, multiply by 1.05: 62,889 × 1.05 = 66,034. On the BA II Plus, set BGN mode: N = 10, I/Y = 5, PV = 0, PMT = −5,000, CPT FV = 66,034.

    €62,889 treats the deposits as made at the end of each year. €81,445 is what €50,000 would grow to if it were all deposited today (50,000 × 1.05^10).

  3. An investor buys one share of Nordic Timber at €100 at the start of year 1. At the end of year 1 the price is €120 and she buys a second share. At the end of year 2 the price is €110 and she sells both shares. The stock pays no dividends.

    The investor's annualised time-weighted and money-weighted returns are closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Time-weighted return: link the holding-period returns. Year 1: 120/100 − 1 = 20%. Year 2: 110/120 − 1 = −8.33%. Annualised: (1.20 × 0.9167)^(1/2) − 1 = 4.88%.

    Money-weighted return: find the IRR of the investor's cash flows: −100 at time 0, −120 at time 1 and +220 at time 2. At r = 0, −100 − 120 + 220 = 0, so the IRR is 0.00%.

    The money-weighted return is lower because she invested more money just before the weaker year. The time-weighted return ignores the timing of her deposit, which is why it is used to judge managers.

  4. A pension fund requires a minimum return of 3% next year. An analyst estimates the following for three portfolios, whose returns are approximately normally distributed:

    PortfolioExpected returnStandard deviation
    A8.0%12%
    B6.5%7%
    C10.0%18%

    Using Roy's safety-first criterion, the fund should choose:

    Answer: A.

    Roy's safety-first ratio = (expected return − threshold return) / standard deviation. The highest ratio gives the lowest probability of falling below the threshold.

    A: (8.0 − 3) / 12 = 0.417. B: (6.5 − 3) / 7 = 0.500. C: (10.0 − 3) / 18 = 0.389.

    Portfolio B has the lowest expected return but the best safety-first ratio. Portfolio C has the highest expected return, but its volatility gives it the greatest chance of a shortfall.

  5. Mei Lin analyses the monthly returns of a hedge fund over ten years and finds:

    Mean0.6%
    Median0.9%
    Skewness−0.8
    Excess kurtosis2.4

    Compared with a normal distribution with the same mean and standard deviation, the fund's return distribution most likely has:

    Answer: A.

    Negative skewness (with the mean below the median) means the left tail is longer: most months are slightly positive, but occasional months bring large losses. Positive excess kurtosis (2.4 above the normal distribution's 3) means fatter tails, so extreme outcomes happen more often than a normal distribution implies.

    Together they point to more frequent large losses. A long right tail would require positive skewness, and fewer extreme outcomes would require negative excess kurtosis.

  6. An analyst tests whether a trading strategy earns a positive mean monthly return. Over 36 months the strategy's sample mean return is 0.90% and the sample standard deviation is 2.40%. She sets the null hypothesis that the mean return is less than or equal to zero, and uses a 5% significance level. The one-tailed critical t-value for 35 degrees of freedom is 1.690.

    The most appropriate conclusion is to:

    Answer: A.

    t = (sample mean − hypothesised mean) / (s / √n) = (0.90 − 0) / (2.40 / √36) = 0.90 / 0.40 = 2.25. Since 2.25 > 1.690, reject the null: the evidence supports a positive mean return at the 5% level.

    0.38 divides the mean by the standard deviation rather than by the standard error. 2.724 is roughly the two-tailed 1% critical value, which does not match a one-tailed 5% test.

  7. A regression of a fund's monthly excess returns on the market's monthly excess returns uses 32 observations. The ANOVA table shows:

    SourceSum of squares
    Regression0.0360
    Residual (error)0.0240

    The standard error of estimate of the regression is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Standard error of estimate = √[SSE / (n − 2)] = √(0.0240 / 30) = √0.0008 = 0.0283. It measures the typical size of the residuals.

    0.0274 divides by n = 32 instead of n − 2. 0.0447 uses the total sum of squares (0.0600) instead of the residual sum of squares. As a by-product, R² = 0.0360 / 0.0600 = 0.60, and F = (0.0360 / 1) / 0.0008 = 45.

  8. A bank's credit model flags loan applicants as high risk. Historically, 6% of borrowers default. The model flags 80% of borrowers who later default, and it also flags 10% of borrowers who do not default.

    If an applicant is flagged as high risk, the probability that the applicant will default is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Bayes' formula: P(default | flagged) = P(flagged | default) × P(default) / P(flagged).

    P(flagged) = 0.80 × 0.06 + 0.10 × 0.94 = 0.048 + 0.094 = 0.142. So P(default | flagged) = 0.048 / 0.142 = 33.8%.

    80% is the probability of being flagged given default, the reverse condition. 4.8% is the joint probability of default and being flagged. Most flagged applicants do not default because defaulters are rare to begin with.

  9. An analyst models the annual returns of two stocks, X and Y, under three economic scenarios:

    ScenarioProbabilityReturn on XReturn on Y
    Boom0.2520%15%
    Normal0.5010%8%
    Recession0.25−5%2%

    The correlation between the returns of X and Y is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    Expected returns: E(X) = 0.25(20) + 0.5(10) + 0.25(−5) = 8.75%; E(Y) = 0.25(15) + 0.5(8) + 0.25(2) = 8.25%.

    Covariance = Σ p × (X − E(X)) × (Y − E(Y)) = 0.25(11.25)(6.75) + 0.5(1.25)(−0.25) + 0.25(−13.75)(−6.25) = 40.31 (%²).

    Standard deviations: σX = √79.69 = 8.93%; σY = √21.19 = 4.60%. Correlation = 40.31 / (8.93 × 4.60) = 0.98.

    40.31 is the covariance, not the correlation. 0.51 is covariance divided by the variance of X, which is the slope (beta) of Y on X.

  10. A researcher studies a random sample of 49 European mid-cap funds. Their mean annual return is 8.0% and the standard deviation of returns across funds is 14.0%. Assume the sample is large enough to use the z-distribution.

    The 95% confidence interval for the population mean annual return is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Standard error = s / √n = 14 / √49 = 2.0%. A 95% interval uses z = 1.96: 8.0 ± 1.96 × 2.0 = 4.08% to 11.92%.

    4.71% to 11.29% uses z = 1.645, which gives a 90% interval. −19.44% to 35.44% uses the standard deviation instead of the standard error, which describes where individual funds lie, not where the mean lies.

Three things to remember for quantitative methods

  • Enter cash flows with signs: money you pay is negative.
  • Know which mean the question wants: arithmetic for one period ahead, geometric for what was actually earned.
  • For probability questions, write the tree before you multiply.

How to use these questions

Answer each question before you look at the explanation, and time yourself: the real exam allows about 90 seconds per question. Mark every question you got right for the wrong reason as wrong. When you miss one, write down the idea behind it rather than the answer, and come back to the page in a week.

These questions are written by First Principles to match the style and level of the exam. They are not taken from CFA Institute's curriculum or mock exams, which you should also use: they are included with your exam registration.

Questions people ask

Are these real CFA exam questions?

No. CFA Institute does not publish past exam questions. These are original questions written to match the Level I format: a short case, often with a table of data, followed by three answer options. Each explanation shows the working and why the two wrong options are wrong.

How many quantitative methods questions are on Level I?

Quantitative methods carries 11–14% of the exam from 2027, which is roughly 20 to 25 of the 180 questions.

Where can I find more Level I practice questions?

Use the practice questions and mock exams in CFA Institute's learning ecosystem first; they come with registration. Then use one third-party question bank, and redo every question you missed.
