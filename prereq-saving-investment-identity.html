Saving, investment, the fiscal balance and trade

By Gowtham KumarLesson 38 of 50

A rearrangement of the GDP identity shows that a country's saving, its government budget and its trade balance are tied together.

S − I = (G − T) + (X − M).

From first principles

Start from two ways of describing GDP:

  • Spending: Y = C + I + G + (X − M).
  • Income use: Y = C + S + T (households consume, save, or pay taxes).

Set them equal and cancel C: I + G + X − M = S + T. Rearranged:

S − I = (G − T) + (X − M)

Private saving beyond what the private sector invests must go somewhere: either to finance a government deficit (G − T > 0), or abroad as a trade surplus (X − M > 0), which means lending to foreigners.

Worked example

Private saving 20% of GDP, investment 18%, so S − I = 2%. If the government runs a 5% deficit, then X − M = 2% − 5% = −3%: a trade deficit, financed by borrowing from abroad.

Exam traps

  • It is an identity, true by definition. It says nothing on its own about cause and effect.
  • A larger government deficit must be matched by more private saving, less investment, or a bigger trade deficit (the "twin deficits" idea when it is the last).

Questions people ask

What does the saving–investment identity say?

Private saving minus investment, plus the government's balance, equals the trade balance. If a country spends more than it saves, the gap must be borrowed from abroad.

What are twin deficits?

A government budget deficit alongside a current account deficit. The identity links them: unless private saving rises to fund the government, the extra borrowing comes from foreigners.

Why does this matter for investors?

Countries with large current account deficits depend on foreign capital and are vulnerable when it pulls back, which often shows up as a falling currency and rising bond yields.
