The LIFO reserve and converting LIFO to FIFO

By Gowtham KumarLesson 16 of 50

US companies using LIFO disclose how much higher their inventory would be under FIFO. That one number lets you restate inventory, cost of sales, profit, equity and every ratio built on them, so LIFO and FIFO companies can be compared.

LIFO reserve=FIFO inventory−LIFO inventory\text{LIFO reserve} = \text{FIFO inventory} - \text{LIFO inventory}LIFO reserve=FIFO inventory−LIFO inventory

From first principles

A US company using LIFO carries inventory at old costs. When prices have been rising, that inventory is understated compared with a FIFO or IFRS company, and cost of sales is overstated. To make comparisons possible, US GAAP requires LIFO users to disclose the LIFO reserve: the difference between inventory under FIFO and under LIFO.

Everything follows from two identities. First, the balance sheet:

FIFO inventory=LIFO inventory+LIFO reserve\text{FIFO inventory} = \text{LIFO inventory} + \text{LIFO reserve}FIFO inventory=LIFO inventory+LIFO reserve

Second, the income statement. Because goods available for sale are the same under both methods, any difference in closing inventory must be mirrored in cost of sales. The change in the reserve during the year is the cumulative difference added this year:

FIFO cost of sales=LIFO cost of sales−(Closing reserve−Opening reserve)\text{FIFO cost of sales} = \text{LIFO cost of sales} - (\text{Closing reserve} - \text{Opening reserve})FIFO cost of sales=LIFO cost of sales−(Closing reserve−Opening reserve)

A rising reserve means LIFO cost of sales was higher, so FIFO cost of sales is lower and FIFO profit higher.

Following the tax through

Higher inventory under FIFO means more cumulative profit, which would have been taxed. So the reserve is split between tax and equity:

Equity under FIFO=Equity under LIFO+Reserve×(1−t)\text{Equity under FIFO} = \text{Equity under LIFO} + \text{Reserve} \times (1 - t)Equity under FIFO=Equity under LIFO+Reserve×(1−t)

Extra tax (as a liability or cash already paid)=Reserve×t\text{Extra tax (as a liability or cash already paid)} = \text{Reserve} \times tExtra tax (as a liability or cash already paid)=Reserve×t

For net income, the same logic applies to the year's change:

FIFO net income=LIFO net income+ΔReserve×(1−t)\text{FIFO net income} = \text{LIFO net income} + \Delta\text{Reserve} \times (1 - t)FIFO net income=LIFO net income+ΔReserve×(1−t)

Worked example 1: converting the numbers

LIFO inventory 600, LIFO reserve 400 (last year 320), LIFO cost of sales 5,000, tax rate 25%.

  • FIFO inventory = 600+400600 + 400600+400 = 1,000.
  • FIFO cost of sales = 5,000−(400−320)5{,}000 - (400 - 320)5,000−(400−320) = 4,920, so pre-tax profit is 80 higher.
  • FIFO net income is higher by 80×0.7580 \times 0.7580×0.75 = 60.
  • Equity rises by 400×0.75400 \times 0.75400×0.75 = 300; the other 100 is tax.

Worked example 2: ratios before and after

The same company has other current assets of 1,400 (so total current assets of 2,000 under LIFO), current liabilities of 1,250, debt of 1,500 and equity of 2,500.

Ratio LIFO FIFO-adjusted
Current ratio 2,000/1,250=1.602{,}000 / 1{,}250 = 1.602,000/1,250=1.60 2,400/1,250=1.922{,}400 / 1{,}250 = 1.922,400/1,250=1.92
Inventory turnover 5,000/600=8.35{,}000 / 600 = 8.35,000/600=8.3 4,920/1,000=4.94{,}920 / 1{,}000 = 4.94,920/1,000=4.9
Debt to equity 1,500/2,500=0.601{,}500 / 2{,}500 = 0.601,500/2,500=0.60 1,500/2,800=0.541{,}500 / 2{,}800 = 0.541,500/2,800=0.54

(Turnover here uses year-end inventory to keep the arithmetic simple.) Under LIFO the company looked less liquid, more indebted and much faster at turning over stock. Most of that was the accounting method, not the business.

When the reserve falls

The reserve shrinks when prices fall, or when the company sells more than it buys and dips into old, cheap LIFO layers (a LIFO liquidation). In a liquidation, cost of sales drops and margins jump for one year only, and the company pays the tax it had deferred. A falling reserve together with falling inventory quantities is the classic warning sign.

How the exam tests it

  • Converting inventory, cost of sales and net income from LIFO to FIFO.
  • Restating ratios: current ratio, inventory turnover, gross margin, debt to equity.
  • Interpreting a fall in the reserve.

Exam traps

  • The direction of the cost-of-sales adjustment: an increase in the reserve means LIFO cost of sales was higher, so subtract it to get FIFO.
  • Use the change in the reserve for the income statement and the level of the reserve for the balance sheet.
  • Equity rises only by the after-tax reserve; the rest is tax.
  • A falling LIFO reserve can signal a LIFO liquidation: a one-time profit boost and a higher tax bill.

Questions people ask

What is the LIFO reserve?

The amount by which inventory under FIFO would exceed inventory under LIFO. US companies using LIFO must disclose it so analysts can compare them with FIFO and IFRS companies.

How do you convert LIFO cost of sales to FIFO?

Subtract the increase in the LIFO reserve during the year (or add a decrease). The change in the reserve is exactly the extra cost that LIFO charged this year.

Why would a company's LIFO reserve fall?

Either input prices fell, or the company sold more units than it bought and used up old, low-cost layers. The second case, a LIFO liquidation, temporarily raises profit and taxes.

Inventory write-downs

Inventory cannot be carried above what it will fetch. When value falls below cost it is written down; IFRS and US GAAP measure the floor differently and differ on whether a write-down can come back.

Financial Statement AnalysisBy Gowtham KumarConcept 183 min read

Inventory ratios: turnover and days on hand

How quickly inventory sells says a lot about demand and management. Turnover and days on hand measure it, and together with receivables and payables days they give the cash conversion cycle.

Financial Statement AnalysisBy Gowtham KumarConcept 194 min read

Capitalise or expense?

Spending that benefits future years is capitalised as an asset and depreciated. The choice moves profit between years, moves cash between activities and changes ratios from return on assets to interest coverage.
