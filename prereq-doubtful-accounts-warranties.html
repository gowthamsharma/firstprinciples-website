Doubtful accounts and warranties: expenses we have to estimate

Some costs of a sale only appear later, such as customers who never pay or products that need repair. Matching requires estimating them now.

2 min read  /  By Gowtham Kumar

If a sale will cost you something later, part of that cost belongs to the period of the sale.

From first principles

Matching means a cost belongs to the year whose sales it helped to make. Two common costs of selling arrive late:

  • Bad debts. Some credit customers will never pay. Waiting until a specific customer defaults would push the expense into a later period, so instead the company estimates the uncollectible share and records a bad debt expense now, building an allowance for doubtful accounts that reduces receivables.
  • Warranties. If 2% of products need a repair that costs 50 on average, every sale carries an expected cost. The company records a warranty expense and a warranty provision (liability) at the time of sale, then uses the provision when claims arrive.

Worked example

Sales on credit this year: 10,000. History says 3% will not be collected. The company records bad debt expense of 300. Next year, a customer owing 120 goes bankrupt: the receivable and the allowance both fall by 120. No new expense, because it was already recognised.

Exam traps

  • Writing off a specific bad debt does not hit profit under the allowance method; the estimate did.
  • These estimates are a lever. Lowering the bad-debt percentage or warranty estimate raises current profit. Watch for allowances that fall as a share of receivables while receivables grow.

Questions people ask

Why not wait until a customer actually defaults?

Because the loss was caused by this year's credit sales. Booking it later would flatter this year and punish a later one. Estimating the expected loss now keeps each year's profit honest.

How can estimates be used to manage earnings?

By setting the allowance or warranty provision too high in good years and releasing it in bad years. Profit then looks smoother than the business really is. A falling allowance while receivables rise deserves questions.

Where do these estimates appear in the accounts?

The allowance sits against receivables on the balance sheet, so receivables are shown net. The warranty provision is a liability. Both changes run through the income statement as expenses.
