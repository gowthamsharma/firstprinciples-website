Doubtful accounts and warranties: expenses we have to estimate

By Gowtham KumarLesson 5 of 50

Some costs of a sale only appear later, such as customers who never pay or products that need repair. Matching requires estimating them now, and those estimates are one of the easiest places to manage earnings.

If a sale will cost you something later, part of that cost belongs to the period of the sale.

From first principles

Matching means a cost belongs to the year whose sales it helped to make. Two common costs of selling arrive late:

  • Bad debts. Some credit customers will never pay. Waiting until a specific customer defaults would push the expense into a later period, so the company estimates the uncollectable share and records a bad debt expense now, building an allowance for doubtful accounts that is deducted from receivables.
  • Warranties. If 2% of products need a repair that costs 50 on average, every sale carries an expected cost. The company records a warranty expense and a warranty provision (a liability) when it sells, then uses the provision when claims arrive.

Two ways to size the allowance

  • Percentage of sales: bad debt expense = credit sales × an expected loss rate. Simple, focused on the income statement.
  • Percentage of receivables, by age (aging): each age bucket of receivables gets a loss rate, older buckets a higher one. This gives the allowance the balance sheet should show; the expense is whatever is needed to get there from the current balance.

Both IFRS and US GAAP now require an expected credit loss approach for receivables: the allowance reflects losses expected over the life of the receivables, using past experience adjusted for current and forecast conditions.

Following the allowance through a year

The allowance behaves like a bucket:

Closing allowance=Opening+Bad debt expense−Write-offs+Recoveries\text{Closing allowance} = \text{Opening} + \text{Bad debt expense} - \text{Write-offs} + \text{Recoveries}Closing allowance=Opening+Bad debt expense−Write-offs+Recoveries

When a specific customer is written off, the receivable and the allowance fall by the same amount. Net receivables do not change and no new expense is recorded: the estimate already put the cost in the income statement.

Worked example 1: rolling the allowance forward

Opening allowance: 400. During the year the company writes off 350 of bad accounts. Its aging analysis says the closing allowance should be 500. What was the bad debt expense?

How analysts read it

These estimates are a lever. Lowering the loss rate or the warranty rate raises profit at once, with no cash effect. Signs worth questioning:

  • the allowance falls as a percentage of receivables while receivables grow faster than sales;
  • large releases of provisions in a weak year, which smooth earnings;
  • warranty provisions that shrink while claims rise.

How the exam tests it

  • Solving the roll-forward for the missing number (expense, write-offs or closing balance).
  • An aging schedule with a pre-existing balance.
  • The effect of a write-off under the allowance method, and the effect of changing an estimate.

Exam traps

  • Writing off a specific bad debt does not hit profit under the allowance method; the estimate already did.
  • With aging, the schedule gives the closing allowance, not the expense. Subtract what is already in the allowance.
  • Recoveries of accounts written off earlier go back into the allowance, not into revenue.
  • Lower estimates raise profit, and that profit is not backed by cash.

Questions people ask

Why not wait until a customer actually defaults?

Because the loss was caused by this year's credit sales. Booking it later would flatter this year and punish a later one. Estimating the expected loss now keeps each year's profit honest.

How can estimates be used to manage earnings?

By setting the allowance or warranty provision too high in good years and releasing it in bad years. Profit then looks smoother than the business really is. A falling allowance while receivables rise deserves questions.

Where do these estimates appear in the accounts?

The allowance sits against receivables on the balance sheet, so receivables are shown net. The warranty provision is a liability. Both changes run through the income statement as expenses.
