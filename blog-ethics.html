How to study CFA ethics

Ethics is 10–15% at every level and often decides close results. Learn the Standards through cases, read every word of the question, and when in doubt, disclose.

2 min read  /  By Gowtham Kumar

Ethics carries 10–15% at every level from 2027. It is also the part of the exam where candidates near the pass line are most often separated. The good news: it is the most learnable topic, because it rewards the same reasoning every time.

Learn the structure first

  • The Code of Ethics describes how to behave: integrity, competence, putting clients first.
  • The Standards of Professional Conduct describe what to do in specific situations, in seven groups: professionalism, integrity of capital markets, duties to clients, duties to employers, investment analysis and recommendations, conflicts of interest, and responsibilities as a CFA Institute member or candidate.

When a question puzzles you, first ask which Standard it is really about. That usually narrows it to one answer.

Learn through cases, not lists

The Standards are short; the guidance and examples that explain them are where the exam's questions come from. Work through cases until you can predict the answer before reading the explanation.

Read every word

Ethics questions turn on single words: always, never, only, must, may, a date, a client's written consent. Many wrong options are good behaviour that does not answer the question asked.

Useful defaults (not rules)

  • When unsure, disclosure is usually part of the right answer.
  • The stricter of local law and the Standards applies.
  • Client interests come before the employer's, and the employer's before your own.

Do not leave it to the end

Many candidates study ethics last because it feels easy, then find it is not. Prep providers suggest studying it once early, then keeping it warm with a few questions every week until the exam.

Questions people ask

Why is ethics so important on the CFA exams?

It carries a meaningful weight at every level, and the questions reward careful reading more than memory. Candidates close to the pass line are often separated by how well they did in ethics.

How should I study ethics?

Read the Standards once to know the structure, then spend most of the time on case questions. Ethics is learned by seeing many situations, not by memorising wording.

What are common ethics traps?

Assuming local law is enough when a stricter standard applies, missing that a small gift could still affect independence, and overlooking duties to a former employer. Read every detail in the question.

When should I revise ethics?

Twice: early, so it frames the other topics, and again in the final weeks, because the details fade quickly and the questions are very sensitive to wording.
