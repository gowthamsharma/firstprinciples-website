Non-recurring and non-operating items

By Gowtham KumarLesson 7 of 50

Analysts forecast what will happen again. The income statement's layers separate the repeatable core from one-offs, from businesses being sold and from changes in accounting.

Value comes from what will recur. Separate it from what will not.

From first principles

A valuation projects earnings into the future. If this year's profit includes a gain on selling a factory, projecting that gain forward overstates value. So the income statement is built in layers that help an analyst separate the repeatable from the one-off:

  • Operating results: the business's core activity.
  • Non-operating items: interest income and expense, gains and losses on investments. What counts as operating depends on the business: for a bank, interest is the core.
  • Unusual or infrequent items: restructuring charges, impairments, litigation settlements, gains on asset sales. They stay within continuing operations, before tax, often disclosed separately.
  • Discontinued operations: a separable component (a major line of business or region) that has been sold or is held for sale. Its results are shown net of tax, below profit from continuing operations, and earnings per share is split between continuing and discontinued.

Changes that are not about the business

Three kinds of accounting change look alike but are treated differently:

Change Example Treatment
Accounting policy switching inventory from FIFO to weighted average retrospective: prior years restated as if the new policy had always applied
Accounting estimate new useful life, new bad-debt rate prospective: current and future years only
Correction of an error revenue double-counted last year restate the prior years and disclose

The reasoning: a new policy should be comparable across years, so the past is redone; an estimate was the best judgement at the time, so it is not "wrong" in hindsight.

Worked example 1: from reported to core earnings

Operating profit before a one-off restructuring charge is 200. Interest expense is 20, the restructuring charge is 30, tax is 25%, and a division being sold lost 12 after tax.

Reported net income=(200−20−30)×0.75−12=100.5\text{Reported net income} = (200 - 20 - 30) \times 0.75 - 12 = 100.5Reported net income=(200−20−30)×0.75−12=100.5

A sustainable estimate excludes the restructuring (after tax: 30×0.75=22.530 \times 0.75 = 22.530×0.75=22.5) and the discontinued division:

Core earnings=100.5+22.5+12=(200−20)×0.75=135\text{Core earnings} = 100.5 + 22.5 + 12 = (200 - 20) \times 0.75 = 135Core earnings=100.5+22.5+12=(200−20)×0.75=135

Worked example 2: the one-off that keeps happening

A company reports "restructuring charges" of 50, 45, 60 and 55 in four consecutive years while revenue barely changes. Labelled one-off, they are in substance a cost of running this business. A sensible analyst treats an average of about 52 a year as a recurring operating cost, and is sceptical of "adjusted" profit figures that leave it out.

How the exam tests it

  • Calculating income from continuing operations, or core earnings, after tax.
  • Placing an item correctly: unusual item (pre-tax, continuing) or discontinued operation (net of tax, below the line).
  • Choosing retrospective or prospective treatment for a change.

Exam traps

  • Adjust after tax. Adding back a pre-tax charge to after-tax earnings overstates core profit.
  • US GAAP no longer has "extraordinary items", and IFRS never allows them.
  • Discontinued operations are net of tax and below continuing operations; unusual items are pre-tax and within them.
  • "Non-recurring" items that recur are operating costs, whatever the label.
  • Policy changes and error corrections restate the past; estimate changes do not.

Questions people ask

Why separate one-off items from recurring profit?

Valuation is about the future. A gain from selling a building will not happen again, so an analyst removes it to see the earnings power the business can keep producing.

When is a business a discontinued operation?

When a whole component that can be clearly distinguished, such as a major line of business or region, has been sold or is held for sale. Its results are shown separately, net of tax, below continuing operations.

Are restructuring charges really one-off?

Not always. Some companies report them almost every year. If an item recurs, treat it as a normal cost of doing business, whatever the label says.
