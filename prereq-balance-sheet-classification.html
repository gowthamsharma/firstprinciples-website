The balance sheet: current and non-current

By Gowtham KumarLesson 10 of 50

Classifying assets and liabilities by timing shows whether a company can pay what it owes soon with what it will have soon. Three liquidity ratios turn that split into numbers.

"Current" means it turns into cash, or must be paid, inside a year (or inside the business's normal production-to-cash loop, when that runs longer). The split is about liquidity.

From first principles

A lender's first worry is simple: if the bills arrive this year, will the cash arrive this year too? So the balance sheet separates:

  • Current assets: expected to become cash or be used up within the next year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer: cash, short-term investments, receivables, inventory, prepaid expenses.
  • Non-current assets: things kept for years: property, plant and equipment, intangible assets, long-term investments, deferred tax assets.
  • Current liabilities: anything payable within the next 12 months: suppliers (accounts payable), short-term borrowing, the current portion of long-term debt, accrued expenses, unearned revenue.
  • Non-current liabilities: due later: bonds, long-term loans, deferred tax liabilities, pension obligations.

The operating cycle is the time from paying for inputs to collecting cash from customers. For a whisky distiller or a shipbuilder it can be several years, so their inventory is still current even though it takes longer than a year to sell.

Banks and other financial firms often use a liquidity-based presentation instead, ordering items from most to least liquid, because the current/non-current split means little for them.

From classification to ratios

Three ratios measure liquidity with stricter and stricter definitions of what will be cash in time:

Current ratio=Current assetsCurrent liabilities\text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}Current ratio=Current liabilitiesCurrent assets​

Quick ratio=Cash+Short-term securities+ReceivablesCurrent liabilities\text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Short-term securities} + \text{Receivables}}{\text{Current liabilities}}Quick ratio=Current liabilitiesCash+Short-term securities+Receivables​

Cash ratio=Cash+Short-term securitiesCurrent liabilities\text{Cash ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Short-term securities}}{\text{Current liabilities}}Cash ratio=Current liabilitiesCash+Short-term securities​

The quick ratio drops inventory and prepaid expenses, the current assets least likely to turn into cash quickly. Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities.

Worked example 1: one company, three ratios

Current assets 300: cash 60, short-term securities 20, receivables 40, inventory 180. Current liabilities 250.

  • Working capital: 300−250=50300 - 250 = 50300−250=50. Current ratio: 300/250300 / 250300/250 = 1.20.
  • Quick ratio: (60+20+40)/250(60 + 20 + 40) / 250(60+20+40)/250 = 0.48.
  • Cash ratio: (60+20)/250(60 + 20) / 250(60+20)/250 = 0.32.

A current ratio above 1 looked comfortable; the quick ratio shows that most of the cover is slow-moving inventory.

Worked example 2: transactions that move the ratio

Current assets 300, current liabilities 200: current ratio 1.5. The company uses 50 of cash to pay suppliers. Both sides fall by 50:

300−50200−50=250150=1.67\frac{300 - 50}{200 - 50} = \frac{250}{150} = 1.67200−50300−50​=150250​=1.67

The ratio rises, because when a ratio is above 1, subtracting the same amount from top and bottom pushes it further up. (Below 1, it would fall.) This is why a company can make its year-end current ratio look better simply by paying bills just before the reporting date.

How the exam tests it

  • Classifying an item (current portion of debt, deferred tax, a long operating cycle).
  • Calculating the three ratios and explaining the gap between them.
  • Predicting how a transaction changes a ratio.

Exam traps

  • The part of a long-term loan due within 12 months is current, even though the loan is long-term.
  • A covenant breach at the reporting date that lets the lender demand repayment makes the whole loan current, unless the lender agrees a waiver before the reporting date.
  • Deferred tax balances are never current under IFRS, and since 2017 not under US GAAP either.
  • Paying a current liability with cash raises a current ratio above 1 and lowers one below 1.
  • Balance sheet values mix measurement bases (historical cost, amortised cost, fair value), so book value is not market value.

Questions people ask

Why does the current versus non-current split matter?

It shows whether short-term obligations are covered by assets that will soon become cash. Working capital and the current ratio are both built on it.

Is a long-term loan always a non-current liability?

No. The part falling due within the next year is moved to current liabilities, and the whole loan can become current if a covenant breach lets the lender demand repayment.

Do all companies order the balance sheet the same way?

No. Many IFRS reporters list long-term items first; US companies usually start with cash. Banks often skip the split entirely and list items by liquidity.
