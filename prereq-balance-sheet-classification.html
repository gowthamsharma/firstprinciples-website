The balance sheet: current and non-current

Classifying assets and liabilities by timing shows whether a company can pay what it owes soon with what it will have soon.

2 min read  /  By Gowtham Kumar

"Current" means it turns into cash, or must be paid, inside a year (or inside the business's normal production-to-cash loop, when that runs longer). The split is about liquidity.

From first principles

A lender's first worry is simple: if the bills arrive this year, will the cash arrive this year too? So the balance sheet separates:

  • Current assets: expected to become cash or be consumed within the next year (or the firm's production-to-cash loop): cash, receivables, inventory, prepaid expenses.
  • Non-current assets: things kept for years: buildings and machines, patents and brands, long-term holdings in other firms, and tax that will be recovered later.
  • Current liabilities: anything payable inside the next 12 months: suppliers, short-term loans, the slice of long-term loans falling due this year, accrued costs and cash received for work not yet done.
  • Non-current liabilities: due later: bonds, long-term loans, deferred tax liabilities, pension obligations.

Banks and other financial firms often use a liquidity-based presentation instead, ordering items from most to least liquid, because the current/non-current split means little for them.

Worked example

Current assets 300, current liabilities 250. Working capital = 50; current ratio = 1.2. But if 180 of the current assets is slow-moving inventory, the quick ratio (cash + marketable securities + receivables) / current liabilities = 120 / 250 = 0.48, a much tighter picture.

Exam traps

  • The slice of a long-term loan due within the year is shown as current, even though the loan itself is long-term.
  • Deferred tax balances never go in the current section under IFRS, whichever way they point.
  • Balance sheet values mix measurement bases (historical cost, amortised cost, fair value), so book value is not market value.

Why it matters at Level III

Liquidity analysis feeds credit work and asset allocation decisions for liability-driven investors, where matching the timing of assets and liabilities is the whole point.

Questions people ask

Why does the current versus non-current split matter?

It shows whether short-term obligations are covered by assets that will soon become cash. Working capital and the current ratio are both built on it.

Is a long-term loan always a non-current liability?

No. The part falling due within the next year is moved to current liabilities, and the whole loan can become current if a covenant breach lets the lender demand repayment.

Do all companies order the balance sheet the same way?

No. Many IFRS reporters list long-term items first; US companies usually start with cash. Banks often skip the split entirely and list items by liquidity.
