CFA Level I practice questions: Derivatives, with worked answers

By Gowtham KumarPractice questions

Ten original derivatives questions written like the real Level I exam: short cases with data, three options each, and worked explanations. Forwards, futures, swaps and options: pricing by no-arbitrage and payoffs. Derivatives carries 6–9% of the exam from 2027.

Derivatives at Level I is about payoffs and no-arbitrage pricing: forwards and futures, swaps, options, put-call parity and the one-period binomial model. Draw the payoff before you calculate and most questions become simple.

Topic weight from 2027: 6–9% of Level I, about 11 to 16 of the 180 questions.

10 questions · Level I format · about 15 to 20 minutes

Choose an answer to see the explanation.

  1. A non-dividend-paying stock trades at $50. The risk-free rate is 4% a year with annual compounding.

    The no-arbitrage price of a six-month forward contract on the stock is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    F0 = S0 × (1 + r)^T = 50 × 1.04^0.5 = $50.99. If the forward were priced higher, an arbitrageur could borrow, buy the stock and sell the forward for a riskless profit, and the reverse if it were lower.

    $52.00 compounds for a full year instead of six months. $50.00 ignores the cost of carrying the stock (the interest forgone).

  2. Three months ago a trader entered a long six-month forward contract on a non-dividend-paying share at a forward price of €52. Today the share trades at €54, and the risk-free rate is 4% a year (annual compounding). The contract has three months left.

    The value of the long forward position today is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Value of a long forward during its life = current spot price − PV of the forward price over the remaining time: Vt = 54 − 52 / 1.04^0.25 = 54 − 51.49 = €2.51.

    €2.00 compares spot with the forward price without discounting. €3.01 discounts the forward price over six months instead of the three months remaining.

  3. A European call and a European put on the same non-dividend-paying stock both have a strike price of 100 and one year to expiry. The stock trades at 100, the call is priced at 8.00, and the risk-free rate is 5% (annual compounding).

    Using put–call parity, the no-arbitrage price of the put is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    Put–call parity: S + p = c + X / (1 + r)^T. So p = c + PV(X) − S = 8.00 + 100 / 1.05 − 100 = 8.00 + 95.24 − 100 = 3.24.

    The put is cheaper than the call because the stock's forward price (105) is above the strike. 8.00 assumes the put equals the call. 12.76 rearranges the formula with the wrong signs (c + S − PV(X)).

  4. An investor writes (sells) a call option on Danube Energy shares with a strike price of €60 and receives a premium of €4. At expiry, Danube shares trade at €67.

    The investor's profit per share at expiry is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    The call is exercised because 67 > 60. The writer must sell at 60 a share worth 67, paying out 7, but keeps the premium of 4. Profit = 4 − 7 = −€3.

    −€7 is the payoff, which ignores the premium received. +€4 would be the profit if the option expired unexercised. The writer's maximum gain is the premium, while the potential loss is unlimited.

  5. An investor buys a share of Atlas Mining at $50 and, at the same time, buys a put option on the share with a strike price of $45 for a premium of $2.

    At the option's expiry, the maximum loss on the combined position and the breakeven share price are closest to:

    Answer: C.

    This is a protective put. Below $45 the put offsets further falls, so the worst case is a $5 fall in the share price plus the $2 premium, a total of $7. To break even, the share price must rise enough to recover the premium: 50 + 2 = $52.

    $5 forgets the premium. $48 subtracts the premium instead of adding it, as you would for a written option.

  6. A stock trades at €40. In one year it will either rise to €50 (u = 1.25) or fall to €32 (d = 0.80). The risk-free rate is 5% a year. An analyst prices a one-year European call option on the stock with a strike price of €42 using a one-period binomial model.

    The value of the call is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Risk-neutral probability of an up move: π = (1 + r − d) / (u − d) = (1.05 − 0.80) / (1.25 − 0.80) = 0.5556. Payoffs: up = 50 − 42 = 8; down = 0. Call value = [0.5556 × 8 + 0.4444 × 0] / 1.05 = €4.23.

    €4.44 forgets to discount at the risk-free rate. €3.81 uses a 50% probability, which is not the risk-neutral probability implied by the model.

  7. A bank prices a three-year plain-vanilla interest rate swap with annual payments. The discount factors for one, two and three years are 0.97, 0.94 and 0.91.

    The fixed swap rate that gives the swap zero value at inception is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    The fixed rate makes the present value of fixed payments equal to the floating leg, which (with notional exchanged at the end) is worth par. Swap rate = (1 − final discount factor) / sum of discount factors = (1 − 0.91) / (0.97 + 0.94 + 0.91) = 0.09 / 2.82 = 3.19%.

    3.00% divides 0.09 by three years instead of by the sum of discount factors. 9.00% is the total, not an annual rate.

  8. A trader buys five gold futures contracts at $2,000 per ounce. Each contract covers 100 ounces. The initial margin is $10,000 per contract and the maintenance margin is $8,000 per contract. At the end of day 1 the settlement price is $1,985, and at the end of day 2 it is $1,975.

    The variation margin the trader must deposit after day 2 is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Initial margin = 5 × 10,000 = $50,000; maintenance = $40,000. Day 1 loss = 15 × 100 × 5 = $7,500, so the balance is $42,500 (no call). Day 2 loss = 10 × 100 × 5 = $5,000, so the balance is $37,500, which is below maintenance. The trader must restore the account to the initial margin: 50,000 − 37,500 = $12,500.

    $2,500 tops up only to the maintenance level, which is how margin works for some stock accounts but not for futures.

  9. A call option on Kestrel Airlines shares has a strike price of £50 and trades at £6. Kestrel shares currently trade at £53.

    The option's intrinsic value and time value are closest to:

    Answer: A.

    Intrinsic value = max(0, S − X) = 53 − 50 = £3, the amount received if the option were exercised now. Time value = option price − intrinsic value = 6 − 3 = £3. This reflects the chance that the share rises further before expiry. Time value falls towards zero as expiry approaches.

    The option is in the money, so its intrinsic value is not zero.

  10. Crude oil trades at $80 a barrel in the spot market. The one-year risk-free rate is 5%. The present value of the cost of storing a barrel for a year is $2, and the present value of the convenience yield (the benefit of holding physical oil) is $3.

    The no-arbitrage one-year forward price is closest to:

    Answer: A.

    Forward price = (spot + PV of costs − PV of benefits) × (1 + r)^T = (80 + 2 − 3) × 1.05 = $82.95.

    $84.00 ignores storage costs and convenience yield. $86.10 includes storage but leaves out the convenience yield. A high convenience yield can push forward prices below spot, a market in backwardation.

Three things to remember for derivatives

  • Payoff ignores the premium; profit subtracts it.
  • Put-call parity: call + PV(strike) = put + stock.
  • In the binomial model, discount the risk-neutral expected payoff at the risk-free rate.

How to use these questions

Answer each question before you look at the explanation, and time yourself: the real exam allows about 90 seconds per question. Mark every question you got right for the wrong reason as wrong. When you miss one, write down the idea behind it rather than the answer, and come back to the page in a week.

These questions are written by First Principles to match the style and level of the exam. They are not taken from CFA Institute's curriculum or mock exams, which you should also use: they are included with your exam registration.

Questions people ask

Are these real CFA exam questions?

No. CFA Institute does not publish past exam questions. These are original questions written to match the Level I format: a short case, often with a table of data, followed by three answer options. Each explanation shows the working and why the two wrong options are wrong.

How many derivatives questions are on Level I?

Derivatives carries 6–9% of the exam from 2027, which is roughly 11 to 16 of the 180 questions.

Where can I find more Level I practice questions?

Use the practice questions and mock exams in CFA Institute's learning ecosystem first; they come with registration. Then use one third-party question bank, and redo every question you missed.
