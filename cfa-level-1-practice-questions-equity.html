CFA Level I practice questions: Equity investments, with worked answers

By Gowtham KumarPractice questions

Ten original equity investments questions written like the real Level I exam: short cases with data, three options each, and worked explanations. Market mechanics, indexes, efficiency, industry analysis and valuation models. Equity investments carries 11–14% of the exam from 2027.

Equity investments covers how markets work (orders, margin, indexes), market efficiency, industry and company analysis, and valuation with dividend discount models and multiples. Expect several margin and index calculations and at least one Gordon growth model.

Topic weight from 2027: 11–14% of Level I, about 20 to 25 of the 180 questions.

10 questions · Level I format · about 15 to 20 minutes

Choose an answer to see the explanation.

  1. Fatima Al-Sayed buys 500 shares of a Gulf telecoms company at AED 40 per share on margin. The initial margin requirement is 50% and the maintenance margin is 25%.

    She will receive a margin call if the share price falls below approximately:

    Answer: C.

    She borrows AED 20 per share (50% of 40). A margin call occurs when equity / price falls to 25%: (P − 20) / P = 0.25, so P = 20 / 0.75 = AED 26.67. The general formula is P0 × (1 − initial margin) / (1 − maintenance margin).

    AED 30.00 is 75% of the purchase price, and AED 20.00 is the loan per share, at which her equity would be zero.

  2. Jonas Berg buys shares of Scandi Biotech at SEK 40 each, paying 50% in cash and borrowing the rest from his broker at 6% a year. One year later he sells the shares at SEK 46, after receiving a dividend of SEK 1 per share. Ignore commissions and taxes.

    The return on his own investment is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Per share, his equity is SEK 20 and the loan SEK 20. Gain = price change 6 + dividend 1 − interest 1.20 (6% × 20) = SEK 5.80. Return = 5.80 / 20 = 29.0%.

    17.5% is the unleveraged return: (6 + 1) / 40. 35.0% ignores the interest on the loan. Leverage magnifies returns in both directions.

  3. An index contains three stocks:

    StockPrice at startPrice at endShares outstanding (millions)
    A505510
    B302740
    C202225

    Over the period, the returns of a price-weighted index and a market-capitalisation-weighted index of these stocks are closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Price-weighted: sum of prices 100 → 104, so +4.00%. Higher-priced stocks such as A have the most influence.

    Cap-weighted: market values at the start are 500 + 1,200 + 500 = 2,200, and at the end 550 + 1,080 + 550 = 2,180, so −0.91%. Stock B is the largest company, and its 10% fall dominates.

    +3.33% is the equal-weighted return, the average of +10%, −10% and +10%.

  4. Arcadia Holdings has 1 million cumulative preferred shares that each pay an annual dividend of $2.00. Because of losses, it paid no preferred dividends in the last two years. This year the board declares total dividends of $7 million.

    The amount available to ordinary shareholders this year is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Cumulative preferred shareholders must receive all dividends in arrears plus the current year's dividend before ordinary shareholders receive anything: 3 years × $2.00 × 1 million = $6 million. That leaves 7 − 6 = $1 million for ordinary shareholders.

    $5 million would apply if the preferred shares were non-cumulative, so that only the current $2 million was owed. Preferred shares rank ahead of ordinary shares, so $7 million is not possible.

  5. Sunrise Utilities has just paid an annual dividend of $2.00 per share. Its dividend is expected to grow at 4% a year indefinitely, and investors require a return of 9% on its shares.

    According to the Gordon growth model, the intrinsic value of a Sunrise share is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    V0 = D1 / (r − g), where D1 is next year's dividend: D1 = 2.00 × 1.04 = 2.08. V0 = 2.08 / (0.09 − 0.04) = $41.60.

    $40.00 uses the dividend just paid (D0) instead of D1. $16.00 divides D1 by r + g instead of r − g.

  6. Lotus Pharma has just paid a dividend of ₹1.50 per share. Analysts expect dividends to grow by 12% a year for the next three years and by 4% a year thereafter. The required return on Lotus shares is 10%.

    The intrinsic value of a Lotus share today is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Dividends: D1 = 1.680, D2 = 1.882, D3 = 2.107. Terminal value at the end of Year 3: P3 = D4 / (r − g) = 2.107 × 1.04 / 0.06 = 36.53.

    Value today = 1.680/1.1 + 1.882/1.1² + (2.107 + 36.53)/1.1³ = 1.527 + 1.555 + 29.03 = ₹32.11.

    ₹36.53 is the terminal value at Year 3, not discounted to today. ₹27.44 is only the present value of the terminal value, leaving out the first three dividends.

  7. A non-callable, non-convertible perpetual preferred share has a par value of €100 and pays an annual dividend of 5% of par. Investors require a 7% return on preferred shares of similar risk.

    The value of the preferred share is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    A perpetual preferred dividend is a perpetuity: V = D / r = (5% × 100) / 0.07 = 5 / 0.07 = €71.43. The share trades below par because the required return (7%) is above the dividend rate (5%).

    €100.00 is par, which is correct only when the required return equals the dividend rate. €140.00 inverts the two rates (7 / 0.05).

  8. Pacific Foods pays out 40% of its earnings as dividends. Its required return on equity is 10%, and dividends and earnings are expected to grow at a constant 5% a year.

    Using the Gordon growth model, Pacific's justified forward price-to-earnings ratio is closest to:

    Answer: B.

    Justified forward P/E = P0 / E1 = payout ratio / (r − g) = 0.40 / (0.10 − 0.05) = 8.0.

    8.4 is the justified trailing P/E = payout × (1 + g) / (r − g) = 0.40 × 1.05 / 0.05, which divides by current rather than next year's earnings. 4.0 divides the payout by r instead of r − g.

  9. An analyst values Meridian Logistics with an EV/EBITDA multiple of 8.0×, based on comparable companies. Meridian's expected EBITDA is $250 million. The company has $600 million of debt, $150 million of cash and $50 million of preferred shares, and 100 million ordinary shares outstanding.

    The estimated value per ordinary share is closest to:

    Answer: C.

    Enterprise value = 8.0 × 250 = $2,000 million. EV is the value of the whole business to all capital providers, so subtract debt and preferred shares and add back cash: equity = 2,000 − 600 − 50 + 150 = $1,500 million. Per share: 1,500 / 100 = $15.00.

    $14.00 subtracts debt only, ignoring cash and the preferred shares. $20.00 divides enterprise value by the share count, as if the company had no debt.

  10. A research team finds that trading rules based on past prices and volumes do not earn abnormal returns after transaction costs. However, a strategy that buys shares with high earnings quality, based on public financial statements, has earned significant risk-adjusted abnormal returns for many years.

    The evidence is most consistent with a market that is:

    Answer: B.

    Weak-form efficiency means prices reflect all past market data, so technical rules should not earn abnormal returns. That fits the first finding. Semi-strong-form efficiency means prices also reflect all public information, including financial statements, so fundamental analysis should not earn persistent abnormal returns. The second finding contradicts it.

    The market therefore appears weak-form efficient but not semi-strong-form efficient. The evidence says nothing about private (inside) information, so strong-form efficiency is not tested.

Three things to remember for equity investments

  • The Gordon growth model needs next year's dividend, D1, not the one just paid.
  • Price-weighted indexes are moved by high-priced stocks; value-weighted by large companies.
  • Subtract net debt to get from enterprise value to equity value.

How to use these questions

Answer each question before you look at the explanation, and time yourself: the real exam allows about 90 seconds per question. Mark every question you got right for the wrong reason as wrong. When you miss one, write down the idea behind it rather than the answer, and come back to the page in a week.

These questions are written by First Principles to match the style and level of the exam. They are not taken from CFA Institute's curriculum or mock exams, which you should also use: they are included with your exam registration.

Questions people ask

Are these real CFA exam questions?

No. CFA Institute does not publish past exam questions. These are original questions written to match the Level I format: a short case, often with a table of data, followed by three answer options. Each explanation shows the working and why the two wrong options are wrong.

How many equity investments questions are on Level I?

Equity investments carries 11–14% of the exam from 2027, which is roughly 20 to 25 of the 180 questions.

Where can I find more Level I practice questions?

Use the practice questions and mock exams in CFA Institute's learning ecosystem first; they come with registration. Then use one third-party question bank, and redo every question you missed.
